Power Structures

A country-by-country guide to who decides, who can block or delay those decisions, and when that balance could shift. It brings together each decision-maker's worldview and way of deciding, the constitutional and actual structure of power, the makeup of the legislature and what its members say issue by issue, and what the government says and does.

The United StatesPresidential system · As of October 6, 2026

The United States is a presidential system in which the president and Congress are elected separately and designed to check each other. Fast-moving decisions — the use of force, the enforcement of sanctions and tariffs — rest with the president. Budgets and laws belong to Congress, which constrains the president by writing conditions and requirements into statute. With Republicans controlling both chambers, Congress rarely stands in his way. That could change with the midterm elections on November 3, 2026.

Decision-maker
Donald Trump President · since January 2025

Trump sees international politics as an arena in which wealthy allies and rivals alike have long taken advantage of the United States. He appears intent on shifting those costs onto others and on being judged by the deals he wins and the wars he ends. He treats tariffs, export licenses, alliance assets, and long-range strikes as bargaining chips; makes decisions within a small circle of aides and through his own public posts; and, his public vows notwithstanding, tends to back down once the costs of a threat rebound on U.S. financial markets. What the government says and does

In its rhetoric, Washington holds to denuclearization, the alliance, and deterrence over Taiwan. Its money and forces, however, are moving in two directions: on the Korean Peninsula it is cutting costs and opening the door to a deal with North Korea, while around Taiwan it is stepping up arms sales and deployments. The Trump administration also treats both the peninsula and Taiwan as objects of bargaining.

Powers under the constitution

  • Executive power lies with the president; legislative and budgetary power, with Congress — including the power to levy taxes and tariffs, regulate foreign commerce, and fund the armed forces (Article I, Sections 8 and 9).
  • Congress declares war; the president commands the military (Article I, Section 8; Article II, Section 2). The War Powers Resolution of 1973 requires the president to notify Congress within 48 hours of committing forces and, absent congressional authorization, to end the deployment within 60 days, extendable by 30 days to allow a safe withdrawal.
  • Treaties require the consent of two-thirds of senators present, and senior appointments such as cabinet secretaries and ambassadors require Senate confirmation (Article II, Section 2).
  • The president can veto legislation; Congress can override with a two-thirds vote in each chamber (Article I, Section 7).
  • Federal courts decide whether the actions of the president or Congress exceed the bounds of the Constitution and the law.

Power in practice

The use of force is, in effect, the president's call

A 2019 resolution to end U.S. involvement in Yemen's civil war and a 2020 resolution on Iran passed both chambers but were vetoed. In 2026, an Iran war powers resolution cleared the House three times before failing in the Senate 49–50 on September 24. No military operation has ever been halted under the War Powers Resolution.

On tariffs, the president wields power borrowed from Congress

The Constitution assigns tariffs to Congress, but Congress has delegated tariff authority to the president through Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act and Sections 301 and 122 of the Trade Act. On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize tariffs, and the administration shifted to tariffs grounded in other statutes.

Rather than block the president outright, Congress writes conditions into law

The fiscal 2026 NDAA bars the use of funds to cut U.S. Forces Korea below 28,500 troops or to complete the transfer of wartime operational control in a way that departs from the plan Seoul and Washington agreed. The restriction lifts 60 days after the defense secretary certifies that doing so serves the national interest and submits an assessment. The Russia sanctions law signed on September 18, 2026, requires the president to impose tariffs within 30 days on major buyers of Russian oil and gas — but leaves him a way to waive them.

The Senate's 60-vote threshold slows the majority

Most bills need 60 votes to end debate and proceed to a vote, so even the majority party struggles to legislate without help from the minority. On July 14, 2026, the fiscal 2027 NDAA stalled on that vote, 50–46.

The decision-maker's powers and latitude

President Trump enjoys broad discretion over the use of force, the enforcement and waiving of sanctions, and tariffs within the authority Congress has delegated. Where Congress has attached conditions in law — as on troop levels in Korea or the transfer of wartime operational control — he must follow the prescribed procedures, and budgets and new legislation require bargaining with Congress.

Issue by issue

The Korean Peninsula
The president cannot cut U.S. troops in Korea or complete the transfer of wartime operational control on his own; he must follow procedures Congress has written into law. The Senate Armed Services Committee's version of the fiscal 2027 NDAA would require the defense secretary's certification before the transfer could be completed at all.
The Russia-Ukraine War
The Russia sanctions law makes tariffs mandatory, but whether to waive them is the president's call. October 18, 2026, is the first test.
Iran and Hormuz
Congress has been unable to stop military action against Iran. The decision to use force rests with the president.
The Taiwan Strait
Congress built the legal framework for supporting Taiwan, from the Taiwan Relations Act of 1979 to the Taiwan Enhanced Resilience Act of 2022. The executive branch decides on arms sales and notifies Congress.
AI and Chips
Semiconductor export controls are an executive-branch power; chip tariffs rest on Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act, authority Congress delegated.

ChinaCommunist Party rule (a one-party state in which the Party leads the state and the military) · As of October 6, 2026

China is a one-party state whose constitution declares, in Article 1, that the leadership of the Communist Party of China is the defining feature of socialism with Chinese characteristics. Formally, the highest organ of state power is the National People's Congress (NPC). In reality, decisions are made by the Party's Politburo and its Standing Committee and by the Party's central commissions headed by General Secretary Xi Jinping; state bodies carry them out through legislation and appointments. Xi is at once the Party's general secretary, the state president, and chairman of the Central Military Commission, and a 2018 constitutional amendment abolished presidential term limits. Effectively no body can block his decisions by vote, and the next occasion for a realignment of power is the Party's 21st National Congress, which is likely to convene in the second half of 2027.

Decision-maker
Xi Jinping General Secretary of the Communist Party of China Central Committee, Chairman of the Party and State Central Military Commissions, President of the People's Republic of China · since November 2012 (general secretary and chairman of the Party Central Military Commission), March 2013 (president)

For Xi, international politics is an arena of struggle in which containment and encirclement by a U.S.-led West are a constant, and reunification with Taiwan and the Party's leadership are tasks from which he cannot retreat, however long they take. He prefers building pressure a little at a time, at each opportunity, to a single decisive move; turns other countries' dependence on China into leverage; and, in choosing people, checks loyalty before competence. Powers under the state and Party constitutions

  • Article 1 of the constitution states that "the leadership of the Communist Party of China is the defining feature of socialism with Chinese characteristics" (added by a 2018 amendment). The Party's organization and powers are set not by the state constitution but by the Party Constitution.
  • The Party's National Congress convenes every five years and elects the Central Committee, whose plenary session elects the Politburo, the Politburo Standing Committee, and the general secretary. When the Central Committee is not in plenary session, the Politburo and its Standing Committee exercise its powers, and the Central Committee decides the membership of the Party's Central Military Commission (Party Constitution, Articles 19, 22, and 23).
  • The NPC amends the constitution, elects the president and the chairman of the state Central Military Commission, and decides questions of war and peace (Constitution, Article 62). Constitutional amendments require the votes of at least two-thirds of all deputies (Article 64).
  • The NPC Standing Committee ratifies and abrogates treaties and important agreements; decides, when the NPC is not in session and the country comes under armed attack, to proclaim a state of war; and decides on mobilization and states of emergency (Article 67). The president makes the corresponding proclamations and ratifications (Articles 80 and 81).
  • The state Central Military Commission directs the country's armed forces and operates under a system in which its chairman bears overall responsibility (Article 93). By convention, the Party and state Central Military Commissions have identical membership and in effect function as a single body.
  • Article 8 of the Anti-Secession Law, enacted on March 14, 2005, provides for the use of "non-peaceful means" if Taiwan's secession becomes a fait accompli or the possibility of peaceful reunification is completely exhausted. That decision rests with the State Council and the Central Military Commission, which report to the NPC Standing Committee after the fact.

Power in practice

Decisions are made in the Party's central commissions

A March 2018 institutional overhaul upgraded the Central Leading Group for Foreign Affairs, the Central Leading Group for Financial and Economic Affairs, and other bodies into commissions, and Xi personally chairs meetings of the foreign affairs, national security, and financial and economic affairs commissions (for example, the National Security Commission on May 30, 2023, and the Financial and Economic Affairs Commission on July 1, 2025). Taiwan policy belongs to the Central Leading Group for Taiwan Affairs; Wang Huning, a member of the Politburo Standing Committee, has been reported as its deputy head, but its membership is not disclosed.

There is no record of the NPC rejecting a Party decision

The constitutional amendment abolishing presidential term limits passed on March 11, 2018, by a vote of 2,958–2, with three abstentions. Xi's third term as general secretary was confirmed on October 23, 2022, and on March 10, 2023, the NPC unanimously reelected him president and chairman of the state Central Military Commission. A search of the public record turned up no case in which the NPC voted down a measure the Party center had settled on.

Purges have left just two members on the Central Military Commission

Of the seven members of the Central Military Commission chosen in October 2022, Li Shangfu (June 2024) and Miao Hua and Vice Chairman He Weidong (October 2025) were the first to be expelled from the Party. On January 24, 2026, the Party center placed Vice Chairman Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli, chief of the Joint Staff Department, under investigation; on August 28, the NPC Standing Committee removed both from the state Central Military Commission; and on September 21, the Politburo approved their expulsion from the Party and the military. The announcement accused them of forming factions and being disloyal to the Party. Only Xi and Zhang Shengmin, promoted to vice chairman in October 2025, remain on the commission.

Xi is constrained not by institutions but by military capacity and the economy

No institution can veto Xi's decisions, but the military's capacity to carry them out and the state of the economy appear to narrow his options. CSIS assessed in February 2026 that some 100 senior officers have been dismissed or have disappeared from view since 2022, degrading the military's capacity for large-scale joint operations in the near term; slowing growth and the property slump, meanwhile, raise the cost of confrontation abroad. No public evidence reveals dissent within the Politburo, so the judgment that elite constraints have weakened is an inference drawn from personnel outcomes.

The decision-maker's powers and latitude

Xi Jinping is the Party's general secretary, the state president, and chairman of the Central Military Commission, and he personally leads the top commissions on foreign affairs, national security, and the economy. The use of force against Taiwan is decided by the State Council and the Central Military Commission, but the commission operates under a system of chairman responsibility and is down to two members, narrowing the channels for dissent within the military. If Xi delays or changes a decision, the reason is more likely to be the military's capacity to execute it, the economic cost, or his calculations about negotiations with Washington than any institutional veto.

RussiaPresidential system (an authoritarian regime that concentrates power in the president) · As of October 6, 2026

Russia is a presidential system in which the president sets the basic direction of domestic and foreign policy and commands the military. The use of force abroad and martial law require a vote of the Federation Council, the upper house, and treaties must be ratified by both the State Duma, the lower house, and the Federation Council. Since 2022, however, parliament has approved the president's requests almost unanimously. In the September 2026 elections, United Russia won 349 of the State Duma's 450 seats, giving it on its own more than the two-thirds the chamber needs to amend the constitution. Foreign-policy decisions are in effect made by President Putin and a small circle of confidants; what constrains them is less parliamentary votes than elite interests, economic conditions, and the provisions on occupied territories written into the constitution in 2022.

Decision-maker
Vladimir Putin President of the Russian Federation · since May 2024 (fifth term; first took office in 2000; prime minister 2008–2012)

For Putin, international politics appears to be a process in which Western pressure, embodied in NATO expansion, closes in on Russia's borders, and Ukraine is not a foreign country but part of historical Russia that was "given away" when the Soviet Union broke up. His pattern has been to deny his intentions right before a decision, create a fait accompli, and then justify it in long-form speeches; and when the West crosses one of his stated red lines, to choose a calibrated response while avoiding direct hostilities with NATO. Powers under the constitution

  • The 1993 constitution was extensively amended in 2020, in changes put to a nationwide vote that concluded on July 1. The president sets the basic direction of domestic and foreign policy (Article 80), is commander in chief of the armed forces (Article 87), exercises overall direction of the government, and forms and chairs the Security Council (Article 83).
  • A presidential term is six years, and no one may serve more than two terms (Article 81(3)). Article 81(3.1), however, does not count terms served before the amendments took effect by anyone who held the office at that time. Putin may therefore run again in 2030 and, if elected, serve until 2036.
  • The president appoints the prime minister from a candidate approved by the State Duma (Article 111). Ministers and agency heads responsible for defense, foreign affairs, internal affairs, and national security are appointed by the president after only consulting the Federation Council (Article 83(e.1)).
  • The Federation Council approves presidential decrees declaring martial law or a state of emergency and decides whether the armed forces may be used outside Russia's territory (Article 102(1)). The president declares martial law in the event of aggression or a direct threat of aggression and notifies both chambers immediately (Article 87(2)).
  • Treaties are ratified by federal law, and a ratification law passed by the State Duma must be considered by the Federation Council (Article 106). A law vetoed by the president takes effect only if each chamber passes it again by two-thirds of all its members (Article 107(3)).
  • Article 67(2.1) prohibits actions aimed at ceding any part of Russia's territory, and calls for such actions, with the exception of border delimitation. The Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson regions, whose annexation Moscow declared in October 2022, appear on the constitution's list of federal subjects (Article 65). Amending the ban on ceding territory (Article 67(2.1)) requires the approval of three-quarters of all members of the Federation Council, two-thirds of all members of the State Duma, and the legislatures of two-thirds of the federal subjects (Articles 108 and 136); the list of federal subjects in Article 65 is changed by federal constitutional law (Article 137).

Power in practice

The president decided on war, and parliament signed off within a day

On February 21, 2022, Putin held a public session of the Security Council to discuss recognizing the independence of the two Donbas "republics," and recognized them that same day. On February 22, the Federation Council unanimously approved his request to use force abroad, and the invasion began on February 24.

Parliamentary votes are nearly unanimous

The treaties annexing the four regions, signed on September 30, 2022, were ratified unanimously by the State Duma on October 3 and by the Federation Council on October 4. The Treaty on Comprehensive Strategic Partnership with North Korea, signed on June 19, 2024, was ratified 397–0 by the State Duma on October 24 and unanimously by the Federation Council on November 6.

Mobilization and ceasefires come by presidential order

The partial mobilization of September 21, 2022, began by presidential decree, without a parliamentary vote. On September 5, 2026, as U.S. envoys visited Moscow, Putin ordered a three-day halt to strikes on Kyiv, which his spokesman announced.

What constrains the president is the cost of war, not an institutional veto

About the only time the elite has openly defied the president was the Wagner Group's armed mutiny in June 2023, which ended within a day. High interest rates, the fiscal strain of the war, and aversion to mobilization appear to narrow the president's options, but there is no confirmed case of any of them working as an institutional check that blocked a specific decision.

The decision-maker's powers and latitude

President Vladimir Putin secured a term running to 2030 in the March 2024 election, in which he won 87.28 percent of the vote, and holds the power to decide on war and ceasefire, mobilization, and treaties. Prime Minister Mikhail Mishustin's government runs the economy, while the president directly commands the security ministries. Parliamentary approval survives as a formality; the real constraints appear to come from the information and judgment of his inner circle and the security services, from economic conditions, and from the 2022 decision to write the occupied territories into the constitution.

Israel is a parliamentary system without a written constitution; a series of Basic Laws serves in its place. A coalition government that holds the confidence of the 120-seat Knesset runs the country. Decisions on war and major military operations belong to the government — in practice, to the security cabinet acting under authority the government has delegated to it. Powers under the Basic Laws

  • Israel has no written constitution; Basic Laws enacted by the Knesset (on the Knesset, the Government, the Judiciary, and other subjects) serve in its place. Most of their provisions can be amended by an ordinary majority of the Knesset.
  • The Knesset has 120 seats, filled by party-list proportional representation in a single nationwide constituency. Parties that win less than 3.25 percent of the vote receive no seats. Members serve four-year terms.
  • The president, a ceremonial head of state, consults the parties after an election and designates a member of the Knesset to form a government. The designee has 28 days, extendable by 14, to assemble a government that wins the Knesset's confidence; if that fails, a second member gets 28 days, and then a member put forward by 61 lawmakers gets 14. If every attempt fails, new elections are held.
  • The state may begin a war, or a major military operation that is nearly certain to lead to war, only by a decision of the government (Basic Law: The Government, Section 40). The government may delegate this power to the Ministerial Committee on National Security Affairs (the security cabinet), and it did so on a standing basis in 2018. Such decisions must be reported as soon as possible to the Knesset's Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee, and the prime minister must inform the full Knesset. Military action necessary for the defense of the state is exempt from these limits.
  • The government can be replaced or the Knesset dissolved in four ways: a constructive no-confidence vote, in which 61 members back an alternative government (Basic Law: The Government, Section 28); dissolution by the prime minister with the president's consent (Section 29); a Knesset dissolution law (Basic Law: The Knesset, Sections 34 and 35); and automatic dissolution if the budget is not passed by the end of March (or, for a new government, within 145 days of taking office).
  • On January 1, 2024, the Supreme Court for the first time struck down an amendment to a Basic Law — one abolishing judicial review of government decisions on grounds of reasonableness — by a vote of 8–7; 13 of the 15 justices held that Basic Laws themselves are subject to judicial review.

Powers under the constitution

  • As head of state and head of government, the president commands the armed forces, concludes and ratifies treaties, and declares war (Articles 66, 73, and 74). The president serves a single five-year term (Article 70).
  • The National Assembly must consent to treaties on mutual assistance or security and to treaties that limit sovereignty or place a heavy financial burden on the state (Article 60(1)); to declarations of war, the dispatch of troops abroad, and the stationing of foreign forces on South Korean soil (Article 60(2)); and to the appointment of the prime minister (Article 86).
  • The government drafts the budget; the Assembly reviews and approves it. The Assembly may cut spending but cannot raise it or add new items without the government's consent (Articles 54 and 57).
  • The Assembly passes laws. The president may return a bill for reconsideration, after which the Assembly can enact it with two-thirds of members present, provided a majority of all members attend (Article 53).
  • The Assembly's checks include audits and investigations of state affairs (Article 61), summoning the prime minister and cabinet members (Article 62), recommending their dismissal (Article 63, nonbinding), and impeachment (Article 65; impeaching the president requires two-thirds of all members). The Constitutional Court rules on impeachments and on the constitutionality of laws (Article 111).
  • The parliamentary features — the government's right to introduce bills (Article 52), consent to the prime minister's appointment (Article 86), and lawmakers' ability to serve in the cabinet (National Assembly Act, Article 29) — do not give the Assembly the power to install or replace a president. If anything, when the ruling party commands a majority, these features fuse the government and the ruling party.

Power in practice

The Assembly's role depends on who holds the majority

When the ruling party has a majority, the Assembly backs the president's agenda; when the opposition does, it blocks him through budget cuts, legislation, and impeachment. Under the same constitution, the Assembly can be either the president's base of support or his most formidable check.

The Assembly has in fact overturned a president's decision

On December 4, 2024, a majority of the Assembly voted to demand an end to martial law, and martial law was lifted (Article 77). On December 14, it voted to impeach then-President Yoon Suk Yeol, and on April 4, 2025, the Constitutional Court removed him from office.

Deployments and treaties have gone through the Assembly

The deployments to Iraq in 2003 and 2004 and the dispatch and renewals of the Cheonghae Unit, the navy's anti-piracy flotilla, since 2009 all won the Assembly's consent. A motion to ratify the 2018 Panmunjom Declaration was submitted but never taken up.

The Constitutional Court can also strike down tools of North Korea policy

On September 26, 2023, the court overturned a ban on sending leaflets into North Korea — added to the Development of Inter-Korean Relations Act by the National Assembly in 2020 — as an excessive restriction on free expression.

The decision-maker's powers and latitude

With his party's majority, President Lee is well placed to secure the Assembly's consent even for decisions that require it, such as deployments and treaties. But he lacks the two-thirds needed to amend the constitution, and laws passed by the Assembly remain subject to review by the Constitutional Court. Widening overseas deployments — a contribution in the Strait of Hormuz, for instance — would require the Assembly's consent, and in September 2026 even members of the ruling party called for prior consultation by the government and the Assembly's consent.

UkraineSemi-presidential system (mixed parliamentary-presidential) · As of October 6, 2026

Ukraine has a mixed system in which the president commands the armed forces and directs foreign policy, while the 450-seat Verkhovna Rada, the country's parliament, appoints the prime minister and the cabinet. The president decides on war and negotiations, but approving martial law and mobilization, ratifying treaties, and appointing the prime minister are the Rada's prerogatives. Under the martial law in force since February 24, 2022 — currently extended through October 31, 2026 — elections, referendums, and constitutional amendments are all barred. Because changes to the country's territory can be decided only by a nationwide referendum (Article 73 of the constitution), a peace agreement with territorial provisions can be completed only once martial law ends or the law is changed.

Decision-maker
Volodymyr Zelensky President of Ukraine (Supreme Commander-in-Chief) · since May 2019

Zelensky appears to regard Russia as a party that does not honor the commitments it signs and the United States as an indispensable but conditional partner, and to believe that security can come only from legally binding guarantees or alliances. Under U.S. pressure he has accepted reversible concessions, such as a ceasefire and giving up the NATO bid, while handing legal changes to the territory to a referendum; he has moved quickly to replace military commanders after open disagreements, but has kept his closest aides until an outside event forced the issue. Powers under the constitution

  • The president represents the state in foreign relations, directs negotiations and the conclusion of treaties, and, as commander in chief, chairs the National Security and Defense Council (NSDC) (Article 106). The council's decisions take effect by presidential decree (Article 107). The president serves a five-year term and may serve no more than two consecutive terms (Article 103).
  • In the event of a threat of aggression, the president decides on martial law and on general or partial mobilization (Article 106(20)), and the Verkhovna Rada must approve the decree within two days of the president's request (Article 85(31)). Declaring war and concluding peace are also powers of the Rada (Article 85(9)).
  • The Rada adopts the budget, ratifies international treaties by law, and appoints the prime minister and the ministers of defense and foreign affairs on the president's nomination, and the other ministers on the prime minister's nomination (Articles 85 and 114). Its decisions require a majority of its constitutional membership, or 226 seats (Article 91).
  • Changes to the country's territory can be decided only by a nationwide referendum (Article 73). Amending Chapters I and III of the constitution, which contain the provisions on territory and referendums, requires a two-thirds vote of the constitutional membership (300 seats) followed by a referendum (Article 156), and the constitution may not be amended under martial law or a state of emergency (Article 157).
  • If the Rada's term expires under martial law, its powers are extended until the first meeting of a new Rada elected after martial law is lifted (Article 83), and the president continues to exercise the powers of office until a newly elected president takes office (Article 108). The martial law statute bars presidential, parliamentary, and local elections while martial law is in force, and the 2021 referendum law bars referendums.
  • A constitutional amendment adopted on February 7, 2019, by 334 votes enshrined full membership in the EU and NATO as the state's strategic course, with the president as guarantor of its implementation (Article 102).

Power in practice

Martial law has been extended 20 times since 2022

The Verkhovna Rada approved the declaration of martial law on February 24, 2022, the day it was imposed, and has approved extensions since then: the first two for 30 days each, and roughly every 90 days after that. In the 20th such vote, on July 14, 2026, 313 lawmakers voted in favor, extending it to October 31, 2026.

The ruling party's majority exists only on paper

The ruling Servant of the People party holds 229 seats, more than the 226 needed for a majority on paper, but its voting discipline has eroded badly since the uproar over the anti-corruption agencies law in July 2025. On January 13, 2026, the appointment of Denys Shmyhal as first deputy prime minister failed with 210 votes (153 of them from Servant of the People) before passing the next day, and on January 15, ratification of an agreement with BGK, Poland's state development bank, failed with 217 votes. According to an analysis by OSW, the Warsaw-based Centre for Eastern Studies, the president also withdrew his nominees for defense and foreign minister during the July cabinet reshuffle for lack of support.

Elections are on hold by law and by interparty agreement

The parliamentary election due in October 2023 and the presidential election due in March 2024 were not held because of martial law. In November 2023, every party in the Rada signed a memorandum agreeing to hold elections no sooner than six months after martial law ends, and on February 25, 2025, the Rada adopted, by 268 votes, a resolution to hold elections once a sustainable peace is achieved.

Security council sanctions bypass a parliamentary vote

Sanctions imposed by the National Security and Defense Council and enacted by presidential decree on February 13, 2025, targeted five people, including former President Petro Poroshenko, a sitting member of parliament; the Rada played no part. On July 10, 2026, the Supreme Court dismissed Poroshenko's challenge to the sanctions.

The decision-maker's powers and latitude

President Volodymyr Zelensky has remained in office past May 2024 under Article 108 of the constitution and personally decides on negotiations, military command, and sanctions imposed through the National Security and Defense Council. Completing any agreement, however, requires ratification by the Verkhovna Rada and, if territory is at stake, a nationwide referendum — and referendums are banned under martial law. With party discipline weak, he appears to need the support of other factions for ratifications and appointments as well.
