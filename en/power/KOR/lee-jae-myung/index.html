Worldview Assessment

President Lee has described South Korea as a country that must manage its security and economic vulnerabilities together, caught between U.S.-China rivalry and the demands of its alliance, and he has set out three external goals side by side: peaceful coexistence with North Korea, staying out of conflicts Seoul does not choose, and building up self-reliant defense. In his decisions, he appears to take de-escalatory steps toward the North without waiting for a response, and to accept Washington's broad framework while drawing his lines at the terms of implementation and at what the law and National Assembly consent allow.

How he sees the world
Lee has said that if the United States and China pull at the same time, Korea could end up "a shrimp caught in the middle," but that it can widen its room for action through coordination and mediation (in-flight press conference, November 23, 2025, C). Taken together with his remark that the alliance with the United States is the foundation of Seoul's diplomacy but Korea cannot cut itself off from China (August 24, 2025, C), his warning that a lump-sum cash investment could bring on a situation like the 1997 financial crisis (September 21, 2025, C), and a speech saying that security that leans on others' strength wavers when circumstances change (September 30, 2026, E), he appears to see the external environment as a problem of vulnerability in which security and the economy are bound up together. Basis: August 24, 2025 · September 21, 2025 · September 30, 2026
What he wants
In his UN General Assembly address, he said Seoul would not pursue unification by absorption and has no intention of engaging in hostile acts (September 23, 2025, E); at his New Year press conference, he said peaceful coexistence comes first even if unification has to wait (January 21, 2026, C); and on Liberation Day, he proposed multilateral talks among the parties concerned to turn the armistice into a peace regime (August 15, 2026, E). To these he has added a principle of non-intervention, "no deployment of troops to be involved in or intervene in a war" (September 18, 2026, A), and the self-reliant defense goals of building nuclear-powered submarines by the mid-2030s and regaining wartime operational control1 (September 30 and October 1, 2026, E). Basis: September 23, 2025 · January 21, 2026 · August 15, 2026 · September 18, 2026 · September 30, 2026
What he thinks works
Toward North Korea, he has acted first without requiring the other side to move, halting loudspeaker broadcasts a week after taking office (June 11, 2025) and expressing regret over the drone incursion (April 6, 2026). With the United States, he accepted a $350 billion investment total but secured a term of implementation, the $20 billion annual cap (November 14, 2025), and in answer to demands for a Hormuz contribution, he drew his lines at "the scope that our laws and basic policy can bear" (August 19, 2026, C) and at National Assembly consent, going no further than a fact-finding team and a review of stronger protection for merchant ships (September 8 and 18, 2026). As when he praised the U.S. president's order to scale back combined exercises as creating conditions for dialogue (August 19, 2026), he also tends to accept a counterpart's decision as a rationale that fits his own goals. Basis: June 11, 2025 · November 14, 2025 · April 6, 2026 · August 19, 2026 · September 8, 2026 · September 18, 2026
People and process
National Security Adviser Wi Sung-lac coordinates foreign and security decisions, but the president has repeatedly announced conclusions himself at press conferences or in X posts, with Cheong Wa Dae filling in the interpretation the next day (August 19, 2026; September 18–19, 2026). The dismissal of Trade Minister Yeo Han-koo, who led trade negotiations (August 15, 2026), and the acceptance of Chief of Staff for Policy Kim Yong-beom's resignation (September 1, 2026) came quickly and without public explanation, and on North Korea policy, disagreements have been reported over Unification Minister Chung Dong-young's phrase "peaceful two states." Basis: August 19, 2026 · September 18, 2026
Where his words and decisions diverge
In January 2017, as mayor of Seongnam and a contender in the opposition's presidential primary, he wrote that Seoul should demand the withdrawal of THAAD2 and lower cost-sharing and be prepared even for a U.S. troop withdrawal (January 22, 2017, D). Calls to withdraw or reconsider THAAD were not unusual in the opposition at the time (among them Choo Mi-ae, then running for Democratic Party leader, in July 2016 and Seoul Mayor Park Won-soon in January 2017), but no other case was found of anyone raising the prospect of a U.S. troop withdrawal. As president, in an office responsible for managing the alliance, he made strengthening the alliance with the United States a diplomatic principle of his inaugural address, and when U.S. Forces Korea relocated THAAD interceptors, he only conveyed his objection and then accepted it (March 10, 2026). His warning that a cash investment could trigger a financial crisis (September 21, 2025, C) prevailed only in part, in a deal with an annual cap (November 14, 2025), and although he pledged in August 2025 to "take proactive, gradual steps to restore" the September 19 Military Agreement, it had not been restored as of May 2026 amid caution in the defense ministry. Positions he took before coming to power carry little weight in forecasting his decisions; in office, many of his decisions appear to follow the constraints of the alliance and of institutions. Basis: January 22, 2017 · September 21, 2025 · November 14, 2025 · March 10, 2026

Relation to the country's strategic culture Assessment

President Lee invokes, unprompted, Korea's historical memory of being a small country wedged between great powers ("when whales fight, the shrimp's back is broken") but recasts it as room to widen Seoul's scope for action through coordination and mediation. In the broad frame of his decisions, he appears to have inherited both the postwar tradition of treating the alliance with the United States as the foundation and the line of reconciliation with North Korea that dates from the Kim Dae-jung government. Yet he picks from these traditions across camps: he takes the Roh Moo-hyun government's principle of non-intervention without using its label of "balancer3," and he places Park Chung Hee and Roh Moo-hyun together in a lineage of self-reliant defense. On the same traditions, Roh chose to send troops to Iraq, the Moon Jae-in government chose an independent deployment to Hormuz, and Yoon Suk Yeol chose to institutionalize trilateral cooperation with the United States and Japan and to suspend the September 19 Military Agreement. The judgment of this section, therefore, is that strategic culture is less a cause of his decisions than a condition that sets the range of options and the language of justification.

Inherited from the national tradition

The historical memory of a small country wedged between great powers. At an in-flight press conference on the way home from the G20 on November 23, 2025, Lee said that if the United States and China pull at the same time, Korea "could end up a shrimp caught in the middle" but can widen its room for action through coordination and mediation. As a presidential candidate on December 5, 2021, he attributed the defeat at the Battle of Ugeumchi4 in 1894 to a lack of science, technology, and weapons, that is, of national strength, and said Korea must become a leading nation so that the same thing never happens again. On September 30, 2026, he said that security that leans on others' strength wavers when circumstances change. On the other hand, former Foreign Minister Yoon Young-kwan argued on March 11, 2026, that Korea should move beyond the idea of the shrimp whose back breaks between two whales, consolidate trilateral cooperation with the United States and Japan, and widen its horizons to the world, a reminder that the same memory can yield different prescriptions. Basis: September 21, 2025 · September 30, 2026 · December 5, 2021 · November 23, 2025

He has inherited the postwar tradition of treating the alliance with the United States as the foundation of security. His inaugural address (June 4, 2025, based on the English full text) pledged to strengthen the alliance with the United States, bolster Korea-U.S.-Japan cooperation on that basis, and manage relations with neighboring countries from the standpoint of national interest and pragmatism, and on August 24, 2025, he again called the alliance the foundation of Seoul's diplomacy. His decisions have likewise stayed within the broad frame of the alliance: the $350 billion investment agreement (November 14, 2025), signing the U.S.-led Pax Silica5 supply-chain declaration (December 12, 2025), and accepting the relocation of THAAD interceptors by U.S. Forces Korea (March 10, 2026). But his reservation that strategic flexibility6 is "not an issue we can easily agree to" also suggests a tendency to keep the alliance's scope centered on the defense of the Korean Peninsula. Basis: August 24, 2025 · November 14, 2025 · December 12, 2025 · March 10, 2026 · June 4, 2025

He continues the Kim Dae-jung government's line of reconciliation with North Korea (the Sunshine Policy7). In his inaugural address on February 25, 1998, President Kim set out three principles: no tolerance of armed provocation, no intention to harm or absorb the North, and reconciliation and cooperation starting from areas where they are possible. In his Liberation Day address on August 15, 2025, President Lee said Seoul respects the North's current system, "will not pursue any form of unification by absorption," and has no intention of engaging in hostile acts. The halt to loudspeaker broadcasts (June 11, 2025) and the expression of regret over the drone incursion (April 6, 2026) appear to be decisions that carried out this line first, without a response from the other side, and he accepted domestic criticism that he was giving up means of pressure on the North for nothing. Large-scale economic cooperation of the kind pursued under Kim Dae-jung is blocked by UN sanctions, however, so what he has inherited seems closer to the language of easing tensions and recognizing the North's system than to economic exchange. Basis: June 12, 2020 · June 11, 2025 · September 23, 2025 · April 6, 2026 · August 15, 2025

Roots outside the tradition

The memory of the 1997 financial crisis and a trading nation's sense of economic security have roots different from the geopolitical tradition of survival among great powers. Lee likened the $350 billion cash investment Washington demanded to the 1997 crisis and used the comparison as an argument for refusing it (September 21, 2025), and right after the U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran he ordered the swift execution of a 100 trillion won market-stabilization program (March 5, 2026). In his address on the 17th anniversary of former President Kim Dae-jung's death on August 18, 2026 (read on his behalf by Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik), he singled out for praise not Kim's North Korea policy but his overcoming of the financial crisis and his building of a high-speed information and communications network. His way of weighing security issues together with financial and trade vulnerabilities appears closer to this experience. Basis: September 21, 2025 · March 5, 2026 · August 18, 2026

His habit of treating law and procedure as the boundaries of a decision also appears to have roots other than strategic culture, and seems tied to his career as a lawyer, a city mayor, and a provincial governor. As governor of Gyeonggi Province, he stopped leaflet launches by designating danger zones under the Framework Act on the Management of Disasters and Safety (June 12, 2020); as president, he treated the tariff memorandum of understanding as not subject to ratification by the National Assembly and took the route of a special act (November 14, 2025); and on a Hormuz contribution, he made "the scope that our laws and basic policy can bear" and National Assembly consent his criteria (August 19 and September 18, 2026). These criteria appear also to have served as a way of limiting a counterpart's demands while avoiding the word "no." Basis: June 12, 2020 · November 14, 2025 · September 8, 2026 · September 18, 2026

How history is used selectively

From the Roh Moo-hyun government's legacy, he takes the principle of non-intervention but not the label of "balancer." At the Air Force Academy commencement on March 8, 2005, President Roh said the Korean people would not be drawn into conflicts in Northeast Asia against their will, and at the commencement of the Korea Army Academy at Yeongcheon on March 22, 2005, he spoke of "the role of a balancer for peace and prosperity in Northeast Asia." President Lee's remarks on staying out of the Taiwan dispute (January 12, 2026) and his ruling out a deployment to intervene in war (September 18, 2026) overlap with the first of these principles, but as far as this research found, he uses "coordination and mediation" and "pragmatic diplomacy centered on the national interest" instead of "balancer," and he included strengthened cooperation with the United States and Japan in his inaugural address. In 2005, Kim Tae-hyo, then a professor, and others criticized the balancer idea as liable to be read as a signal that Seoul was drifting away from Washington and Tokyo; no material was found in which Lee himself explained why he avoids the term. Basis: January 12, 2026 · September 18, 2026 · June 4, 2025 · November 23, 2025

He draws on a lineage of self-reliant defense that cuts across political camps. In his Armed Forces Day address on October 1, 2026, he invoked both President Park Chung Hee, who launched the defense industry with the aim of making even a single rifle at home, and President Roh Moo-hyun, who pushed defense reform and the transfer of wartime operational control, and pledged to build nuclear-powered submarines by the mid-2030s and to regain wartime operational control. Roh called regaining wartime operational control "putting the nation's sovereignty right" on August 15, 2006; Lee said on September 30, 2026, that "self-reliant defense is sovereignty." As far as this research found, however, he has not repeated since taking office his talk as mayor of Seongnam of being prepared for a U.S. troop withdrawal (January 22, 2017), and the language of self-reliant defense came after the scaling back of combined exercises (August 2026) and the pressure over Hormuz (September 2026). It therefore appears to serve less as a line that replaces the alliance than as a language of justification running alongside it. Basis: January 22, 2017 · September 30, 2026 · October 1, 2026

Checked against classics and scholarship

Victor D. Cha · Alignment Despite Antagonism: The United States-Korea-Japan Security Triangle (Stanford, 1999); supplemented by Powerplay: The Origins of the American Alliance System in Asia (Princeton, 2016) Conditional

Interpretation
Cha proposed a "quasi-alliance" model in which South Korea and Japan cooperate when their fears of abandonment8 and entrapment by the United States are symmetrical and clash when they are asymmetrical, and tested it against the periods of the Nixon Doctrine (1969–71), détente (1972–74), and Carter's troop-withdrawal plan (1975–79). In his 2016 book, he traced Washington's choice of bilateral alliances in Asia to a desire to restrain risk-taking by allied leaders such as Syngman Rhee and Chiang Kai-shek.
Comparison
The framework's basic proposition, that the side fearing entrapment keeps its alliance commitments weak, fits Lee's staying out of the Taiwan dispute (January 12, 2026), his ruling out a deployment to intervene in war (September 18, 2026), and his reservations on strategic flexibility for U.S. Forces Korea (August 24, 2025). The U.S. president's de facto unilateral reduction of combined exercises (August 2026) and relocation of THAAD interceptors (March 2026) also appear to echo the 2016 book's argument about control exercised through alliances.
Where it falls short
By the model, mounting fear of abandonment should lead Seoul to strengthen its alliance commitments or widen cooperation with Japan. Yet even after the U.S. president raised the size of U.S. Forces Korea and cut combined exercises, Lee ruled out sending combat troops, and his response ran less toward stronger commitments than toward the language of self-reliant defense (September 30, 2026) and higher defense spending. This research could not establish Japan's fear of abandonment or the level of Korea-Japan security cooperation in the same period, so the hypothesis about Korea-Japan cooperation was not tested. There are also limits to applying a model built on Cold War evidence to an individual leader in 2026.

David C. Kang · China Rising: Peace, Power, and Order in East Asia (Columbia, 2007); supplemented by "Between Balancing and Bandwagoning: South Korea's Response to China," Journal of East Asian Studies 9(1), 2009, pp. 1–28 Conditional

Interpretation
Kang argued that East Asian states have seen more benefit than danger in China's rise and so have chosen accommodation over balancing, and that historically the China-centered hierarchical order kept the peace through norms as well as power. Of South Korea, he argued that it sees China more as an economic opportunity than a military threat and has deepened interdependence while barely changing its military posture or alliance structure (2009 article abstract).
Comparison
The agreement on a "full restoration" of Korea-China relations (January 5, 2026), the position describing "one China" in terms of "respect" rather than "principle" (January 2, 2026), staying out of the Taiwan dispute (January 12, 2026), and the remark that Korea cannot cut itself off from China (August 24, 2025) fit Kang's model of accommodating China while maintaining the alliance.
Where it falls short
Signing the U.S.-led Pax Silica supply-chain declaration (December 12, 2025) was a decision to reduce non-market practices and dependence, and the push to build nuclear-powered submarines (the November 2025 fact sheet; the October 1, 2026, address) runs against the observation that Seoul has barely changed its military posture. Lee also justifies relations with China in the language of economics and pragmatism rather than hierarchy or cultural affinity, which seems closer to an explanation based on calculations of interest than to Kang's cultural and hierarchical account. A review by Jenny Kim also criticizes the 2007 book for having been written before China's assertiveness became pronounced.

Min-hyung Kim · Hedging between the United States and China? South Korea's ideology-driven behavior and its implications for national security, International Relations of the Asia-Pacific 23(1), 2023, pp.129-158 Conditional

Interpretation
Kim argued that South Korea's behavior between the United States and China cannot be explained by hedging theory alone, and that domestic ideology divides its decisions: conservatives prioritize the alliance and a hard line toward the North, progressives autonomy and engagement with the North. As examples he cited the Moon Jae-in government's "three noes" on THAAD, its hint at terminating the Korea-Japan General Security of Military Information Agreement (GSOMIA), its push for the transfer of wartime operational control, and its reluctance to join the Indo-Pacific strategy.
Comparison
The halt to loudspeaker broadcasts (June 11, 2025), the regret over the drone incursion (April 6, 2026), the priority on peaceful coexistence (January 21, 2026), the pledge to regain wartime operational control (October 1, 2026), and staying out of the Taiwan dispute (January 12, 2026) overlap with the cluster the article identifies as characteristic of progressive ideology.
Where it falls short
But Lee has also made alliance-first decisions that the article attributes to conservatives, such as strengthening Korea-U.S.-Japan cooperation in his inaugural address, signing Pax Silica (December 12, 2025), accepting the relocation of THAAD interceptors (March 10, 2026), and the $350 billion U.S. investment (November 14, 2025), and he has named President Park Chung Hee as the starting point of self-reliant defense. Structural conditions, namely dependence on the alliance and trade pressure, rather than ideology appear to have narrowed the range of his decisions. This section does not adopt the article's normative assessment of the Moon Jae-in government's decisions.

Strategic culture should be seen not as what caused President Lee's decisions but as a condition that sets the range of options and the language of justification. On the same traditions, President Roh Moo-hyun, who spoke of a "balancer," sent engineering and medical units to Iraq with the National Assembly's consent on April 2, 2003; the Moon Jae-in government, which had set out the "three noes" on THAAD (Foreign Minister Kang Kyung-wha's answer in the National Assembly on October 30, 2017), extended the Cheonghae Unit's area of operations to Hormuz on January 21, 2020, opting for an independent operation; and President Yoon Suk Yeol signed the Camp David joint statement of the United States, Japan, and South Korea, which named the Taiwan Strait, on August 18, 2023, and suspended the September 19 Military Agreement on June 4, 2024. President Lee's next decision should therefore not be forecast from an essentialist claim that Korea has always walked a tightrope between great powers.

Domestic coalition and room to maneuver Assessment

President Lee was elected in June 2025 with 49.42 percent of the vote, and he stands on the support of the Democratic Party, which holds a majority of 161 seats in the National Assembly; the camp favoring engagement with North Korea within the party and the government; organized labor, including the Federation of Korean Trade Unions, and progressive civic groups; and centrist and independent voters he courted by describing his party as "center-right." Reconciliation with the North and non-intervention in war match the demands of his core supporters, so he can push them hard. On the alliance and trade, however, he appears to have repeatedly leaned toward centrist voters and U.S. pressure, accepting opposition from his core supporters, and on deployments, which require National Assembly consent, opposition within the ruling party appears to act as a real brake.

Coalition

The Democratic Party and its majority in the National Assembly (161 seats, after the June 2026 by-elections)It wants to win the April 2028 general election and keep the government's approval up, and on politically costly votes such as deployments and the U.S. investment, its members want the views of their districts and support bases heard. In the September 2026 debate over a Hormuz contribution, members including Lee Ki-heon, Kim Byung-joo, and Song Young-gil publicly opposed a deployment, while Park Jie-won said sending non-combat personnel was unavoidable, splitting the party; the leadership issued no official position before the government's decision.
Likely the greatest. Consent to overseas deployments (Article 60(2) of the Constitution), the Special Act on Investment in the United States, and the budget all go through the National Assembly. With Kim Min-seok, reported to have had the president's backing, taking over the party in August 2026, the chance of a clash between the party and Cheong Wa Dae has fallen, but on issues such as a deployment, where defections by members have been aired in public, a ruling-party majority does not appear to guarantee passage.
The engagement and autonomy camp on North Korea within the party and the government (Unification Minister Chung Dong-young, National Intelligence Service Director Lee Jong-seok, former party leader Jung Chung-rae, and former officials such as Jeong Se-hyun and Moon Chung-in)It wants inter-Korean relations put ahead of coordination with Washington, de facto recognition of the North's system under the "peaceful two states" formula, and a change to the arrangement under which the Ministry of Foreign Affairs leads consultations with the United States on North Korea policy. In December 2025, Jung Chung-rae, then party leader, sided with Minister Chung, saying that inter-Korean relations would be constrained if every step had to "get Washington's sign-off."
Likely large on North Korea. The May 2026 Unification White Paper included the phrase "peaceful two states oriented toward unification," and the direction matches decisions such as the halt to loudspeaker broadcasts and the regret over the drone incursion. But because the president also employs alliance-minded figures such as National Security Adviser Wi Sung-lac, its influence on the alliance and trade appears limited.
Organized labor and progressive civic groups (the Federation of Korean Trade Unions, the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions, and peace and antiwar groups such as the People's Solidarity for Participatory Democracy)At an extraordinary convention of delegates on April 29, 2025, the Federation of Korean Trade Unions (FKTU) voted with 89.73 percent to back the Democratic Party and signed a policy agreement with it, while the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions (KCTU) endorsed no candidate. On October 21, 2025, 574 organizations, including the KCTU, demanded a full review of the U.S. investment, telling the government it "must not give away anything more," and in September 2026, the People's Solidarity for Participatory Democracy (PSPD) and other groups opposed any form of deployment to Hormuz and demanded that the president declare a refusal.
Appears moderate. These groups have influence in street mobilization and in shaping public opposition to a deployment, but in August 2026 the president, braving the KCTU's anger, ordered that the scope of labor disputes under the Yellow Envelope9 Law be set by enforcement decree, and he went ahead with the U.S. investment deal, so on economic and trade issues their demands do not appear to have constrained his decisions.
Centrist and independent votersThey appear to prioritize economic stability and management of the alliance and to keep their distance from North Korea policy language that seems to unsettle the constitutional commitment to unification. As party leader on February 19, 2025, Lee targeted them by saying that "the Democratic Party is at heart a center-right party that values growth," which drew objections from some in the party, including former Prime Minister Kim Boo-kyum.
Likely large on alliance and trade decisions. Lee won with less than a majority of the vote (49.42 percent), his job approval in Gallup Korea polling fell in the third week of September 2026 to 37 percent, the lowest of his presidency, and decisions such as the $350 billion investment deal and acceptance of the THAAD interceptor relocation have been closer to these voters' preference for stability than to the wishes of his core supporters.

Decisions that strained the coalition

December 2025When the Ministry of Unification said it would stay out of the Korea-U.S. consultations on North Korea policy led by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and hold separate talks with Washington, Jung Chung-rae, then Democratic Party leader, sided with Minister Chung Dong-young at a meeting of the party's Supreme Council, saying it would be hard to find a way to unlock inter-Korean relations if every step had to "get Washington's sign-off." Earlier, in September and October 2025, the disagreement had become public when National Security Adviser Wi Sung-lac rebutted Minister Chung's "peaceful two states" remarks, saying the government does not accept a two-state theory.
How it was handled · At the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Ministry of Unification policy briefings on December 19, 2025, the president took neither side, saying "it helps for each ministry to have its own position," and ordered the creation of a meeting of security-related ministers bringing together the foreign, unification, and defense ministries. The May 2026 Unification White Paper included the phrase "peaceful two states oriented toward unification," and the Ministry of Unification explained that this did not mean recognizing North Korea as a state in law.
March 2026When U.S. Forces Korea shipped out six THAAD launchers and other equipment from the Seongju base for the Iran operation, civic groups that have long campaigned against the THAAD deployment, including the Peace Council for THAAD Withdrawal, said this showed THAAD had never been essentially about defending against North Korea and demanded that the remaining radar be withdrawn as well. The demand points in the same direction as the THAAD withdrawal the president called for in 2017 as mayor of Seongnam.
How it was handled · The president conveyed his objection but did not block the relocation, and Cheong Wa Dae said the movement of some U.S. Forces Korea assets abroad reflected strategic flexibility, adding, with reference to the size of the defense budget and the level of the defense industry, that people "need not worry too much." As far as this research found, the government did not ask for the radar to be withdrawn.
September 2026As the government weighed sending a fact-finding team and non-combat options in response to President Trump's demand for a Hormuz contribution, Democratic Party members including Lee Ki-heon, Kim Byung-joo, and Song Young-gil publicly opposed a deployment, and Kim Joon-hyung of the Rebuilding Korea Party alleged that the government had already decided on a deployment and was merely testing public opinion. On September 16, a coalition of civic groups including the PSPD demanded that the president declare at his September 18 press conference that he would refuse to deploy.
How it was handled · On September 18, the president declared that there would be "no deployment of troops to be involved in or intervene in a war," largely meeting the demands of opponents within the party, but he left open a review of stronger protection of merchant ships by the Cheonghae10 Unit, and the next day Cheong Wa Dae explained that he had not rejected the U.S. request. On September 23, the same coalition of civic groups judged the statement insufficient, saying it left room for a workaround.
August 2026When the president repeatedly instructed the labor minister at Cabinet meetings to set, by enforcement decree, the criteria for what may be the subject of labor disputes under the amended Trade Union Act (the Yellow Envelope Law), the KCTU denounced this as "siding with business and overstepping authority." After the labor ministry issued enforcement guidelines in September that excluded performance bonuses and plant relocations and sales from mandatory bargaining, the KCTU began a two-week sit-in on September 7 in front of the Seoul Regional Employment and Labor Office.
How it was handled · The president did not withdraw his instruction, saying that setting the details needed to implement a law is a power the Constitution gives the executive, and the labor ministry implemented the guidelines unchanged.

Room to maneuver

On North Korea, his core supporters demand engagement, so he has ample room to go further toward easing tensions, but centrist voters and the debate over Articles 3 and 4 of the Constitution appear to constrain him from cementing "two states" as legal recognition or rushing to restore the September 19 Military Agreement. On Hormuz, public opposition from ruling-party lawmakers and civic groups, public opposition to a deployment reported at 55 percent, and the requirement of National Assembly consent combine to leave very little room to yield toward involvement in combat; an adjustment of the Cheonghae Unit's role centered on protecting merchant ships appears to be the ceiling. On the U.S. investment and semiconductor tariffs, his core supporters' opposition has not constrained his decisions, so there is room to concede more on totals and the finalizing of projects; but after President Trump announced projects on October 1, 2026 (nuclear power plants and Alaska LNG), the Progressive Party demanded they be nullified and the People Power Party demanded an explanation, saying they differed from what had been reported to the National Assembly, so commitments beyond what has been reported to the Assembly look costly. Lee has said he will not seek a second term, but the party still has its interests ahead of the 2028 general election, so if his job approval falls further, he may harden toward his supporters' positions on alliance issues.

Words before and after a change of office

Differences in his words as his office changed are large on the alliance and U.S. Forces Korea and small on Taiwan and engagement with North Korea. On January 22, 2017, as mayor of Seongnam and a contender in the opposition primary, he wrote that Seoul should demand lower cost-sharing and the withdrawal of THAAD "even if prepared for a U.S. troop withdrawal" (D). As president, however, he made strengthening the alliance a diplomatic principle in his inaugural address, only conveyed his objection when THAAD launchers were shipped out in March 2026, and, as far as this research found, did not take up civic groups' demand that the radar be withdrawn. His 2017 position appears to have been not unusual in the opposition at the time: Choo Mi-ae, then running for Democratic Party leader, said on July 16, 2016, that the opposition naturally opposed the THAAD deployment and demanded National Assembly consent; Seoul Mayor Park Won-soon called for a full reconsideration of the deployment on January 16, 2017; and former party leader Sohn Hak-kyu opposed THAAD in February 2017, saying it would heighten tensions and an arms race. No other contender, however, was found to have gone as far as speaking of being prepared for a U.S. troop withdrawal, and Lee's own position shifted even as a candidate: in late 2016 he floated a compromise of a "time-limited deployment" before returning to calls for withdrawal in January 2017. By contrast, his remark on non-intervention in cross-strait issues as opposition leader (March 22, 2024) carried over into his statements in office on "respecting one China" and staying out of the Taiwan dispute (January 2026), so the gap there is small.

Earlier leaders who faced the same structure

President Roh Moo-hyun's motion to send troops to Iraq passed on April 2, 2003, by 179 to 68, with nine abstentions, but the 96 members of the ruling Millennium Democratic Party split 49 in favor, 43 against, and four abstaining; in March 2007, Kim Geun-tae, a former chairman of the ruling party, and Chun Jung-bae, who had left it, went on hunger strike to demand a halt to the Korea-U.S. FTA negotiations. President Moon Jae-in went ahead with the deployment of four additional THAAD launchers on September 7, 2017, drawing criticism from Kim Jong-dae of the Justice Party that a promise had been trampled within two months, and on January 21, 2020, he decided on an independent deployment that extended the Cheonghae Unit's area of operations to Hormuz.

Checked against theory

Robert D. Putnam · Diplomacy and Domestic Politics: The Logic of Two-Level Games, International Organization 42(3), 1988, pp.427-460 Conditional

Interpretation

Conversely, pressure from the other country can sway domestic opinion and widen the win-set ("reverberation," p. 455), and a negotiator may use international negotiations to shift the domestic balance of power toward the policies he prefers (p. 457).

Key terms

  1. Wartime operational control (OPCON) · The authority to command South Korean forces in wartime. It is held by the commander of the ROK-U.S. Combined Forces Command, a U.S. four-star general. ↩
  2. THAAD · Terminal High Altitude Area Defense, a missile defense system. U.S. Forces Korea deployed it in Seongju, North Gyeongsang Province, in 2017. ↩
  3. Northeast Asian balancer · The idea, put forward by the Roh Moo-hyun government in 2005, that South Korea would act as the balancer of power in Northeast Asia. ↩
  4. Battle of Ugeumchi · The 1894 battle at Ugeumchi, near Gongju, in which the Donghak peasant army suffered a crushing defeat at the hands of Joseon government troops and the Japanese army. ↩
  5. Pax Silica · A U.S.-led declaration on semiconductor and AI supply-chain cooperation, adopted at its first meeting on December 12, 2025. South Korea is a signatory. ↩
  6. Strategic flexibility · The concept that U.S. Forces Korea can be used in conflicts beyond the Korean Peninsula. Seoul and Washington agreed on the principle in 2006. ↩
  7. Sunshine Policy · The Kim Dae-jung government's policy of reconciliation and cooperation with North Korea. ↩
  8. Entrapment and abandonment · The two fears of alliance theory: entrapment is being dragged into an ally's conflict; abandonment is being deserted by an ally when it is needed. ↩
  9. Yellow Envelope Act · The popular name for amendments to South Korea's trade union law that widen subcontracted workers' right to bargain with the principal contractor and limit damage claims against strikes. ↩
  10. Cheonghae Unit · A South Korean naval unit deployed to the Gulf of Aden and other waters since 2009 to fight piracy and escort ships. ↩
  11. Win-set · In Robert Putnam's two-level game theory, the range of international agreements that domestic constituencies will accept. The narrower the range, the less room a leader has to make concessions. ↩
  12. Selectorate · In the theory developed by Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and others, the set of people who can influence the choice of a leader. The core within it that actually keeps the leader in power is called the winning coalition. ↩
  13. Extended deterrence · The U.S. commitment to provide allies with deterrence backed by its military power, including nuclear weapons. ↩
