Power Structures · Armed forces

The armed forces of South Korea

Command · Capabilities · Deployment · Posture changes · As of October 8, 2026

← Power Structures · South KoreaDecision-maker · Lee Jae-myungSouth Korea's National AssemblyCountry profile · South Korea

The president commands South Korea’s armed forces, but a declaration of war or an overseas deployment requires the National Assembly’s consent, and martial law must be lifted if a majority of the Assembly’s total membership demands it. In September 2026, a trial court sentenced the commanders who carried out the December 3, 2024, martial law decree to prison terms of nine to 18 years. Wartime operational control still rests with the commander of the Combined Forces Command, a U.S. four-star general, and the government aims to set a transition year at the Security Consultative Meeting in November 2026. Active-duty strength is falling toward roughly 450,000, yet the proposed 2027 defense budget tops ₩73 trillion and leans heavily on missiles, surveillance assets, and nuclear-powered submarines. Forces remain concentrated along the front facing the Military Demarcation Line, in the capital region, and on the Northwest Islands.

Command and control

The president is commander in chief of the armed forces (Article 74 of the Constitution). The minister of national defense, acting on the president’s orders, directs and supervises the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the service chiefs of staff, while the JCS chairman, on the minister’s orders, directs and supervises the operational units of each service (Armed Forces Organization Act, Articles 8 and 9). Wartime operational control, however, belongs to the commander of the Combined Forces Command: in a war, the operations of most South Korean forces would be directed through a combined command led by a U.S. four-star general. The emergency martial law declared on December 3, 2024, stood for only about two and a half hours before the National Assembly demanded its repeal, and the president lifted it in the early hours of December 4. Since then, the military leaders who executed the decree have faced criminal punishment, the Martial Law Act has been amended, and the Defense Counterintelligence Command has been dissolved. Civilian control of the military is being reinforced in both law and institutional design.

  • The South Korean military has seized power twice, in the May 16 coup of 1961 and the December 12 coup of 1979. Since democratization in 1987, there has been no military-led coup attempt.
  • Under the martial law of December 3, 2024, the martial law commander and the heads of the Special Warfare Command, the Capital Defense Command, the Defense Counterintelligence Command, and the Defense Intelligence Command sent troops to the National Assembly and the National Election Commission, but the troops withdrew once the Assembly demanded that martial law be lifted.
  • On February 19, 2026, the Seoul Central District Court sentenced former President Yoon Suk Yeol to life imprisonment as ringleader of an insurrection and former Defense Minister Kim Yong-hyun to 30 years (Dailian). On September 21, the same court sentenced former Counterintelligence Commander Yeo In-hyung to 18 years, former Capital Defense Commander Lee Jin-woo to 15, former Defense Intelligence Commander Moon Sang-ho to 12, former Army Chief of Staff Park An-su, who served as martial law commander, to 10, and former Special Warfare Commander Kwak Jong-geun to nine (Financial News). All are first-instance rulings.
  • On September 1, 2025, the Lee Jae-myung administration replaced all seven active-duty four-star generals who had led the military during martial law (Herald Business), and JCS Chairman Jin Yong-sung reportedly ordered the replacement of every general officer on the Joint Chiefs staff in November 2025 (Herald Business).
  • The Ministry of National Defense announced on June 10, 2026, that it would dissolve the Defense Counterintelligence Command; after Cabinet approval on July 21, it inaugurated the successor bodies, including the Defense Counterintelligence Headquarters, on August 3 (Yonhap). In its October 7 report to the parliamentary audit, the ministry said it would seek an amendment to the Framework Act on Military Status and Service giving service members a legal basis to refuse unlawful orders (Financial News).

Capabilities

A falling birth rate has shrunk South Korea’s active-duty force from about 500,000 in 2022 to about 450,000 as of July 2025, and the conscription pool of 20-year-old men is projected to fall to about 143,000 by 2040. The government is trying to offset the loss of manpower with missiles, surveillance and reconnaissance assets, and unmanned systems, and its 2027 budget proposal raises funding by 29 percent for the Korean three-axis system—Kill Chain, Korea Air and Missile Defense, and Korea Massive Punishment and Retaliation—built to counter North Korea’s nuclear and missile threat. South Korea has no nuclear weapons. A special bill to build the nuclear-powered submarines Washington approved in November 2025 cleared a Cabinet meeting on September 29, 2026.

Proficiency

Proficiency · judgment estimate
Upper-middle · Proficiency maintained through combined U.S.-South Korea exercises, without combat experience, is under downward pressure Steady to slightly declining (judgment estimate). Passing FOC verification in November 2026 would effectively confirm South Korea’s ability to lead a combined command
  • South Korea’s last large-scale combat was the Vietnam War; later engagements, such as the 2010 shelling of Yeonpyeong Island, have been limited clashes.
  • Combined field training for the March 2026 Freedom Shield (FS) exercise was planned at 22 events, fewer than half of the previous year’s 51, and events at brigade level or above fell from 13 to six (JCS official, Herald Business).
  • On President Trump’s orders, the August 2026 Ulchi Freedom Shield (UFS) exercise was cut to end on August 21 instead of August 27, and some field training was scaled back. The Ministry of National Defense said the planned joint U.S.-South Korea evaluation went ahead as scheduled (News Tomato).
  • In July 2026, it emerged that I Corps had changed its guidelines so that crew-served weapons such as the K6 machine gun were not loaded with live rounds during GOP and GP guard duty; the Ground Operations Command began inspecting guard operations across all its subordinate corps on July 30 (Segye Ilbo).
  • Army conscripts serve just 18 months, and recruiting the career officers and noncommissioned officers who form the core of a skilled force remains difficult. The government plans to raise the monthly pay of an entry-level staff sergeant to about ₩3 million in 2027 and increase the commissioning bonus for short-term NCOs to ₩12 million (Segye Ilbo).

Deployment

South Korea’s forces are weighted toward the front along the Military Demarcation Line (MDL) and toward the capital region. The Ground Operations Command oversees five regional corps (the Capital Corps and I, II, III, and V Corps) and the VII Maneuver Corps, which together guard the armistice line and defend the capital area. Baengnyeong and Yeonpyeong islands, near the Northern Limit Line (NLL) in the Yellow Sea, are held by the Marine-led Northwest Islands Defense Command. U.S. Forces Korea is concentrated south of the capital region, at Pyeongtaek, Osan, and elsewhere, and in March 2026 some of its air defense assets were moved out of the country; The Washington Post, citing U.S. officials, reported that part of the THAAD system had been sent to the Middle East. South Korea keeps overseas contingents such as the Cheonghae Unit, but they are small; its strategic weight lies on the peninsula.
