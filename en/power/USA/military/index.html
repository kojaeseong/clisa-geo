Power Structures · Armed forces

The armed forces of the United States

Command · Capabilities · Deployment · Posture changes · As of October 8, 2026

← Power Structures · The United StatesDecision-maker · Donald TrumpU.S. CongressCountry profile · The United States

Command of the U.S. military rests with the president, who alone can order the use of nuclear weapons. With the world's largest defense budget ($954 billion in 2025) and 11 aircraft carriers, the United States far outstrips every other country in its ability to project power across oceans. But the 2026 Iran war consumed Patriot and THAAD interceptors in large numbers, leaving less to spare for other theaters. Forces stationed abroad are concentrated in the Indo-Pacific and Europe; actual combat power in the Middle East comes from rotating carrier and amphibious groups.

Command and control

The Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war and the president command of the armed forces, but in practice it is the president who starts and stops the use of force. In the 2026 Iran war, the administration sidestepped the War Powers Resolution's 60-day clock by sending Congress a letter on May 1, the day the deadline expired, declaring that hostilities had terminated. The House (June 3) and the Senate (June 23) adopted a concurrent resolution directing withdrawal, but because such a resolution never goes to the president for signature, it is regarded as legally nonbinding. Congress's real lever is the conditions it attaches to the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA): the fiscal 2026 law bars the use of funds to reduce U.S. forces in Europe below 76,000 or in South Korea below 28,500. At home, the Supreme Court narrowed the president's authority to federalize the National Guard on December 23, 2025.

  • The United States has had no national military coup or coup attempt since its founding, and the Powell & Thyne coup dataset records no U.S. case. There is no regime-protection force. Because Washington, D.C., has no governor, however, its National Guard is commanded directly by the president.
  • President Donald Trump fired Gen. Charles Q. Brown Jr., chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, on February 21, 2025; Adm. Lisa Franchetti, the chief of naval operations, and Gen. James Slife, the Air Force vice chief of staff, were fired the same day (Military Times).
  • On August 22, 2025, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth fired Lt. Gen. Jeffrey Kruse, director of the Defense Intelligence Agency, whose damage assessment of the strikes on Iran's nuclear facilities had stirred controversy (Military Times). On October 16, 2025, Hegseth announced that Adm. Alvin Holsey, commander of U.S. Southern Command, who directed the strikes on drug boats, would retire at year's end, about one year into the job (USNI News). Media reports also described disagreement over the strikes.
  • On April 2, 2026, in the middle of the Iran war, Gen. Randy George, the Army chief of staff, retired immediately at Hegseth's demand; the same day Hegseth also removed Gen. David Hodne, head of Army Transformation and Training Command, and Maj. Gen. William Green, the Army's chief of chaplains (Defense News). Army Secretary Daniel Driscoll, whose relationship with Hegseth was reported to be icy, resigned on August 31 (Axios).
  • On September 30, 2026, Hegseth announced a further cut that, together with last year's 10 percent reduction, would shrink general and flag officer positions by a total of 20 percent by January 1, 2027 (Al Jazeera). More than 20 military leaders have reportedly been fired or pushed into retirement since January 2025 (Al Jazeera).
  • Checks come not from within the military but from the courts and Congress. After the Supreme Court's December 2025 ruling, Trump withdrew National Guard deployments to Chicago, Los Angeles, and Portland, and in January 2026 he held off on invoking the Insurrection Act. Gen. Dan Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, repeatedly raised the risk of interceptor shortages in private during the Iran war, according to multiple media reports (Al Jazeera).

Capabilities

With 11 aircraft carriers, 32 large amphibious ships, and the world's largest defense budget, the United States far outstrips every other country in its ability to project power across distant oceans. But the Iran war that began on February 28, 2026, has consumed large quantities of Patriot and THAAD interceptors and of long-range strike weapons such as the Tomahawk, and the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) estimates that restoring key munitions to prewar levels will take one to four years. Should a crisis on the Korean Peninsula or in the Taiwan Strait coincide with the ongoing Iran war, missile defense and long-range strike munitions could be the first to run short.

Proficiency

Proficiency · judgment estimate
Combat experience among the world's best · Training and munitions stocks declining in units not deployed to combat Proficiency among the world's highest (judgment estimate). If the Iran war drags on, however, readiness in nondeployed fighter units and amphibious forces will likely decline further
  • In little more than a year, U.S. forces fought a string of large-scale operations: the June 2025 strikes on Iran's nuclear facilities, the January 3, 2026, operation to capture Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro, and the Iran war since February 28, 2026. Operation Epic Fury, in the Iran war, drew on 25 Air Force squadrons (about 325 aircraft), according to Mitchell Institute data (Military Times, September 11, 2026).
  • Fighter pilots averaged about 5.5 sorties a month in FY2023, roughly 60 percent of the combat-readiness requirement of nine to 10; the Air Force has not released figures since. A Mitchell Institute researcher said nondeployed units were flying about once a month in 2026, though that is not an official figure.
  • Amphibious ship readiness fell to 41 percent in 2025, and in April 2026 the commandant of the Marine Corps said that 31 amphibious ships fall short of what the combatant commanders require (Military Times).
  • During the Iran war, the Pentagon's inspector general acknowledged a strategic shortfall in inventories and bottlenecks in resupply (The National), and on July 17, 2026, Iranian missiles penetrated air defenses at Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan, killing three U.S. service members (Al Jazeera).
  • The U.S. military has been an all-volunteer force since 1973 and has held large-scale combined exercises every year with South Korea, Japan, and NATO. In August 2026, however, the U.S.-South Korean Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise was cut from 11 days to five on presidential orders.

Deployment

Because adding forces in one region means drawing them down in another, where the U.S. military stations its forces, and in what numbers, shows which regions Washington actually puts first. Forces permanently stationed abroad are concentrated in the Indo-Pacific and Europe, and that distribution has barely moved over the past six quarters. The combat power committed to the Iran war comes from rotating carrier and amphibious groups that do not appear in the stationing statistics, so fleet positions have to be read alongside the permanent figures. The permanently stationed figure for South Korea excludes rotational forces and is therefore measured differently from the 28,500 floor Congress set on troop reductions.
