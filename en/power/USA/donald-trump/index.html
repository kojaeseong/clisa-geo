Worldview Assessment

Trump sees international politics as an arena in which wealthy allies and rivals alike have long taken advantage of the United States. He appears intent on shifting those costs onto others and on being judged by the deals he wins and the wars he ends. He treats tariffs, export licenses, alliance assets, and long-range strikes as bargaining chips; makes decisions within a small circle of aides and through his own public posts; and, his public vows notwithstanding, tends to back down once the costs of a threat rebound on U.S. financial markets.

How he sees the world
His view that wealthy allies such as Japan and Saudi Arabia are free-riding on American protection has barely changed in nearly 40 years, from a 1987 newspaper advertisement and a 1990 interview (D) to his 2024 remark that, on Taiwan, the United States is "no different than an insurance company" (C). His 2025 rebuke to Volodymyr Zelensky — "You don't have the cards" (C) — reflects a habit of treating wars and alliances as questions of leverage rather than of right and wrong. Basis: September 2, 1987 · March 1, 1990 · July 16, 2024 · February 28, 2025
What he wants
In his 2025 inaugural address, he said his proudest legacy would be that of a "peacemaker" (E) and that he would measure success "not only by the battles we win but also by the wars that we end." Yet one line he has held since he was a candidate — Iran cannot have a nuclear weapon (E) — he has enforced at real cost. He withdrew from the nuclear deal, struck Iran's nuclear facilities in 2025, and went to war in 2026. That line, in other words, outranks the goal of peace. Basis: May 8, 2018 · June 21, 2025 · February 28, 2026
What he thinks works
The doctrine of The Art of the Deal (D) — leverage as the greatest source of power, seeming desperate to make a deal as the worst mistake — appears to run through his walkout in Hanoi1 ("Sometimes you have to walk," A) and his decision to hold back arms sales to Taiwan. He has imposed tariffs on allies (2018), turned export licensing into a revenue deal in which Washington takes 25 percent of sales (2025), and, while relying on standoff tools such as targeted killings and airstrikes, cast his use of force as a means of ending wars (A). Basis: November 1, 1987 · March 8, 2018 · February 28, 2019 · January 3, 2020 · December 8, 2025 · May 16, 2026
People and process
In his first term, he pushed through tariffs at the cost of Gary Cohn's resignation, overruled hard-liners such as John Bolton to call off a strike on Iran, and, according to reports, chose the option of killing Qassem Soleimani, which the Pentagon had included largely to round out the menu. In his second term, Marco Rubio serves as both secretary of state and national security adviser, and the envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner handle the most important negotiations. Decisions often surface on Truth Social before any interagency process — as with allowing Nvidia's H200 chips to be sold to China and scaling back the Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise. The pattern suggests a system in which advisers supply options and the decisions rest with the president alone. Basis: March 8, 2018 · June 20, 2019 · January 3, 2020 · December 8, 2025 · August 16, 2026
Where his words and decisions diverge
His vows that a North Korean ability to strike the U.S. mainland "won't happen" (C), that Vladimir Putin would face "very severe consequences" (C), and, to investors, that "my policies will never change" (C) have all gone unmet, and his 48-hour deadline on Hormuz was pushed back three times. Conversely, the strike on Iran that, according to a statement read by his press secretary, he would decide on "within the next two weeks" came two days later. The intensity of a threat is a poor guide to whether he will carry it out: he retreats when the costs rebound on U.S. markets, but he is also capable of striking before his own deadline. Basis: June 12, 2018 · June 20, 2019 · April 9, 2025 · June 21, 2025 · August 15, 2025 · February 28, 2026

Relation to the country's strategic culture Assessment

From the Jacksonian2 tradition of American foreign policy, as Walter Russell Mead has defined it, Trump has inherited a distrust of elites, resentment of allies' free-riding, indifference to spreading democracy and building nations, and the instinct to use overwhelming force when threatened; he borrows William McKinley's 19th-century protectionism, "America First," and "peace through strength" as his language of justification. Yet the basic structure of his decisions, which treats alliance assets and threats themselves as objects of bargaining, looks closer to the deal-making culture of commercial real estate, and in that respect it departs from a Jacksonian tradition that prizes honor and trustworthiness. His habit of retreating when the costs of a threat rebound on the bond and stock markets suggests that the Hamiltonian3 commercial and financial tradition, which he keeps at arm's length, still sets the range of his options.

Inherited from the national tradition

He inherited a distrust of elites and of the foreign policy establishment. In a March 15, 2017, speech marking the 250th anniversary of Andrew Jackson's birth, Trump said that Jackson "confronted and defied an arrogant elite" and then asked, "Does that sound familiar to you?" — comparing himself directly to Jackson. In a 2016 column, Mead explained that Jacksonians reject grand projects such as nation building and global transformation; withdrawing from the Iran nuclear deal over the objections of the leaders of Britain, France, and Germany, and imposing steel tariffs at the cost of the resignation of Gary Cohn, director of the National Economic Council, were decisions that went against the foreign policy and economic establishment. Basis: March 8, 2018 · May 8, 2018 · March 15, 2017

It carries resentment of allies who do not pay their way and a self-definition as "America First." His criticism of free-riding, dating back to the 1987 advertisement (D), led to a March 2016 New York Times interview in which he described himself as "Not isolationist, but I am America First" (C, before taking office), and to an April 27, 2016, speech in which he declared that America First would be "the major and overriding theme" of his administration (E, before taking office). The National Security Strategy of December 4, 2025, made burden shifting an official principle, declaring that "the days of the United States propping up the entire world order like Atlas are over," and the scaling back of the combined exercises and the hold on arms sales to Taiwan have been explained in this language. Basis: September 2, 1987 · July 16, 2024 · August 16, 2026 · March 26, 2016·April 27, 2016·December 4, 2025

It is a view of war that, once a threat is perceived, uses overwhelming force and targets even the adversary's leadership. Mead held that Jacksonians place no limits on the use of American force in self-defense (2016), and Trump has made choices of the same kind again and again: the killing of Soleimani (2020), the B-2 strikes on nuclear facilities (2025), and the 2026 war in which Khamenei was killed. He has cast these not as escalation but as actions taken "to stop a war" (January 3, 2020, A). Basis: January 3, 2020 · June 21, 2025 · February 28, 2026

Roots outside the tradition

The deal-making culture of New York commercial real estate has roots distinct from the Jacksonian tradition. The Art of the Deal (1987, D) names leverage as the greatest strength and seeming desperate to make a deal as the worst mistake, and the walkout in Hanoi, his remark to Zelensky that he did not "have the cards," the hold on arms sales to Taiwan as a "negotiating chip," and the decision to attach a 25 percent cut of sales to export licenses all appear to fit that framework. The Jacksonian tradition as Mead describes it treats standing by allies as a matter of good faith, whereas Trump uses alliance assets in bargains with foreign leaders — which suggests that this other root is the stronger force. Tony Schwartz, however, has said that he ghostwrote the book, which limits how far it can be read as Trump's own thinking. Basis: November 1, 1987 · February 28, 2019 · February 28, 2025 · December 8, 2025 · May 16, 2026

The Hamiltonian school, which Mead classifies as the tradition centered on protecting commerce, is the opposing pole that Trump keeps at a distance yet ends up following. Because Hamilton himself proposed protective tariffs and bounties in his 1791 Report on Manufactures, tariffs as such can be justified in Hamiltonian language too; but the Hamiltonian tradition as Mead describes it prizes the stability of the international commercial and financial order. The pause in reciprocal tariffs a week after turmoil in the Treasury market (April 9, 2025) and the resumption of Nvidia's sales to China (December 8, 2025) appear to be cases in which the interests of financial markets and big business set his options. Basis: April 9, 2025 · April 4, 2025 · December 8, 2025

How history is used selectively

He draws on the protectionist past selectively, taking only the tariffs. In his inaugural address on January 20, 2025, Trump said McKinley "made our country very rich through tariffs and through talent"; the same day, Executive Order 14172 restored the name Mount McKinley to Denali; and he has singled out 1870 to 1913 as the years when America was richest, saying, "That's when we were a tariff country." Yet in his last speech, on September 5, 1901, McKinley declared that "the period of exclusiveness is past," championing reciprocal trade treaties and calling retaliatory measures out of keeping with the spirit of the times. Of Jackson, too, Trump said in his 2017 speech that he had protected workers with tariffs, but the Tariff of 1832, enacted during Jackson's presidency, lowered the rates set in 1828. Basis: March 8, 2018 · April 9, 2025 · January 20, 2025 · March 15, 2017

He borrows slogans while dropping the conditions that originally came with them. "Peace through strength" was Ronald Reagan's slogan, but Reagan, at the signing of the INF Treaty in 1987, invoked "trust, but verify," stressing rigorous verification and the protection of allies' interests, and in 1988 he criticized protectionism, arguing that the Smoot-Hawley tariff had helped bring on the Great Depression. Trump gave up the combined exercises first, with no verification timetable (2018), and his press secretary went so far as to say that Trump had coined the slogan (2025). "America First," likewise, was the name of a noninterventionist group founded in 1940 and dissolved right after Pearl Harbor; Trump took only the name, insisting he was "not isolationist," and in practice used force against Iran. Basis: June 12, 2018 · February 28, 2019 · February 28, 2026 · June 23, 2025

Checked against classics and scholarship

Walter Russell Mead · Special Providence: American Foreign Policy and How It Changed the World (Knopf, 2001); "The Jacksonian Revolt: American Populism and the Liberal Order" (Foreign Affairs, March/April 2017) Conditional

Interpretation
Mead explained U.S. foreign policy as a contest among four traditions: the Hamiltonian, centered on protecting commerce; the Wilsonian, on moral principle; the Jeffersonian, on preserving democracy at home; and the Jacksonian, on popular values and military strength. In his account, the Jacksonian tradition distrusts elites and globalism and, absent a threat, prefers to live and let live, but once attacked it sets no limits on the use of force, and it is generally unenthusiastic about free trade. In his writings of 2016 and 2017, he interpreted Trump's election as this tradition rising up against the liberal international order.
Comparison
Distrust of elites (the 2017 Jackson speech), demands that allies bear costs (1987–2026), indifference to nation building and spreading democracy, skepticism of free trade (the 2018 and 2025 tariffs), and heavy strikes on Iran (2020, 2025, 2026) fit the Jacksonian account well.
Where it falls short
Mead held that Jacksonians support standing by allies, yet Trump gave away the combined exercises and arms sales to Taiwan as bargaining chips (2018, 2026), backed off the "very severe consequences" he had promised Putin (August 15, 2025), and allowed advanced chips to be sold to a rival in return for a cut of the proceeds (December 8, 2025). And unlike Mead's account of Jacksonians who came to feel betrayed in Iraq, the 2026 Iran war has now lasted more than seven months.

Russell Weigley (including Antulio Echevarria's reinterpretation) · The American Way of War: A History of United States Military Strategy and Policy (Macmillan, 1973); Antulio J. Echevarria II, Toward an American Way of War (Strategic Studies Institute, U.S. Army War College, 2004) Conflicts

Interpretation
Weigley argued that the American way of war, whether through a strategy of attrition or of annihilation4, has pursued overwhelming military victory. Building on this, Echevarria contended that the United States has less a way of war than a "way of battle," and faulted it for neglecting the task of turning military victory into political results (2004, pp. 1, 6).
Comparison
Echevarria's revision partly fits. The 2025 strikes on nuclear facilities and the 2026 killing of Iran's leadership were tactically overwhelming, but Trump himself has said that peace with Iran is not possible (September 28, 2026, C), and the Hormuz blockade and the collapse of the ceasefire followed, so the step of converting victory into a political settlement appears to be missing. Mead, too, observed in a July 2026 column that Trump "can also claim to have ended one war at least three times."
Where it falls short
The core of Weigley's model, the strategy of annihilation — destroying enemy forces through mass mobilization and ground war — does not match Trump's decisions. He has avoided seizing territory with ground troops and chosen only standoff strikes, called off a strike just before execution (June 20, 2019), and postponed the Hormuz deadline three times. Because force has served as a means of pressure in negotiations rather than as a means of decisive battle, his record conflicts with this model.

Colin Dueck · Age of Iron: On Conservative Nationalism (Oxford University Press, 2019) Fits

Interpretation
Dueck regards conservative nationalism as the oldest democratic tradition in American foreign relations and traces three strands within the Republican Party that took shape in opposition to Wilson's liberal internationalism: Henry Cabot Lodge's conservative internationalism, William Borah's hard-line unilateralism, and Robert La Follette's noninterventionism. Trump, in his view, did not create this current but exploited it against a backdrop of 21st-century diplomatic, economic, and military frustrations, and his course amounts less to dismantling the postwar order than to a "directional" shift that reorients costly commitments.
Comparison
Trump has chosen to contest the costs of the alliance system rather than formally withdraw from it. He approved an $11.1 billion arms sale to Taiwan in December 2025, pressed South Korea on exercise levels and cost-sharing without cutting U.S. troops there, and the National Security Strategy (December 4, 2025) made burden shifting, not withdrawal, its principle. Conflict among strands within the party, such as the free-trade Republicans' backlash against the 2018 steel tariffs, also fits Dueck's framework.
Where it falls short
Dueck's framework explains partisan and intellectual currents, but decisions that use alliance assets in personal bargains with foreign leaders (the hold on the Taiwan package on May 16, 2026, and the UFS cutback on August 16, 2026) are better explained by Trump's own style of deal-making. The prediction that this current will outlast Trump cannot yet be tested.

Strategic culture should be treated not as the cause of Trump's decisions but as a condition that sets the range of his options and his language of justification. Reagan, who also ran under the banner of "peace through strength," imposed 100 percent tariffs on Japanese computers, color televisions, and power tools in 1987 over violations of the semiconductor agreement, even as he defended free trade and concluded arms control treaties built on verification; George W. Bush, carried by Jacksonian anger after 9/11, set the Wilsonian goal of "ending tyranny" in 2005 and withdrew his own 2002 steel tariffs in 2003. Since the same tradition has produced such different decisions, Trump's next move should not be predicted from an essentialist claim that America is Jacksonian by nature.

Domestic coalition and room to maneuver Assessment

Trump holds power through a coalition anchored by MAGA5 supporters, who dominate Republican primaries, and taking in evangelicals, congressional Republicans, donors from business, Wall Street, and the tech industry, and farm regions. As of September 2026, 70 to 80 percent of Republicans still back him, and no force within the party appears able to reverse his decisions. The coalition is split, however, along two lines — opposition to new wars versus a hard line on Iran and support for Israel, and tariffs versus their costs to markets and farmers — and he appears to have managed these splits by deciding in favor of one side and then handling the other side's backlash with demands for loyalty and with compensation. In the near term, the November 3 midterms appear to be the condition that most narrows his latitude.

Room to maneuver

On Iran, his room both to concede and to escalate appears narrow until the November 3 midterms. A concession that looked like surrender would provoke evangelicals and hawks, while escalation such as sending in ground troops would cost him more noninterventionists and price-sensitive voters, so a status quo of neither war nor peace looks like the easiest course; after the election, his room both for the expanded bombing he has hinted at and for a deal is likely to widen. On China, chips, and tariffs, business and tech donors and farm regions want a deal with Beijing, and the pushback from China hawks remains at the legislative stage, so his room for concessions appears relatively wide. On Russia and Ukraine, the Russia sanctions law has written the demands of congressional hawks into statute and narrowed his room for concessions to Moscow, but the national-interest waiver leaves scope for enforcing it selectively, country by country. The Korean Peninsula and Taiwan draw little attention within his base and appear to be where his personal discretion is greatest; if the Democrats take the House, checks through appropriations and hearings are likely to grow on all of these issues.

Words before and after a change of office

His rhetoric has moved from promises of nonintervention before taking office toward the use of force in office, and the degree varies by issue. On war and peace, the gap is wide. In his 2024 victory speech, he said, "I'm not going to start a war. I'm going to stop wars." and in his 2025 inaugural address he said he would measure success by "the wars we never get into"; yet in his second term he ordered strikes on Iran's nuclear facilities (June 2025) and a war on Iran that has run for more than seven months (since February 2026), and in March 2025 he walked back his campaign promise to end the Ukraine war within 24 hours, saying he had been "a little bit sarcastic." By contrast, his criticism of allies' free-riding and his tariffs have changed little, from the 1987 advertisement through the 2016 and 2024 campaigns to his decisions in office, and he has consistently enforced by force the line he drew as a candidate in 2016 that Iran cannot have a nuclear weapon.

Earlier leaders who faced the same structure

Richard Nixon, having built an anticommunist record, announced on July 15, 1971, that he would visit China and went to Beijing in February 1972; conservatives including William F. Buckley Jr. met in December 1971 and agreed to field a candidate against him, and Representative John Ashbrook won 9.8 percent in the 1972 New Hampshire primary. Ronald Reagan signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty with the Soviet Union in December 1987 and was denounced by Howard Phillips and others as a "useful idiot for Soviet propaganda."

Checked against theory

Robert Putnam · Diplomacy and Domestic Politics: The Logic of Two-Level Games (International Organization 42, no. 3, 1988, pp. 427–460) Conditional

Interpretation
Putnam argued that negotiators bargain simultaneously with the other side (Level I) and with the domestic constituencies that must ratify a deal (Level II), and that when the range of agreements acceptable at home (the win-set6) is small, a negotiator's leverage can actually grow, on the grounds that a given deal could never be accepted at home (p. 440). In domestic side payments, he held, distribution to the existing coalition matters more than the total amount (p. 450), and negotiators have preferences of their own, including a desire to shift the domestic balance of power (p. 457).
Comparison
The logic of distributing side payments fits well: he gave $12 billion to farmers hurt by the tariffs, signed the Epstein files law to defuse the issue, and isolated defectors such as Carlson and Greene. But rather than invoking opposition within his own base as a bargaining chip — the claim that his hands are tied — Trump has decided ahead of his domestic base and managed the fallout afterward, going to war despite the noninterventionists' objections, for instance. If anything, analysts argue that Iran is holding out by targeting America's own Level II constraint, the midterms, which suggests that a small win-set is working as a weakness for Washington.

Bruce Bueno de Mesquita, Alastair Smith, Randolph Siverson, and James Morrow · The Logic of Political Survival (MIT Press, 2003) Conditional

Interpretation
They argued that a leader's survival depends on the loyalty of the winning coalition; in democracies, where the winning coalition is large, leaders must deliver public goods (policy results) rather than private rewards to survive, while the smaller the winning coalition, the more rewards are concentrated privately on a few.
Comparison
The United States is a system with a large winning coalition, and the fall in his approval rating to 32 percent as gasoline prices rose with the prolonged Iran war, along with the Cook Political Report's projection that the Democrats will take the House, fits the argument that failures to deliver public goods come back as survival costs. Yet Trump is also handing targeted rewards to the subcoalitions that control primaries and donations — aid to farmers, permission for Nvidia's sales, his signature on the Epstein law — which suggests a structure in which a small intraparty selectorate7 is nested inside a large winning coalition.

How he decides: hypotheses and forecasts

Patterns drawn from the decision record. Each hypothesis carries a forecast that the next decision can confirm or refute.

1. When he uses force, Trump chooses standoff means — airstrikes, targeted killings, naval blockades — and avoids seizing territory with ground troops. He lets his deadlines slip repeatedly, then acts without warning once negotiations stall.

Evidence and counterexamples
Supporting cases include January 3, 2020 (a targeted killing); June 21, 2025 (a strike earlier than his "two weeks"); February 28, 2026 (war launched while talks were under way); and the Hormuz deadline postponed three times in March and April 2026. Against it: the strike he called off at the last minute on June 20, 2019, and the fact that the 2026 Iran war became a campaign of more than seven months rather than a single blow.
What would confirm or refute it
If, after the November 3 midterms, the standoff with Iran produces only intensified strikes or blockades, the hypothesis holds; if U.S. ground combat units are sent to seize Iranian territory, such as Kharg Island, it fails. If new deadlines are repeatedly enforced on schedule without postponement, the "delay, then surprise" element fails.

2. Trump treats alliance assets — joint exercises, arms sales to Taiwan, troop levels — as chips in personal bargains with foreign leaders, and gives them up before the other side makes verifiable concessions.

Evidence and counterexamples
Supporting cases include June 12, 2018 (exercises suspended on the day of the summit); May 16, 2026 (a Taiwan package held back, with Trump saying it depended on Beijing); and August 16, 2026 (Ulchi Freedom Shield scaled back, citing his relationship with Kim Jong Un). Against it: at the Hanoi summit on February 28, 2019, he refused to grant sanctions relief, and in December 2025 he approved an $11.1 billion arms sale to Taiwan.
What would confirm or refute it
If further cuts to joint exercises, troop reductions in Korea, or a longer hold on the Taiwan package precede a push for talks with Kim or a trade deal with Xi, the hypothesis holds. If Washington notifies Congress of the $14 billion package without concrete concessions from Beijing, or holds the spring 2027 exercises at full scale, it fails.

3. Trump's economic threats are postponed or withdrawn when the costs fall on U.S. financial markets or oil prices, and carried out when the costs fall on allies or third countries.

Evidence and counterexamples
Supporting cases include April 9, 2025 (reciprocal tariffs paused after turmoil in the Treasury market); August 15, 2025 (threatened sanctions on Russia dropped); March 8, 2018 (steel tariffs imposed even on allies); August 2025 (secondary tariffs over Russian oil imposed on India alone, with no such step against China, the largest buyer); and August 16, 2026 (exercises scaled back at allies' expense). Against it: on October 22, 2025, he sanctioned Russia's oil majors despite the risk to oil prices, and on April 9, 2025, he raised tariffs on China rather than pausing them.
What would confirm or refute it
If the tariffs on buyers of Russian energy under the Russia sanctions law (signed September 18, 2026; deadline October 18) are applied to India, Turkey, and others while China receives a national-interest waiver, the hypothesis holds. If the tariffs are actually imposed on Chinese imports, it fails.

Time horizon and power base

Time horizon · His term ends on January 20, 2029, and the 22nd Amendment bars him from seeking a third. The midterm elections on November 3, 2026, are the turning point that will decide how hard the House and Senate can push back, and polls are reported to favor the Democrats. Born on June 14, 1946, he is 80 as of October 2026; first in the line of succession is Vice President JD Vance.

Power base · His power rests on the Republican base and on narrow Republican majorities in both chambers of Congress, and foreign and security decisions are concentrated in a small circle of aides. The core of that circle consists of Susie Wiles, the White House chief of staff; Marco Rubio, secretary of state and national security adviser (formally appointed to the second post in September 2026); Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth; Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent; Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick; and Jamieson Greer, the U.S. trade representative. Negotiations over Iran, Russia, and Ukraine are handled directly by the special envoy Steve Witkoff and by Jared Kushner, Trump's son-in-law and peace envoy.

Possible conflicts with personal interest · According to reports, the timing of negotiations with Iran hinges on the November midterms and on oil prices, and other reports say renewed strikes are under consideration for after the election. Kushner, the envoy handling Iran and the Middle East, runs Affinity Partners, a private equity firm that has taken money from Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, Qatar, and the UAE; in April 2026, Representative Jamie Raskin, the ranking Democrat on the House Judiciary Committee, formally raised the conflict of interest in a letter.

Who decides and who can veto, issue by issue

IssueDecidesVeto players
The Korean PeninsulaWhether to hold a U.S.-North Korea summit, how large the combined exercises should be, and questions of extended deterrence9 (including nuclear use) are the president's decisions alone, as the scaling back of Ulchi Freedom Shield (UFS) in August 2026 showed. The president also leads on lifting unilateral U.S. sanctions on North Korea, but sanctions grounded in statute, such as the North Korea Sanctions and Policy Enhancement Act of 2016, carry requirements to certify to Congress. Carrying out any reduction of U.S. Forces Korea (USFK) is up to the president and the secretary of defense.Through the fiscal 2026 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA), Congress barred the use of that act's funds to reduce troop levels below 28,500; the restriction lifts only 60 days after the secretary of defense submits a national-interest certification. The House version of the fiscal 2027 bill (passed in July 2026, 216–212) extends the restriction to other available funds and also bars spending to complete the transfer of wartime operational control10 (OPCON), but the Senate version has stalled since a failed cloture vote in July. Lifting UN sanctions on North Korea requires a Security Council resolution, so no U.S. president can lift them alone.
The Taiwan StraitThe use of force and military intervention in a Taiwan contingency are decisions for the president as commander in chief; the Taiwan Relations Act (1979) imposes no obligation to defend the island, which leaves intervention to presidential discretion. The president and the State Department (Rubio) decide the timing and size of arms sales, and a $14 billion package is currently on hold by the president's choice. In May 2026, Trump was reported to have said that he does not tell Xi Jinping whether the United States would defend Taiwan.Under Section 36 of the Arms Export Control Act (AECA), major sales must be notified to Congress, which can block a sale by joint resolution, but overriding a presidential veto takes two-thirds of both chambers. Congress, by contrast, has virtually no legal means of forcing a sale the president has put on hold to go ahead, so its response to the current delay has been limited to expressions of concern from members of both parties. If a military intervention dragged on, the 60-day limit under the War Powers Resolution and Congress's power of the purse would become constraints.
The Russia-Ukraine WarThe president decides the terms of a ceasefire and the negotiating position on territory, and the envoys Witkoff and Kushner carry them between Moscow and Kyiv (most recently on September 5–6, 2026). Under Supreme Court precedent (Zivotofsky v. Kerry, 2015), recognizing Russian sovereignty over occupied territory falls within the president's recognition power. The president can also ease Russia sanctions that rest on executive orders.Section 216 of the Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act (CAATSA) of 2017 subjects the lifting or easing of certain Russia sanctions to congressional review, and the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, signed on September 18, 2026, provides for tariffs of up to 100 percent on countries that import Russian energy, among other measures, while giving the president the power to grant waivers by certifying the national interest. Aid to Ukraine depends on congressional appropriations; the fiscal 2026 NDAA authorized $400 million a year for the Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative (USAI) and requires a report within 48 hours of any suspension of intelligence support. A security guarantee in treaty form would need the consent of two-thirds of the Senate.
Iran and HormuzThe president alone has decided whether to launch strikes, impose a naval blockade, or strike again; negotiations are handled by the envoys Witkoff and Kushner, with Qatar and Pakistan mediating. The ceasefire has wobbled since the Islamabad memorandum of understanding of June 17, and in late September Trump rejected an Iranian offer to reopen the strait within seven days. Escorting ships through the Strait of Hormuz is likewise a military operation under the president's command.Congress can direct the withdrawal of forces through a war powers resolution, but the measure both chambers passed in June was a concurrent resolution, which needs no presidential signature and was therefore not binding, and a Senate vote on September 24 failed by a narrow margin. A binding joint resolution would also need two-thirds of both chambers to override a veto. Under the Iran Nuclear Agreement Review Act of 2015, any nuclear deal that includes sanctions relief must be submitted to Congress for a review period, and the 2026 Graham sanctions law extended the Iran Sanctions Act by five years.
AI and ChipsExport controls are administered by the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security under the Export Control Reform Act of 2018, but the president sets their direction himself, as when he allowed H200 sales (an instruction issued in December 2025). On tariffs, after the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 on February 20, 2026, that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) confers no power to impose them, the approach changed: at the president's direction, the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative began imposing Section 301 tariffs (10 percent and 12.5 percent) on 60 countries on July 24. Bessent and Greer handle the response on rare earths and the tariff negotiations.The courts are the main check on tariff authority: on May 7, the Court of International Trade ruled the Section 122 tariffs unlawful as well (the ruling is stayed pending appeal), and litigation over the Section 301 tariffs is under way. In Congress, the AI OVERWATCH Act, which would let Congress block chip export licenses by joint resolution, cleared the House Foreign Affairs Committee 42–2 and was folded into the Senate version of the NDAA, but it has not become law. Interagency review can also slow implementation, as when an additional State Department security review delayed H200 shipments.

Decision-making environment and firsthand accounts

Major foreign and security decisions are made by the president himself and are often announced first in Truth Social posts (allowing H200 sales in December 2025, scaling back UFS in August 2026). Marco Rubio is both secretary of state and national security adviser (formally appointed to the second post in September 2026), and the envoys Witkoff and Kushner handle negotiations with Iran and Russia, so decisions appear to move through channels that report directly to the president rather than through regular interagency procedures. In the Signal chat leaked in March 2025, Vice President Vance's dissent was settled among the aides, and the president's decision was relayed through Stephen Miller as a "green light." In the first term, the president was reported to have picked an extreme option the Pentagon had included largely to round out the menu (killing Soleimani) after watching television coverage (a New York Times report, cited secondhand).

Key terms

  1. Hanoi summit · The second U.S.-North Korea summit, held in Hanoi, Vietnam, in February 2019. It ended without an agreement. ↩
  2. Jacksonian tradition · One of the four traditions of American foreign policy identified by Walter Russell Mead: a populist nationalist tradition that distrusts elites and international institutions and answers attack with overwhelming force, but is wary of intervention in distant places. ↩
  3. Hamiltonian tradition · In the same scheme, the tradition that pursues the national interest through trade, finance, and the international economic order. ↩
  4. Strategy of annihilation · A strategy that seeks to end a war by destroying the enemy's armed forces in decisive battle. Russell Weigley saw it as the American way of war. ↩
  5. MAGA · Trump's slogan "Make America Great Again," and the term for the supporters who rally behind it. ↩
  6. Win-set · In Robert Putnam's two-level game theory, the range of international agreements that domestic constituencies will accept. The narrower the range, the less room a leader has to make concessions. ↩
  7. Selectorate · In the theory developed by Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and others, the set of people who can influence the choice of a leader. The core within it that actually keeps the leader in power is called the winning coalition. ↩
  8. National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) · The law Congress passes every year to set authorities and policy for the defense budget. ↩
  9. Extended deterrence · The U.S. commitment to provide allies with deterrence backed by its military power, including nuclear weapons. ↩
  10. Wartime operational control (OPCON) · The authority to command South Korean forces in wartime. It is held by the commander of the ROK-U.S. Combined Forces Command, a U.S. four-star general. ↩
