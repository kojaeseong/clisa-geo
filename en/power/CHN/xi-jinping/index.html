Worldview Assessment

For Xi, international politics is an arena of struggle in which containment and encirclement by a U.S.-led West are a constant, and reunification with Taiwan and the Party's leadership are tasks from which he cannot retreat, however long they take. He prefers building pressure a little at a time, at each opportunity, to a single decisive move; turns other countries' dependence on China into leverage; and, in choosing people, checks loyalty before competence.

How he sees the world
His tendency to treat outside criticism as interference in China's internal affairs was evident before he reached the top (remarks in Mexico, 2009, C); once in power, he named the all-round containment by "Western countries led by the United States" directly (speech, March 2023, E). Language casting international relations as an arena of "struggle" rather than cooperation was also presented as an achievement in the 2022 Party Congress report. Basis: February 11, 2009 · October 16, 2022 · March 6, 2023
What he wants
He has defined Taiwan as a problem that "cannot be passed down from generation to generation" (2019, E) but has set no public deadline. At his 2023 meeting with Biden, he reportedly dismissed U.S. military predictions that he would move on Taiwan in 2025 or 2027 and said he preferred a peaceful path (B, U.S. account). Basis: January 2, 2019 · November 15, 2023
What he thinks works
As a local official in 1990, he wrote of the gradualism of "dripping water wearing through stone" (D); in 2020, he called for "assassin's mace1" technologies and a capacity to counter outside pressure, turning other countries' dependence on China into a weapon (E). The mineral export controls that began three years later, and their repeated time-limited suspensions, appear to put both ideas directly into practice. Basis: March 1990 · April 10, 2020 · July 3, 2023 · November 7, 2025
People and process
The loyalty standard he set for Party members in a 2005 column — readiness to risk everything in times of crisis — was reused as the standard for officials in the 2022 Party Congress report (D→E), and the 2023–2026 purges of the military leadership enforced it. Basis: April 27, 2005 · October 16, 2022 · January 24, 2026
Where his words and decisions diverge
He crossed his own 2015 pledge that China did not intend to militarize the South China Sea, and he lifted the zero-COVID policy he had vowed to pursue "unwaveringly" six days after nationwide protests. His external pledges appear to bind him only loosely; under domestic pressure that touches regime stability, he changes course quickly, but he does not appear to say anything that concedes a retreat. Basis: May 2, 2018 · December 7, 2022

Relation to the country's strategic culture Assessment

Xi borrows from China's traditional strategic culture the idea of unification (da yitong2, "grand unification") as the highest form of legitimacy, the practice of reading shi3 (勢, "the momentum or propensity of things") and building pressure gradually, and the memory of humiliation since 1840; but the skeleton of his decisions appears closer to the Leninist4 organizational culture of Party leadership and loyalty vetting. The gap between his rhetoric of a "peace-loving civilization" and his actual coercive conduct resembles the split between word and deed that Johnston found in the Ming dynasty. Because he has not yet resorted to large-scale force, however, the reading that traditional culture drives him toward offensive war remains unconfirmed.

Inherited from the national tradition

Xi has inherited the concept of da yitong ("grand unification"), which treats unification as the highest form of legitimacy. At a June 2, 2023, symposium on cultural inheritance and development, he said that the remarkable unity of Chinese civilization means that "national unification is forever the core of China's core interests," and the full text of the same speech published in Qiushi uses the phrase "the tradition of grand unification." His framing of Taiwan's reunification as a task that cannot be put off from generation to generation, and the central government's direct enactment of the Hong Kong national security law, were justified in this language. Basis: January 2, 2019 · June 30, 2020 · May 14, 2026 · June 2, 2023

The second inheritance is the practice of reading shi (勢) and accumulating pressure bit by bit rather than seeking a decisive battle. The strategic style that Kissinger explained through weiqi (the board game known in the West as Go) and the concept of shi appears to connect with Xi's 1990 gradualism of "dripping water wearing through stone," with his view of "profound changes unseen in a century" (bainian weiyou zhi da bianju), first officially confirmed on December 28, 2017, and with his January 11, 2021, judgment that "time and momentum are on our side" (shi yu shi zai women yibian). The step-by-step militarization of the South China Sea's artificial islands and the repeated time-limited suspensions of mineral export controls appear to follow the same pattern. This may, however, be less a distinctively Chinese practice than a delaying strategy commonly chosen by rising powers. Basis: March 1990 · May 2, 2018 · July 3, 2023 · November 7, 2025 · January 11, 2021

The third is the memory that takes the humiliation following the Opium War of 1840 as the starting point of the national story. On November 29, 2012, visiting "The Road to Rejuvenation" exhibition — where he first set out the "Chinese Dream" — Xi called national rejuvenation "the greatest dream of the Chinese people since the advent of modern times." In his July 1, 2021, speech on the Party's centenary, he said that after the Opium War "the country endured intense humiliation, the people were subjected to great pain, and the Chinese civilization was plunged into darkness," then warned that foreign forces attempting to bully China would pay a heavy price. His remarks treating outside criticism as interference also appear to fit within the frame of this memory, though Ford points out that the humiliation narrative draws its force less from actual injury than from a former empire's lofty self-image. Basis: February 11, 2009 · March 6, 2023 · November 29, 2012·July 1, 2021

Roots outside the tradition

The Leninist organizational culture in which the Party leads everything and commands the gun has roots distinct from the culture of the traditional dynasties. At an all-army political work conference held in late October 2014 at the site of the 1929 Gutian Conference, Xi called the Party's absolute leadership over the military "the soul of a strong military," and the 19th Party Congress in 2017 wrote Mao Zedong's dictum that "the Party leads everything" into the Party Constitution. Torigian explains that in the political culture of the revolutionary elite that raised Xi, the Party was an object of loyalty that came before personal suffering. The line running from the loyalty standard in his 2005 column to the 2022 standard for officials and on to the 2026 purge of the military leadership appears closer to this root. Basis: April 27, 2005 · October 16, 2022 · January 24, 2026 · July 1, 2021

The Marxist language of struggle and contradiction also has roots distinct from tradition. In his 2022 Party Congress report, Xi held up as an achievement the "fighting spirit" shown against outside containment and blockade, and in his June 2, 2023, speech he cast traditional culture as something to be integrated with the basic tenets of Marxism (the so-called second integration). The pattern appears to be not tradition guiding ideology but ideology selecting from tradition. Basis: October 16, 2022 · March 6, 2023

How history is used selectively

This is the practice of selecting only the peaceable passages from the classics to build a narrative of a "peace-loving civilization." In a May 15, 2014, speech, Xi quoted the classical injunction to value harmony and the line that a great power fond of war will perish, and said that the blood of the Chinese nation carries "no gene for invading others or seeking world hegemony" (meiyou qinlüe taren, chengba shijie de jiyin); in 2023 he fixed "peacefulness" as one of the five defining features of Chinese civilization. Since the militarization of the South China Sea and live-fire exercises around Taiwan continued over the same period, this rhetoric appears to be an instance of the selective citation of ancient strategic thought that Ghiselli identifies. Basis: September 25, 2015 · May 2, 2018 · August 4, 2022 · May 15, 2014

The very assessment of tradition has been reversed to suit the Party's needs. Under Mao Zedong, the Party attacked Confucius in the 1973–1976 "Criticize Lin5, Criticize Confucius" campaign, whereas Xi, at the Confucius Institute in Qufu on November 26, 2013, said that research on Confucian thought should stand on historical materialism, "make the past serve the present" (gu wei jin yong), and take only its essence. That the same Party has treated the same tradition in opposite ways supports the reading that tradition does less to produce decisions than to supply the Party with parts it can select for justification. Basis: November 26, 2013 · June 2, 2023

Checked against classics and scholarship

Henry Kissinger · On China (Penguin Press, 2011; Korean edition, Minumsa, 2012) Conditional

Interpretation
Comparing Chinese strategy to weiqi (Go), Kissinger argued that rather than seeking decisive victory as in chess, it patiently accumulates relative advantage, pursues strategic encirclement, and reads and exploits shi (勢), the flow of the strategic configuration — a tradition he saw continuing into modern leaders (chapter 1, "The Singularity of China," pp. 5–32). He also examined the worldview of the tributary order, in which China saw itself as the center of civilization and understood foreign relations as hierarchical rather than equal, and how that sense of superiority contributed to its decline in the 19th century (chapters 2–3).
Comparison
The step-by-step militarization of the South China Sea (2015–2018), the repeated cycle of mineral export controls and time-limited suspensions (2023–2026), exercises around Taiwan that end in about two days after each trigger, and the assessment that "time and momentum are on our side" fit the weiqi-and-shi reading well. But the book appeared in 2011, before Xi took power, and was written on the premise of Deng Xiaoping–style restraint and U.S.-China "co-evolution."
Where it falls short
The "no limits" Sino-Russian joint statement of February 4, 2022, was a choice to throw in with one camp rather than the weiqi-style flexibility of keeping many moves open, and decisions that cut operational capability, such as the 2026 purge of the military leadership, cannot be explained by a theory of exploiting shi. The "Pacific Community"-style cooperation Kissinger hoped for in his epilogue has also given way to strategic competition.

Alastair Iain Johnston · Cultural Realism: Strategic Culture and Grand Strategy in Chinese History (Princeton, 1995) Conditional

Interpretation
Examining the Ming dynasty's (1368–1644) strategy toward the Mongols and the Seven Military Classics through cognitive mapping and symbolic analysis, Johnston argued that Confucian-Mencian discourse emphasizing virtuous rule and defense remained largely at the level of idealized symbols, while actual decisions followed a hard-line "parabellum6" realpolitik that saw conflict as zero-sum and favored offensive force when the balance of power was favorable. He concluded that this realpolitik was rooted more in ideas than in the structure of the international system.
Comparison
The split between peaceful rhetoric and coercive conduct fits well: missile deployments (2018) followed the 2015 statement that China did not intend to militarize, and encirclement exercises around Taiwan were repeated alongside rhetoric about having "no gene for invasion" (2014) and "peacefulness" (2023). But the parabellum model's central prediction — offensive use of force when the balance favors China — has not materialized as a major war under Xi, whose instruments have stayed below the threshold of war: the coast guard, military exercises, and export controls.
Where it falls short
Even with China's military stronger than at any time in its history, Xi reportedly expressed a preference for a peaceful path and an open-ended timeline for reunification with Taiwan (November 15, 2023, B), and in negotiations with Washington he suspended rare-earth controls for a fixed period (November 7, 2025). Decisions in which he gave way to domestic pressure, such as the lifting of zero-COVID in December 2022, are also better explained by calculations of regime stability than by strategic culture. Transposing conclusions drawn from the Ming dynasty directly onto an individual 21st-century leader is also a stretch.

Yuan-kang Wang · Harmony and War: Confucian Culture and Chinese Power Politics (Columbia, 2011) Conditional

Interpretation
Drawing on cases from the Song and Ming dynasties, Wang argued that whether China used force depended not on Confucian culture but on relative power, and that Confucianism served less as a cause of decisions than as a language of justification — a structural-realist answer to Johnston's cultural explanation.
Comparison
The pattern of growing assertiveness in the South China Sea and in export controls as the power gap with the United States narrows, and of retreating into time-limited suspensions when Washington pushes back hard, appears to be adjustment according to power.
Where it falls short
Structural factors alone cannot explain a legitimacy project such as reunification with Taiwan, which is hard to account for by the balance of power, or domestic decisions that sap national strength, such as the purge of the military leadership. Participants in the review roundtable also raised counterexamples of restraint despite superior power, such as the Ming's relations with Joseon Korea.

Strategic culture should be treated not as the cause of Xi's decisions but as a condition that sets the range of his options and the language in which he justifies them. Within the same tradition, Mao Zedong attacked Confucius and chose continuous revolution, while Deng Xiaoping chose restraint and opening; Xi's next decision should therefore not be predicted from an essentialist view that "this is simply how China is."

Domestic coalition and room to maneuver Assessment

Xi holds power not through an electoral coalition but with the backing of a narrow selectorate7: the Party's central elite, a purged People's Liberation Army, and the discipline and political-legal8 apparatus. Since the removal of term limits in 2018 and the makeup of the Standing Committee chosen in 2022, that elite's ability to coordinate collective sanctions against him appears to have weakened sharply. He therefore has wide latitude to make concessions on foreign issues, but on issues such as Taiwan, where the Party's legitimacy and nationalist opinion are at stake, the path to a verbal retreat is closed; on domestic issues, he appears to change course when backlash from urban residents and economic interests grows, while never admitting a retreat.

Coalition

The Party's central elite (Politburo Standing Committee, Politburo, Central Committee)Party leadership and regime stability, the security of their own positions, and governing legitimacy sustained through growth.
A March 11, 2018, constitutional amendment removed the term limit on the presidency, and none of the seven Standing Committee members chosen at the First Plenum of the 20th Central Committee on October 23, 2022 (Xi Jinping, Li Qiang, Zhao Leji, Wang Huning, Cai Qi, Ding Xuexiang, Li Xi) has been marked as a successor. Formally, this is the body that selects Xi, but its capacity to coordinate collective opposition appears to have declined.
The People's Liberation Army (the Central Military Commission9 and theater commands)Budgets and modernization, a firm stance on Taiwan, and job security for the top brass.
Both CMC vice chairmen chosen in 2022 (Zhang Youxia and He Weidong) have been purged (October 17, 2025, and January 24, 2026), and the CMC is reported to consist of only Xi and Zhang Shengmin. The military's power to make collective demands or resist policy has weakened, but the vacuum in operational command appears, conversely, to constrain the feasibility of using force.
The discipline, political-legal, and state security apparatus (Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, Central Political and Legal Affairs Commission, Ministry of State Security)A line that puts "political security" first, and the preservation and expansion of their oversight and policing powers.
Standing Committee member Li Xi heads the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, and Chen Wenqing, a former minister of state security, has led the Central Political and Legal Affairs Commission since October 28, 2022. These bodies look less like independent constituencies than like instruments Xi uses to control the elite and the military, and their effect appears to be to give security logic more weight in policymaking.
Nationalist opinion and urban residents (online opinion, university students, urban homeowning households)Abroad, a hard line toward Taiwan and the United States; at home, a return to normal life and stable home values.
They have no way to replace leaders through elections, but they can send signals that shake the regime's legitimacy, as with the disappointment over Pelosi's visit in August 2022 and the zero-COVID protests that November. Because the authorities can stoke or cool this opinion through censorship and propaganda, the size of the constraint appears to be something they can adjust themselves.

Decisions that strained the coalition

November 2022After a November 24, 2022, apartment fire in Urumqi killed 10 people, anger spread over claims that lockdown measures had blocked their escape, and protests broke out in Shanghai, Beijing (at Tsinghua University), Nanjing, Wuhan, Urumqi, and elsewhere, with some protesters chanting for Xi to step down. A month earlier, the 20th Party Congress report had declared that China had "tenaciously pursued a dynamic zero-Covid policy."
How it was handled · Police dispersed protesters and related posts were deleted, while on December 7, 2022, the "10 new measures" from the State Council's Joint Prevention and Control Mechanism effectively ended mass testing and compulsory quarantine. On March 6, 2023, Xi described this as "dynamically optimizing and adjusting" COVID measures, declining to call it a policy retreat.
August 2022When U.S. House Speaker Nancy Pelosi arrived in Taiwan on August 2, 2022, nationalist hashtags on Weibo drew billions of views, and figures such as Hu Xijin, the former editor of the Global Times, voiced disappointment that the government's military response had been weaker than expected.
How it was handled · From August 4, Beijing staged large-scale live-fire exercises around Taiwan, with ballistic missiles landing in Japan's exclusive economic zone. At the same time, Weibo outages were reported, and the Foreign Ministry asked the public for patience, saying the United States and Taiwan would "gradually and persistently feel the countermeasures."
November 2020Shortly after Jack Ma publicly criticized financial regulators, Ant Group's $37 billion initial public offering was halted on November 3, 2020, and the regulatory crackdown on platform companies that followed eroded the confidence of private businesses and investors. The decision ran counter to Xi's record of supporting the private economy as a provincial official.
How it was handled · At the Central Economic Work Conference on December 15, 2022, Xi declared, "I have always supported private enterprises." On February 17, 2025, he chaired a symposium with private entrepreneurs, pledging the "two unwaverings" (liangge haobu dongyao) and the removal of obstacles to fair competition (the official report lists six speakers, including Ren Zhengfei and Lei Jun; Jack Ma's attendance is confirmed only by press reports). The Private Economy Promotion Law was passed on April 30, 2025, and took effect on May 20.
July 2022A movement of buyers of stalled presale apartments refusing to pay their mortgages spread to more than 200 projects and at least 80 developers. The housing slump was squeezing both the assets of urban households and local government finances.
How it was handled · The China Banking and Insurance Regulatory Commission instructed banks to meet developers' reasonable funding needs, and a Politburo meeting chaired by Xi on July 28, 2022, wrote "guaranteeing the delivery of homes" (baojiaolou) into its readout for the first time. A September 26, 2024, Politburo meeting called for the property market to "halt its decline and stabilize" (zhidie huiwen) and ordered more lending to "whitelisted" projects, but policy stayed with local-government responsibility and targeted support rather than a large-scale direct bailout.

Room to maneuver

On Taiwan, Xi appears to have almost no room for verbal concessions. Because the Party's legitimacy and nationalist opinion are both at stake, he cannot take back language such as "the first red line"; but he has wide discretion over how forcefully he acts — choosing the scale and timing of exercises, as in 2022, or substituting coast guard patrols, as in 2026 — and the vacuum in the military leadership appears to narrow his room to harden toward the use of force. On chips, rare earths, and tariffs, he has broad room to concede through time-limited suspensions and extensions, apparently because he can package them in a narrative of "counter capacity" and technological self-reliance and so faces little domestic backlash. On issues where his domestic base has little interest — North Korea, Iran, and Ukraine — his calculations in bargaining with Washington, more than domestic constraints, appear to set the limits. On domestic issues tied directly to the lives of urban households, such as housing and the private economy, he can reverse course abruptly, as with the lifting of COVID controls in 2022, but even then he is likely to avoid any words that admit a retreat.

Words before and after a change of office

The difference between what Xi said before and after taking power appears small. As vice president, in Mexico on February 11, 2009, he called foreigners who meddle in China's affairs "foreigners with full bellies and nothing better to do," and the loyalty standard in his April 27, 2005, column as Zhejiang Party secretary reappeared almost verbatim in the 2022 Party Congress report; his views of outside criticism and of the Party organization point the same way before and after. What changed was the volume and firmness of his public statements. Before taking power, as a local official and vice president, he rarely spoke on foreign affairs; once in power, he began to speak directly of "struggle" and of containment by "Western countries led by the United States." On the private economy, there was a gap between his record of support in Fujian and Zhejiang and the crackdown of 2020–2021, but since 2022 he has moved to close it, saying he has "always supported" private enterprises.

Earlier leaders who faced the same structure

In a September 4, 1989, talk with leading members of the Central Committee, Deng Xiaoping laid down the guideline that, on the international situation, China should "observe the situation coolly," "hold our ground," and "act calmly." After the bombing of the Chinese Embassy in Belgrade in May 1999, large anti-American protests were allowed under Jiang Zemin's leadership, and Hu Jintao, then vice president, endorsed the protests in a May 9 televised address as an expression of patriotism while calling on protesters to refrain from "extreme and illegal acts."

Checked against theory

Jessica Chen Weiss · Powerful Patriots: Nationalist Protest in China's Foreign Relations (Oxford University Press, 2014); supplemented by Weiss and Dafoe, "Authoritarian Audiences and Government Rhetoric in International Crises: Evidence from China" (2018 manuscript) Conditional

Interpretation
Weiss argues that authoritarian leaders can use anti-foreign protests as signals: allowing them conveys a resolve that cannot back down, while suppressing them conveys a willingness to negotiate. She finds that anti-Japanese protests in 1985, 2005, and 2012 and anti-American protests in 1999 were allowed, that anti-Japanese protests in 1990, 1996, and 2010 and anti-American protests in 2001 were suppressed, and that protests over Taiwan have never been allowed. A follow-up experimental study found that Chinese citizens also think less of leaders who back down after making threats, but that government explanations along the lines of biding one's time sharply reduce that cost.
Comparison
During Pelosi's 2022 visit, the authorities allowed no street protests and cooled opinion on Weibo, sending a signal of resolve through large-scale exercises instead, and the Foreign Ministry's explanation that the countermeasures would be felt "gradually and persistently" appears consistent with the explanatory effect the experimental study describes. Under Xi, however, protest is rarely used as a signal at all, and public opinion appears to have become less a tool for signaling resolve than a constraint the authorities regulate.

Jessica L. Weeks · Autocratic Audience Costs: Regime Type and Signaling Resolve, International Organization 62(1), 2008, pp.35-64 Conditional

Interpretation
Weeks argues that nondemocratic leaders can also be held accountable at home and can therefore generate audience costs. That depends on three conditions — whether domestic political groups can coordinate to punish the leader, whether they view backing down unfavorably, and whether outsiders can observe the likelihood of punishment — and where elites can solve the coordination problem, such regimes are no worse at signaling than democracies.
Comparison
By this logic, the more Xi weakens the elite's and the military leadership's capacity for collective coordination by replacing them, the smaller the domestic cost of backing down. His execution of major reversals without elite resistance — the lifting of COVID controls in 2022 and the time-limited suspension of rare-earth controls in 2025–2026 — is consistent with this. The same logic implies, however, that because the likelihood of domestic punishment cannot be observed from outside, his warnings on Taiwan may carry less binding force as signals; no case has yet tested this directly.

How he decides: hypotheses and forecasts

Patterns drawn from the decision record. Each hypothesis carries a forecast that the next decision can confirm or refute.

1. When Xi withdraws an economic measure that hurts the other side, he does so only through a dated, time-limited suspension that leaves the legal basis intact, and he preserves his leverage by renewing that deadline for short periods at each summit.

Evidence and counterexamples
Supporting cases: November 7, 2025 (a one-year suspension of expanded rare-earth controls); November 9, 2025 (the ban on exports to the United States suspended until November 27, 2026); and September 24, 2026 (the suspension extended by two months, to January 10, 2027, according to a U.S. announcement). The April 2025 licensing requirement on seven medium and heavy rare earths was not even suspended, and on June 22, 2026, during the suspension, Beijing added 10 U.S. companies to its control list. A possible counterexample is the complete lifting of zero-COVID on December 7, 2022; but that was a domestic policy, and because it was packaged as "dynamic optimization" without any admission of retreat, it can also be read as a variant of the same pattern.
What would confirm or refute it
If, around the next deadline (January 10, 2027), the Ministry of Commerce again issues a dated suspension or reimposes the controls, the hypothesis holds; if it issues an announcement repealing (feizhi or chexiao) Nos. 61 and 62 or No. 46, it fails.

2. Large named joint exercises around Taiwan are held briefly, for one to three days, right after specific triggers (a presidential inauguration or speech, a high-level U.S. visit, a large arms sale); while a U.S.-China summit is on the calendar, coast guard "law enforcement" patrols take their place.

Evidence and counterexamples
Exercises following triggers: August 4, 2022; May 23, 2024; October 14, 2024; April 1, 2025; and December 29, 2025. From January to September 2026, a period of back-to-back summits, no named encirclement exercise was reported; instead, a coast guard "quasi-quarantine" operation in waters east of Taiwan on June 1–6 and monthly rotating coast guard patrols filled the gap. The counterexample is Strait Thunder-2025A in April 2025, which came during the tariff war but before a summit date had been fixed.
What would confirm or refute it
If, between the APEC summit in Shenzhen in November 2026 and the G20 summit in Miami in December 2026, a large arms sale or a U.S. transit by President Lai is not followed within two weeks by a named exercise, the trigger-exercise link weakens. If a named exercise is held while a summit is on the calendar, the substitution element fails.

3. In matters of succession and military command, Xi puts confirmation of personal loyalty ahead of continuity in operational capability; he will not name a successor and intends to remain general secretary beyond 2027.

Evidence and counterexamples
Supporting cases: the 2023 overhaul of the Rocket Force leadership; the October 17, 2025, expulsion from the Party and the military of Vice Chairman He Weidong and eight others; and the January 24, 2026, investigation of Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli, which left the CMC with two members. The loyalty standard of being ready to risk everything in a crisis runs consistently from the 2005 column (D) to the 2022 report (E). Against it: in October 2025, Xi promoted Zhang Shengmin to vice chairman, keeping the CMC minimally functional.
What would confirm or refute it
If, at the Fifth Plenum10 on October 26–29, 2026, or at the 21st Party Congress in 2027, someone born in the late 1960s or later is named CMC vice chairman or to a clear succession post, the hypothesis fails; if Xi keeps both the general secretaryship and the CMC chairmanship, it holds.

Time horizon and power base

Time horizon · Born in June 1953, Xi is 73. His third term as general secretary began at the 20th Party Congress in October 2022. The next milestones are the 21st Party Congress, expected in the second half of 2027, and the March 2028 session of the National People's Congress11 (NPC), when his term as president expires. A 2018 constitutional amendment removed the term limit on the presidency, and no successor has been named. Before then, the Fifth Plenary Session of the 20th Central Committee will meet October 26–29, 2026, with "exercising full and rigorous Party self-governance" on its agenda.

Power base · Xi's power rests on his chairmanship of the Central Military Commission (CMC) and his grip on Party discipline and personnel. In January 2026, CMC Vice Chairman Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli, chief of the Joint Staff Department, were placed under investigation; in August, the NPC Standing Committee removed both from the state CMC, and in September they were expelled from the Party and the military. That reportedly leaves only Xi and Zhang Shengmin, a vice chairman who rose through the political-work and discipline system, on the commission. His confirmed inner circle includes Cai Qi, a member of the Politburo Standing Committee and director of the General Office of the Central Committee, who also became president of the Central Party School in June 2026, and Wang Yi, a Politburo member and foreign minister.

Possible conflicts with personal interest · The purges make Xi personally more secure, but one analysis (CSIS, February 2026) argues that, by leaving no operational commanders on the CMC, they erode the People's Liberation Army's readiness for joint operations. Declining to name a successor likewise helps him hold on to power but is likely to undermine the regime's ability to manage an orderly succession.

Key terms

  1. Assassin's mace (shashoujian, 杀手锏) · A Chinese expression for a secret weapon that overpowers an opponent in a single stroke. Xi Jinping has applied it to core technologies that make other countries dependent on China. ↩
  2. Grand unification (da yitong, 大一統) · The traditional Chinese political idea that all under heaven should be united under a single legitimate dynasty. It originates in the Gongyang Commentary on the Spring and Autumn Annals. ↩
  3. Shi (勢) · The force and momentum that a configuration of circumstances generates. In Chinese military classics such as Sun Tzu's Art of War, it means reading the potential of a situation and turning it to advantage. ↩
  4. Leninist party organization · The organizing principle under which a vanguard party of professional revolutionaries leads the state and the military, and within the party lower bodies obey the decisions of higher ones (democratic centralism). ↩
  5. Criticize Lin, Criticize Confucius (pi Lin pi Kong, 批林批孔) · A political campaign in China from 1973 to 1976 that attacked Lin Biao and Confucius together. ↩
  6. Parabellum · From the Latin maxim "If you want peace, prepare for war." Alastair Iain Johnston used the term for a hard realpolitik disposition that treats conflict as zero-sum and resorts to force when the balance of power is favorable. ↩
  7. Selectorate · In the theory developed by Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and others, the set of people who can influence the choice of a leader. The core within it that actually keeps the leader in power is called the winning coalition. ↩
  8. Political-legal apparatus (zhengfa, 政法) · The system through which the Chinese Communist Party directs the police, procuratorates, courts, and state security organs together. ↩
  9. Central Military Commission · China's supreme military command body. The party and the state each have a commission with identical membership, and its chairman holds final command authority. ↩
  10. Fifth Plenum · The fifth plenary session of the Chinese Communist Party's Central Committee. Plenums held between party congresses set major policy lines and personnel appointments. ↩
  11. National People's Congress (NPC) · China's highest organ of state power. Its Standing Committee handles legislation when the full congress is not in session. ↩
  12. Anti-Secession Law · A law China enacted in 2005. It sets out the conditions under which Beijing may use non-peaceful means against "secession," such as Taiwanese independence (Article 8). ↩
  13. Thucydides Trap · The hypothesis that a rising power and an established power are prone to collide. The name was coined by Graham Allison. ↩
  14. Veto player · An institution or person that can block or delay a decision-maker's decisions (in the United States, Congress and the courts; in Taiwan, the Legislative Yuan). ↩
