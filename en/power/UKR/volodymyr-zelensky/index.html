Worldview Assessment

Zelensky appears to regard Russia as a party that does not honor the commitments it signs and the United States as an indispensable but conditional partner, and to believe that security can come only from legally binding guarantees or alliances. Under U.S. pressure he has accepted reversible concessions, such as a ceasefire and giving up the NATO bid, while handing legal changes to the territory to a referendum; he has moved quickly to replace military commanders after open disagreements, but has kept his closest aides until an outside event forced the issue.

How he sees the world
His distrust of Russia's signature was evident from early in his term. When he agreed to the Steinmeier1 formula in 2019, he attached the condition that "there can be no elections at the barrel of machine guns" (A); at the White House in 2025, he said Putin had broken the 2019 ceasefire agreement (C); and in agreeing in 2026 to halt strikes on energy facilities, he said publicly that he was not sure Russia would comply. On December 6, 2024, the 30th anniversary of the Budapest Memorandum2, he said that no country's signature or promises are enough to ensure security (E; verified through English-language reporting). Of the United States, his November 21, 2025, address framed the choice as one between "the loss of our dignity" and "the risk of losing a key partner" (E), revealing dependence and strain at once. Basis: October 1, 2019 · March 11, 2025 · November 21, 2025 · September 13, 2026
What he wants
The consistent goal in his public statements is to refuse legal recognition of occupied territory and to preserve an independent, sovereign state. Of Crimea he said "there's nothing to talk about here" (April 22, 2025; C); he declared that "Ukrainians will not gift their land to the occupier" (August 9, 2025; E); and on August 23, 2026, he summed up his strategy as bringing the war to an end and preserving an independent and sovereign state (C). The way he describes the aim of the war, however, has shifted: from his 2019 inaugural pledge that he was ready to give up, "without any hesitation, my position to bring peace" (E), to his 2022 Independence Day declaration, "We used to say: peace. Now we say: victory" (E), to his acceptance in February 2026 of a freeze along the current contact line (C). Basis: April 22, 2025 · August 9, 2025 · February 17, 2026 · August 23, 2026
What he thinks works
A recurring pattern is to buy time with reversible concessions whenever U.S. support is at stake: accepting a 30-day ceasefire after the suspension of aid and intelligence (March 2025), signaling readiness to take binding security guarantees in place of NATO membership (December 2025), and halting strikes on Russian energy facilities (September 2026). Hard-to-reverse questions such as neutrality and territory, by contrast, he has said since March 27, 2022, must go to a referendum (C). His attempt to build negotiating leverage by military means, the Kursk3 incursion (August 2024) and the subsequent offer to swap territory, failed to produce the intended effect because Russia rejected the swap. Basis: March 27, 2022 · August 6, 2024 · March 11, 2025 · December 14, 2025 · September 13, 2026
People and process
He has resolved open disagreements with the military leadership by replacing commanders. After publicly disagreeing in November 2023 with Zaluzhnyi's assessment of a stalemate (C), he dismissed him the following February; in July 2026, he ended the conflict between the defense minister and the commander-in-chief by replacing both after giving them a two-week deadline. By contrast, he accepted the resignation of his closest aide, Yermak, only on the day NABU4 searched his premises, and he reversed the law placing the anti-corruption bodies under control nine days after protests and objections from the EU. The pattern appears to be that he keeps close aides until an outside event intervenes but responds quickly to public dissent from the military leadership. Basis: February 8, 2024 · July 2025 · November 2025 · July 21, 2026
Where words and decisions diverge
The gaps have arisen mainly in statements about the form and sequence of negotiations. His position that talks were possible only with "another president of Russia" (September 30, 2022; E) was reversed by his offer of direct talks with Putin in May 2025; his 2022 application for accelerated NATO membership (A) became, in December 2025, something that could be traded for security guarantees; and his doubts about a ceasefire without guarantees (February 28, 2025; C) gave way 11 days later to acceptance of a 30-day ceasefire. His refusal to recognize occupied territory legally and to withdraw from the Donbas, however, has not changed in either E- or C-grade statements, which suggests that U.S. pressure has altered procedure and sequence while the line on the legal status of territory still holds. Basis: October 4, 2022 · March 11, 2025 · May 2025 · August 9, 2025 · December 14, 2025

Relation to the country's strategic culture Assessment

Zelensky inherited the memory of the independent state lost in 1917–1921 as the lesson that internal division costs a nation its state, the failure of the 1994 Budapest Memorandum as the lesson that signatures alone do not secure a country, and the Holodomor5 as a lens for interpreting Russia's present conduct; but this language came to the fore only after Putin's 2021 essay on history and the full-scale invasion of 2022. In 2019 he set out with a civic, bilingual identity that put being Ukrainian in one's heart ahead of monuments and street names, and with the anti-establishment mandate of a media producer who bypassed conventional politics to appeal directly to the public; his practice of handing territorial questions to a referendum appears closer to these roots than to tradition. Because Kuchma, Yanukovych, and Poroshenko made different decisions within the same tradition, this section concludes that strategic culture is better understood not as a cause of his decisions but as a condition that set the range of his options and the language in which he justified them.

Inherited from the national tradition

He inherited a memory of the Ukrainian People's Republic (UNR), its brief independence in 1917–1921 and its loss, as the lesson that only unity can preserve the state. In his first Statehood Day address, on July 28, 2022, Zelensky counted the UNR and Skoropadskyi's Hetmanate among the stages of Ukrainian statehood; in his Day of Unity address on January 22, 2024, he said that "to gain your own statehood, you have to fight. To preserve it, you have to unite"; and on January 22, 2025, he attributed the loss of independence then to "ambition, discord, and enemies who took advantage of them." His rejection on August 23, 2026, of Fedorov's call for wartime elections as a "tsunami" that would split the country appears to draw on the same language. Because postponing elections can also be explained by the legal ban on elections under martial law, however, it is safer to see this memory as supplying a justification than as producing the decision. Basis: August 23, 2026 · July 28, 2022, January 22, 2024, January 22, 2025

He absorbed the 1994 Budapest Memorandum as the lesson that no country's signature is enough for security. President Kuchma signed the memorandum on December 5, 1994, and in the negotiations Washington insisted on the word "assurances" rather than "guarantees," which could have entailed military obligations. At the Munich Security Conference on February 19, 2022, five days before the full-scale invasion, Zelensky said he was initiating consultations under the memorandum, and doing so "for the last time"; on December 6, 2024, the 30th anniversary of its signing, he said real alliances and weapons were needed; and on February 4, 2025, he went so far as to say that if NATO membership were blocked, Ukraine should be given nuclear weapons (C). When he said on December 14, 2025, that he would accept security guarantees in place of NATO membership but insisted that they be legally binding and backed by the U.S. Congress, the lesson appears to have shaped the form of his demand. Basis: December 14, 2025 · March 29, 2022 · March 11, 2025 · February 19, 2022, December 6, 2024 · February 4, 2025

He uses the memory of the 1932–1933 Holodomor as a framework for interpreting Russia's present conduct. On Holodomor Remembrance Day, November 26, 2022, Zelensky convened a food security summit in Kyiv, declaring that "never again should hunger be used as a weapon" and launching the Grain From Ukraine initiative; in his November 25, 2023, remembrance address, he said that in the last century "famine came from Moscow," placing the theft of grain and attacks on ports on the same continuum. The memory appears to have served both as a language of domestic cohesion and as an instrument of food diplomacy aimed at North Africa and the Middle East. None of the decisions in the record above connects directly to this memory, so its influence on his negotiating behavior has not been established. Basis: November 26, 2022 · November 25, 2023

Roots outside the tradition

A root distinct from traditional strategic culture lies in his career as a media producer and the anti-establishment mandate of 2019. He ran the production company Kvartal 95 and starred in Servant of the People, a television series in which a schoolteacher becomes president; in the 2019 campaign he skipped press conferences and debates and reached voters directly through social media and touring shows (Drach, Reuters Institute, 2020). In his inaugural address, he announced the dissolution of the Rada6 and said that "the President is not an icon, an idol or a portrait"; signing the referendum law on January 29, 2021, he said, "We promised it – we did it," adding that important decisions could now be made by all citizens, not only by politicians. His practice of handing neutrality (March 27, 2022) and territory (since December 26, 2025) to a referendum rather than to his own signature appears more directly tied to this pledge of direct democracy than to the Cossack7 tradition of self-rule. Basis: March 27, 2022 · February 17, 2026 · October 1, 2019 · May 20, 2019, January 29, 2021

The same roots show in his wartime communication. As with the "We are all here" video recorded with members of his government outside the Presidential Office in Kyiv on February 25, 2022, he announced decisions directly on video, and in March 2022 he tailored his addresses to each country's historical memory, invoking Churchill before the British House of Commons, Pearl Harbor and 9/11 before the U.S. Congress, and the Berlin Wall before the Bundestag. Moving foreign publics directly to win support appears to have been one of his principal instruments, but the suspension of U.S. aid and intelligence after the public clash at the White House on February 28, 2025, also exposed its limit: it cannot substitute for the decisions of another government. Basis: February 25, 2022 · March 11, 2025 · March 8, 2022, 03-16, 03-17

How history is used selectively

How far he drew on national historical memory varied sharply over time. In his inaugural address on May 20, 2019, he said that "being Ukrainian is not a line in the passport" but a matter of the heart, addressing Ukrainian and Russian speakers alike, and in his New Year's address on December 31, 2019, he played down disputes over monuments and street names (what difference does it make, in effect, which monument you wait beside for your date), in contrast with Poroshenko's "Army, Language, Faith." After Putin's essay "On the Historical Unity of Russians and Ukrainians" (July 12, 2021), however, he declared in a July 28, 2021, address that "cousins and very distant relatives should not encroach" on the legacy of Kyivan Rus, established Statehood Day the same year, and on August 24, 2024, signed a law banning religious organizations affiliated with the Russian Orthodox Church, calling it a step toward "liberation from Moscow's devils." The politics of memory appears to have intensified in stages in response to Russia's historical claims and its invasion, supporting the reading that circumstances chose how tradition would be used rather than tradition guiding his decisions. Basis: October 1, 2019 · October 4, 2022 · May 20, 2019, December 31, 2019, July 28, 2021, August 24, 2024

The Budapest lesson, too, was applied differently from issue to issue. On a final settlement, meaning security guarantees in place of NATO and the question of territory, he invoked the lesson that signatures alone are not enough and demanded parliamentary ratification and binding force. On reversible interim steps, however, he relied on the other side's signature or on U.S. promises: the multinational guarantee proposal at Istanbul on March 29, 2022, the acceptance of a 30-day ceasefire without guarantees on March 11, 2025, and the halt to strikes on energy facilities on September 13, 2026, which he accepted while saying he was not sure Russia would comply. The lesson appears to work not as a principle for rejecting every agreement but as a condition attached to irreversible concessions, the same distinction drawn in the first hypothesis above. Basis: March 29, 2022 · March 11, 2025 · September 13, 2026 · December 14, 2025

Checked against classics and scholarship

Serhii Plokhy · The Gates of Europe: A History of Ukraine (Basic Books, 2015; revised edition, 2021) Fits

Interpretation
Plokhy argues that Ukraine, a strategic corridor between East and West, was shaped by empires from Rome and the Ottomans to Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, and that the crisis since 2014 is a recurrence of history that has again put Ukraine at the center of a global struggle. While treating the Cossacks as central to the formation of Ukrainian identity, he acknowledges regional diversity and portrays Ukraine as a civic nation, held together as a political community rather than by ethnic homogeneity.
Comparison
The narrative of statehood Zelensky wove in his addresses of July 28, 2021, and July 28, 2022, linking Kyivan Rus, the Cossack state, the UNR, and 1991 into a single continuum; his description at Munich on February 19, 2022, of Ukraine's forces as "an army that protects the whole of Europe"; and his civic definition in 2019 of being Ukrainian as a matter of the heart all closely track Plokhy's framework. The view of Ukraine as a frontier between empires also fits his insistence on binding Western guarantees.
Where it falls short
The framework explains why Ukraine is contested but does not predict a leader's choices. From the same geopolitical position, Yanukovych chose the 2010 non-alignment law and the Kharkiv accords, and Zelensky himself chose to freeze the conflict and accept the Steinmeier formula in 2019 before moving in the opposite direction after 2022. The Cossack tradition was mentioned in the July 28, 2022, address only as one stage of statehood, and no evidence linking it to his decisions was found.

Timothy Snyder · The Reconstruction of Nations: Poland, Ukraine, Lithuania, Belarus, 1569-1999 (Yale, 2003); Yale lecture course "The Making of Modern Ukraine" (2022) Conditional

Interpretation
In The Reconstruction of Nations, Snyder argues that the Polish, Ukrainian, Lithuanian, and Belarusian nations were made and remade over four centuries of political development and the violence of the 20th century; in his 2022 lectures, he made "Can a nation be chosen?" the central question and defined Ukraine as a postcolonial, multilingual nation. In his account, the resistance showed that Ukraine already existed as a society and a polity before February 23, 2022.
Comparison
The civic definitions in the 2019 inaugural address, which embraced Russian-speaking voters, and in the New Year's address of December 31, 2019; the social cohesion that followed his decision to stay in the capital on February 25, 2022; and wartime opinion, with 60 percent opposing the cession of the Donbas in KIIS polling, all fit the reading of a chosen, multilingual nation.
Where it falls short
As the war has dragged on, Zelensky's decisions have moved from a civic, multilingual framework toward the securitization of religion and memory, as the law of August 24, 2024, banning organizations affiliated with the Russian Orthodox Church illustrates. And because Snyder's framework concerns the choices of a society, it cannot explain the negotiating behavior of an individual leader who made concessions on a ceasefire and NATO under U.S. pressure.

Andrew Wilson · The Ukrainians: Unexpected Nation (Yale, 2000; 5th ed., 2022); Ukrainian Nationalism in the 1990s: A Minority Faith (Cambridge, 1997) Conflicts

Interpretation
Wilson portrays Ukrainian national identity as contested, built on myths and competing narratives, and Ukraine as a country where Russian predominates even in the capital and "Eurasian roots" contend with a Western orientation. His 1997 book treated Ukrainian nationalism in the 1990s as a "minority faith" concentrated in the west and center of the country. The fifth edition of 2022 adds two chapters on the period since 2014 and the war.
Comparison
It fits the Zelensky of 2019 in some respects. That Poroshenko, campaigning on "Army, Language, Faith," lost the runoff by a wide margin while a candidate who played down disputes over language and monuments took 73 percent matches Wilson's observation about the limits of ethnocultural nationalism. Kuchma's remark at a Moscow book fair in 2003, "We have created Ukraine, and now it is time to create Ukrainians," reflects the same concern.
Where it falls short
After 2022, Zelensky, who grew up speaking Russian, became the voice of the statehood narrative, and opposition to ceding the Donbas became the majority view nationwide, so the 1990s thesis premised on regional division cannot explain the wartime decision environment. Wilson himself appears to address this shift in the fifth edition and in a 2024 article, but their content could not be checked.

Strategic culture should be understood not as the cause of Zelensky's decisions but as a condition that set the range of his options and the language in which he justified them. Within the same tradition, Kuchma signed the Budapest Memorandum and wrote Ukraine Is Not Russia (2003) while pursuing a multivector foreign policy, and signed the Minsk agreements of 2014–2015 as Ukraine's representative; Yanukovych chose the 2010 non-alignment law and the Kharkiv accords, which extended the Black Sea Fleet's basing rights to 2042 (236 votes in the Rada); and Poroshenko, while championing "Army, Language, Faith" and writing the NATO and EU course into the constitution, accepted the Minsk agreements under military pressure. Zelensky's own choices in 2019 and after 2022 differ sharply, so his next decision should not be predicted from an essentialist premise that this is simply how Ukraine is.

Domestic coalition and room to maneuver Assessment

Zelensky began with a large electoral mandate, 73 percent in the 2019 runoff and an outright majority for Servant of the People, but in wartime, with elections suspended, his effective base appears to have become issue-by-issue votes in the Rada, the trust of the military and veterans, a civil society willing to take to the streets, and Western partners who hold both aid and its conditions. This base gives him relatively wide latitude for concessions most of the public accepts, such as a ceasefire or a frozen front line, but only narrow latitude on legal recognition of occupied territory, withdrawal from areas under Ukrainian control, or any erosion of the anti-corruption bodies' independence, as the street backlash of 2019, 2025, and 2026 showed.

Coalition

Servant of the People faction in the Rada (leader: David Arakhamia)It seeks parliamentary involvement in major decisions and respect for voting procedures; according to Carnegie's analysis, Arakhamia has built up his independence by criticizing the government and demanding a role for parliament.
Because the party has lacked a majority of its own for nearly two years and depends on issue-by-issue support from other factions, its power over decisions that require a Rada vote (extending martial law, 313 votes in July 2026; confirming the defense minister, 312 votes in August; ratifying an agreement) appears to lie more in delay and in attaching conditions than in outright veto. According to analysts, the Presidential Office's grip on parliament weakened after Yermak's resignation in November 2025, so that power is likely growing.
The military leadership, serving soldiers, and veterans, together with the nationalist civic groups that move with themThey reject territorial concessions and any agreement that looks like capitulation (the "No to capitulation" protests of 2019), and in 2026 demanded Fedorov-style defense reform and anti-corruption measures, better rules on pay, contracts, and unauthorized absence, and competent commanders.
In a KIIS survey (May–June 2026), figures with military backgrounds (Zaluzhnyi at 73 percent, Budanov at 70 percent, and Robert Brovdi at 70 percent) were more trusted than the president (61 percent), so open resistance from this group appears to bear directly on his legitimacy. Twice, in 2019 and in 2026, street protests led by veterans forced him to revise decisions or contain the fallout.
Public opinion (KIIS surveys) and anti-corruption civil societyThe public opposes ceding the Donbas (60 percent, July–August 2026) yet accepts a freeze along the front line backed by security guarantees (59 percent), wants elections held after a peace agreement (57 percent, December 2025), and names rooting out corruption in government as its top domestic concern (50 percent, September 2026).
Trust in the president remains a majority at 54 percent (August–September 2026), but 72 percent of respondents hold him responsible for corruption among his associates, which points to strong public pressure on corruption. With elections suspended in wartime, public opinion acts on decisions not through the ballot but through street protests and the referendum condition the president himself has pledged.
Western partners (an external base: the U.S. administration, the European Commission, and EU member states)Washington has pressed for a ceasefire and progress in negotiations; the EU has made the rule of law and anti-corruption reform, above all stronger independence and wider jurisdiction for NABU and the Specialized Anti-Corruption Prosecutor's Office (SAPO), conditions for disbursing aid.
The EU's €90 billion loan for 2026–2027 comes with "strong conditionality," and the U.S. suspension of aid and intelligence in March 2025 led within 11 days to acceptance of a 30-day ceasefire, so this external base appears to exert more immediate influence than any domestic group. Domestic trust in the United States is low, however, at 21 percent (December 2025), so simply accepting U.S. demands is likely to increase friction with his domestic base.

Decisions that strained the coalition

October 2019When he agreed to the Steinmeier formula on October 1, opposition parties (European Solidarity, Batkivshchyna, Svoboda, and Holos), together with veterans and nationalist groups, staged "No to capitulation" protests, with turnout put at about 12,000 on October 6 and about 50,000 on October 14 (Wikipedia's tally). Volunteer veterans rejected the planned disengagement at Zolote and said they would stay put.
How it was handled · On the day of signing, he publicly set the condition that there would be no elections while armed forces remained; on October 26, he went to Zolote in person and demanded that the volunteers withdraw their weapons, telling one of them, "You can't be voicing any ultimatums to me in the first place"; and disengagement at Zolote began on October 29. At the Normandy summit in Paris on December 9, he publicly set out his lines: no federalization, no trading of territory, and restoration of border control before elections. The protesters, saying those lines had not been crossed, ended their sit-in. The formula was never implemented.
July 2025When he signed a law on July 22 placing NABU and SAPO under the control of the prosecutor general, pensioners, young professionals, serving soldiers, and civic activists protested in several cities, including outside the Presidential Office and the Rada in Kyiv, and the European Commission raised objections.
How it was handled · Within nine days he reversed course: the Rada passed a bill he submitted in his own name restoring the bodies' independence by 331–0 on July 31 (its first livestreamed vote since the full-scale invasion), and he signed it the same day, with the law taking effect on August 1. The European Commission said the law addressed the key issues but that it would review the final text.
February 2026With the withdrawal of Ukrainian forces from the parts of the Donbas they control a sticking point in the U.S.-brokered peace plan, 75 percent of respondents in a KIIS survey (November–December 2025) called the "Russian plan," a package that bundles withdrawal from the rest of Donetsk Oblast with recognition of the occupied territories as Russian, "completely unacceptable." When the president said he would put territorial questions to a referendum, about 50 civic groups declared on February 23, 2026, that this would be unlawful, citing among other things the ban on referendums under martial law.
How it was handled · On February 17, 2026, he rejected withdrawal from the Donbas, saying people would "never forgive this," said a freeze along the current contact line was acceptable, and maintained the condition that any agreement must go to a referendum. No official government response to the civic groups' objection could be found.
July 2026After he dismissed Defense Minister Fedorov on July 15, protests spread to more than 20 cities, including Kyiv, Kharkiv, Lviv, and Odesa (2,000 to 3,000 people in Kyiv on July 24), and veterans such as Dmytro Koziatynskyi demanded Fedorov's reinstatement and the dismissal of Commander-in-Chief Syrskyi.
How it was handled · On July 21, he replaced Syrskyi with Drapatyi but did not bring Fedorov back, and on August 19 the Rada confirmed Khmara as defense minister (312 votes). When Fedorov called for wartime elections on August 18, the protest leaders came out against elections and the movement split; the protests are generally recorded as having ended around August 19. On August 23, the president rejected wartime elections, saying they would split the country.

Room to maneuver

Legal recognition of occupied territory and withdrawal from areas held by Ukrainian forces appear to be where his room for concession is narrowest. With 60 percent of the public opposed to ceding the Donbas, soldiers and veterans having already taken to the streets in 2019, and referendums barred under martial law, there is little scope for the president to sign such terms himself, and the only open route is to hand them to a referendum. By contrast, a frozen front line or partial ceasefire backed by security guarantees, or binding guarantees in place of NATO membership, are accepted by a majority of the public, and similar concessions in March and December 2025 drew no street backlash, so his room there looks comparatively wide. The anti-corruption bodies and military appointments are areas where he has had to reverse or adjust course when Western aid conditions and street opinion coincide; yet, as his refusal to reinstate Fedorov and his continued postponement of elections show, he also has room to hold firm, drawing on his appointment powers and public support for elections after peace (57 percent). With domestic trust in the United States at just 21 percent, the domestic cost of standing up to U.S. demands is small, but his dependence on aid makes losing his external base costly, which appears to limit how hard a line he can take with Washington.

Words before and after a change of office

The gap between the candidate and the newly inaugurated president was small. His 2019 campaign centered on a lasting ceasefire in the Donbas, and he suggested the fighting could end if the shooting simply stopped (as summarized by FIIA); in his inaugural address he declared that "our first task is ceasefire in the Donbas" and that he was ready to give up, "without any hesitation, my position to bring peace," and in his first year he acted on those words by agreeing to the Steinmeier formula and the disengagement at Zolote. The larger shift came over time within the same office. Analysts date a hardening of his line to the autumn of 2020 (FIIA, 2022); in October 2022 he put into effect a decree declaring negotiations with Putin impossible, moving the means and tone of negotiation sharply toward confrontation, before partly returning to ceasefire proposals and offers of direct talks from 2025. By contrast, his inaugural statement that Crimea and the Donbas are Ukrainian territory and the refusal to trade territory he declared in Paris in 2019 remained unchanged through 2026, so on the legal status of territory the gap is small.

Earlier leaders who faced the same structure

After the Minsk8 II agreement of February 2015, President Poroshenko pushed a constitutional amendment on decentralization, presented as implementing the deal, through its first reading in the Rada on August 31 with 265 votes. The same day, in clashes outside the Rada, a grenade thrown by a Donbas veteran, among other attacks, killed three members of the National Guard and injured more than 130 people (OSW's tally); the Radical Party left the coalition, and the second reading had still not been held as of April 2016. His party, European Solidarity, was among the organizers of the "No to capitulation" protests of 2019.

Checked against theory

Robert Putnam · "Diplomacy and Domestic Politics: The Logic of Two-Level Games," International Organization 42(3), 1988, pp. 427–460 Conditional

Interpretation
Putnam argued that a negotiator must reach agreement with the other side while also securing ratification at home, whether through formal votes or informally through public opinion, and that if the other side believes the range of agreements acceptable at home (the win-set9) is narrow, that narrowness can become leverage in the negotiation.
Comparison
Zelensky's statement that people would "never forgive" a withdrawal from the Donbas, and the referendum condition he attached to territory, appear to be cases of putting a narrow domestic win-set on the table, and his line on the legal status of territory held through 2026. The leverage effect, however, was not borne out. Washington extracted acceptance of a ceasefire by suspending aid in March 2025, and Russia did not drop its withdrawal demand; in a structure where one counterpart is also the patron, domestic constraints appear not to have translated into concessions from the other side.

Bruce Bueno de Mesquita, Alastair Smith, Randolph Siverson, and James Morrow · The Logic of Political Survival (MIT Press, 2003) Conditional

Interpretation
They argue that leaders with large winning coalitions must sustain support through public goods and policy performance rather than private rewards, and therefore put more effort into wars they can win; if they lose, coalition members readily defect to challengers, so their risk of losing power is high.
Comparison
His refusal to make territorial concessions that would look like defeat, and his concentration of resources on the war effort, fit the theory's prediction for a leader with a large coalition. The theory, however, presupposes replacement through elections. In Ukraine, with elections suspended under martial law, defections from the winning coalition have surfaced not at the ballot box but in street protests (July 2025, July 2026) and in Western aid conditions, and the president has responded each time by partly changing policy or personnel, so the mechanism through which the theory operates appears to differ.

How he decides: hypotheses and forecasts

Patterns drawn from the decision record. Each hypothesis carries a forecast that the next decision can confirm or refute.

1. Under U.S. pressure, Zelensky accepts reversible concessions (a ceasefire, a frozen front line, giving up the NATO bid, a halt to energy strikes), but legal changes to the territory (recognizing occupied land, withdrawing from areas Ukraine controls) he hands to a referendum rather than to his own signature, and as preconditions he demands a ceasefire of at least 60 days and security guarantees.

Evidence and counterexamples
Supporting: Unlike his moves on March 11, 2025 (accepting a 30-day ceasefire), December 14, 2025 (signaling willingness to drop the NATO bid), and September 13, 2026 (conditionally accepting a halt to energy strikes), his statements on territory (April 22 and August 9, 2025; February 17, 2026) did not change, and since December 26, 2025, he has made a referendum the condition. Counterexamples: On October 1, 2019, he agreed to the Steinmeier formula directly, without a referendum and in the face of domestic backlash, and on March 29, 2022, his delegation tabled a proposal to defer the Crimea question for 15 years.
What would confirm or refute it
Confirmed if, at the trilateral talks in the UAE or elsewhere, Ukraine agrees to a document on a partial truce or a frozen front line while keeping territorial clauses subject to a referendum. Refuted if the president signs, without a referendum, a document providing for withdrawal from the parts of Donetsk Oblast under Ukrainian control, or seeks to settle territorial clauses through Rada ratification alone.
85%By June 30, 2027, the Ukrainian government will not have signed a document committing Ukrainian forces to withdraw from the parts of Donetsk Oblast under Ukrainian control

2. On personnel and institutional questions involving aides and the military leadership, Zelensky reverses or amends decisions within one to three weeks when street protests coincide with conditions attached to Western financial support; on questions that bear on his own tenure, such as the timing of elections, he holds to his condition that a ceasefire and security guarantees must come first, whatever the pressure.

Evidence and counterexamples
Supporting: Unlike his handling of the NABU law of July 2025 (signed on the 22nd, reversed on the 31st), the command change of July 21, 2026 (replacing the commander-in-chief six days into the protests), and Yermak's resignation in November 2025 (on the day of the searches), on elections he maintained the precondition of a ceasefire and security guarantees on December 11, 2025, February 11, 2026, and August 23, 2026. Counterexamples: On February 8, 2024, he replaced Zaluzhnyi, who enjoyed 92 percent trust, and he never granted the central demand of the July 2026 protests, Fedorov's reinstatement.
What would confirm or refute it
Confirmed if a bill extending martial law is again submitted and passed before the October 31 expiry and no election date is set before a ceasefire. Refuted if a law permitting elections under martial law without a ceasefire is passed, or a presidential election date is announced.

Time horizon and power base

Time horizon · Zelensky took office on May 20, 2019, and his five-year constitutional term was due to end on May 20, 2024. He remains in office because the martial law statute and the electoral code bar elections while martial law is in force, and Article 108 of the constitution provides that the president exercises his powers until a newly elected president takes office. Martial law runs through October 31 under the Rada's 20th extension (July 2026, 313 votes); the next milestone is the extension vote due just before it lapses. Article 103's limit of two consecutive terms does not bar him from running again, and if the office fell vacant, the speaker of the Verkhovna Rada (Ruslan Stefanchuk) would act as president.

Power base · He began with 73 percent of the vote in the 2019 runoff and an outright majority for his party, Servant of the People, but since around 2024 the party has lacked a stable majority of its own and has relied on issue-by-issue support from other factions (Carnegie, April 2026). His inner circle comprises Kyrylo Budanov, head of the Presidential Office (since January 2, 2026; formerly chief of military intelligence, the HUR); Rustem Umerov, secretary of the National Security and Defense Council (NSDC10) and head of the negotiating delegation; David Arakhamia, leader of the Servant of the People faction; Prime Minister Serhii Koretskyi (since July 16, 2026); Defense Minister Yevhen Khmara (since August 19); and Commander-in-Chief Mykhailo Drapatyi (since July 21). Andriy Yermak, the former head of the Presidential Office who had led the negotiations, resigned on November 28, 2025, the day the National Anti-Corruption Bureau (NABU) searched his premises, and in May 2026 he appeared in court on money-laundering charges.

Possible conflicts with personal interest · The longer elections are postponed, the more the president's personal legitimacy becomes an item on the negotiating table. Russia has questioned his authority to sign (Putin in January 2025, Lavrov in August 2025), Washington has pressed for elections, and in August 2026 Mykhailo Fedorov, the dismissed defense minister, called for a wartime vote. An agreement that provides for elections after a ceasefire would therefore end the war for the country but put the president personally at risk of losing his mandate, so the national interest and his own may diverge over the timing of a ceasefire and the election clause. Moreover, with his closest aide, Yermak, on trial in a corruption case (the NABU director has said the president himself is not under investigation), the independence of the anti-corruption bodies has already collided with EU accession and financial support once, in July 2025.

Who decides and who can veto, issue by issue

IssueDecidesVeto players
The Russia-Ukraine WarUnder Article 106 of the constitution, the president represents the state in foreign relations and directs negotiations and the conclusion of treaties, and as supreme commander-in-chief he has the final say on the terms of a ceasefire. Day-to-day negotiation is handled by a delegation led by NSDC Secretary Umerov (with Budanov, Arakhamia, and the chief of the General Staff), while major decisions, such as accepting the proposal for trilateral talks in the UAE in September 2026, are made by the president himself. Zelensky has said he would put any agreement involving territory to ratification by the Rada, to a referendum, or to both (December 26, 2025), and the 20-point draft likewise makes its entry into force conditional on ratification or approval by referendum within 60 days.Article 73 of the constitution reserves changes to the territory for an all-Ukrainian referendum, and the law bars referendums and elections under martial law (about 50 civic groups called a referendum unlawful in February 2026), so martial law would first have to be lifted or the law amended. The constitution cannot be amended under martial law (Article 157), and the course toward NATO membership was written into it by a 2019 amendment. The Rada holds the power to ratify treaties (Article 85) and to approve each 90-day extension of martial law, but the ruling party has no majority of its own, and as of July 2025 only 12 of the Constitutional Court's 18 seats were filled, making it difficult for the court to rule. Though they carry no legal authority, public opinion (KIIS, July–August 2026: 60 percent oppose ceding the Donbas, while 59 percent accept freezing the front line in exchange for security guarantees) and resistance within the military (nationwide protests after the defense minister's dismissal in July 2026 led to a change of commander-in-chief) have functioned as de facto vetoes.

Decision-making environment and firsthand accounts

The formal bodies are the NSDC, which the president chairs (Article 107 of the constitution), and the Headquarters of the Supreme Commander-in-Chief (the Stavka). Since Yermak's resignation in November 2025, Umerov, as NSDC secretary, has headed the negotiating delegation, with Budanov, Arakhamia, and the chief of the General Staff taking part (Geneva, February 2026). According to reports, the delegation submits a report to the president after each round and the president decides the next move (Abu Dhabi, January–February 2026). Under Yermak, the Presidential Office was widely seen as tightly controlling information and access; since his departure, one analysis finds, power has dispersed toward the Rada and the government (Carnegie, April 2026). In July 2026, the president settled the conflict between the defense minister and the commander-in-chief through personnel changes after giving the two men a two-week deadline (by his own account).

  • David Arakhamia, leader of the Servant of the People faction (head of the delegation at the 2022 Istanbul talks) · He said Russia's core demand was neutrality (staying out of NATO), and that Ukraine refused because of the NATO course in the constitution and because "there was no confidence in the Russians that they would do it." He said the delegation had no authority to sign; only Zelensky and Putin could. He denied that Boris Johnson, then British prime minister, had forced Kyiv's hand, as some accounts claim. (Testimony given after the fact by a participant (November 2023). It is consistent with reporting on the content of the March 2022 Istanbul proposal, but Russian and British accounts differ.)
  • Mykhailo Fedorov, former defense minister (a member of every government since Zelensky's first cabinet in 2019; dismissed in July 2026) · In a video after his dismissal, he said Ukraine faced a "systemic crisis of governance," that the old system "protects itself. It is scared of change," and called for a mechanism for holding elections in wartime. (As the dismissed official, he has a stake in the matter. Zelensky rebutted him on August 23 by disclosing the course of the conflict between the defense minister and the commander-in-chief and his rejection of a proposed "ministry of war"; both sides acknowledge that the conflict existed.)
  • Donald Trump, President of the United States (counterpart head of state) · On February 19, 2025, he called Zelensky "a Dictator without Elections"; in an interview with The New York Times in January 2026, he said "he didn't have the cards from Day 1" and that Zelensky's only card was Trump himself. On March 5, 2026, he said "it's unthinkable that he's the obstacle," blaming Ukraine for the stalled negotiations. (Public statements confirmed by multiple outlets. They may, however, be instruments of negotiating pressure rather than assessments, and are best weighed against actual U.S. decisions on Ukraine (such as the approval in September 2026 of a $2.68 billion sale of air-defense equipment).)
  • Russian President Vladimir Putin and Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov (the adversary) · On January 28, 2025, Putin said that, his term having expired, Zelensky "has no right to sign anything," and that only the speaker of the Rada could repeal the 2022 decree banning negotiations; in August 2025, Lavrov questioned the legitimacy of the signatory. On June 16, 2026, the Kremlin again repeated its invitation for Zelensky to come to Moscow. (Confirmed as official statements. Article 108 of Ukraine's constitution provides for the president to exercise his powers until his successor takes office, and leading constitutional scholars regard his continued tenure as constitutional. Moscow's insistence on hosting any meeting appears to signal that it does not regard Zelensky as an equal counterpart.)

Key terms

  1. Steinmeier formula · A plan proposed in 2015 by Frank-Walter Steinmeier, then Germany's foreign minister, that set the sequence of local elections in Donbas and the granting of special status. When the Ukrainian government agreed to it in 2019, it provoked a backlash at home. ↩
  2. Budapest Memorandum · The 1994 document in which the United States, Britain, and Russia pledged to respect Ukraine's sovereignty and territorial integrity in return for Ukraine giving up its nuclear weapons. ↩
  3. Kursk incursion · The August 2024 operation in which Ukrainian forces pushed into Russia's Kursk region. ↩
  4. NABU · The National Anti-Corruption Bureau of Ukraine. ↩
  5. Holodomor · The famine of 1932–1933 in which millions of Ukrainians starved to death as a result of grain requisitions by Stalin's regime. ↩
  6. Verkhovna Rada · Ukraine's unicameral parliament. ↩
  7. Cossacks · Self-governing armed communities of the Ukrainian steppe from the 15th to the 18th century. Ukrainians remember them as the root of their tradition of freedom and self-rule. ↩
  8. Minsk agreements · The two agreements of 2014 and 2015 meant to secure a ceasefire in the Donbas war. They were never implemented. ↩
  9. Win-set · In Robert Putnam's two-level game theory, the range of international agreements that domestic constituencies will accept. The narrower the range, the less room a leader has to make concessions. ↩
  10. NSDC · Ukraine's National Security and Defense Council, a body chaired by the president that coordinates security policy. ↩
  11. Buffer zone · An intermediate zone that prevents direct clashes between great powers. Russia has long viewed the countries along its borders this way. ↩
