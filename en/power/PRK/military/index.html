The armed forces of North Korea

Command · Capabilities · Deployment · Posture changes · As of October 8, 2026

← Power Structures · North KoreaDecision-maker · Kim Jong UnNorth Korea's Party and military bodies and the Supreme People's AssemblyCountry profile · North Korea

North Korea's armed forces answer to one man: Kim Jong Un, who is both chairman of the State Affairs Commission and chairman of the Party Central Military Commission, and in whom Article 89 of the 2026 revised constitution also vests command of the nuclear forces. With roughly 60 nuclear warheads (a SIPRI estimate), long-range artillery massed near the Military Demarcation Line that can reach the Seoul metropolitan area, and short-range ballistic missiles that can reach all of South Korea, Pyongyang can hold the South at risk, and in 2026 both its ballistic missile testing and its naval buildup expanded sharply. Its military weight rests on three things: forward deployment south of the Pyongyang–Wonsan line, the "border fortification" along the Military Demarcation Line, and the troop deployment to Russia.

Command and control

As chairman of the State Affairs Commission, Kim commands all of the state's armed forces; as general secretary of the Workers' Party of Korea, he also chairs the Party Central Military Commission. State and party command over the military thus rest in the same hands. Inside the military, the General Political Bureau (the party's apparatus within the armed forces) and the Military Security Bureau (the counterintelligence body) keep watch over commanders, and on August 29, 2026, a meeting of the Party Central Military Commission moved the director of the General Political Bureau to defense minister and the head of the Military Security Bureau to first deputy director of the General Political Bureau, and installed the Air Force's political commissar as head of the Military Security Bureau, changing the leadership of both bodies at once. Under the law of September 8, 2022, and Article 89 of the 2026 constitution, the chairman of the State Affairs Commission commands the nuclear forces and may delegate authority over their use to the national nuclear forces command organization. No state body can block a mobilization of the military or a command appointment by vote.

Does the military follow the decision-maker?

Loyalty · judgment estimate
Follows the decision-maker · Collective defection by the military highly unlikely Probability of a military-led coup by 2030: 1–3% (judgment estimate)
  • Kim reshuffles the military high command often, and reverses himself just as readily. At the Ninth Party Congress in February 2026, Pak Jong Chon and Ri Pyong Chol were dropped from the roster of Central Committee members, yet following the Second Meeting of the Ninth Party Central Military Commission on August 29, Pak returned as vice chairman of the Central Military Commission and reappeared on the roster of Politburo members. The same meeting dismissed Defense Minister No Kwang Chol, who moved to the party's Munitions Industry Department as first vice director, and named Kim Song Gi, then director of the General Political Bureau, to replace him (KCNA, August 30, as carried by Yonhap and Korea JoongAng Daily).
  • That meeting also moved Yu Kwang U, director of the Military Security Bureau, to first deputy director of the General Political Bureau, and installed Om Ju Ho, political commissar of the Air Force, as head of the Military Security Bureau. With General Political Bureau director Kim Song Gi moving to the defense ministry, the leadership of both bodies that monitor the armed forces changed at a single meeting. Around the same time, Pyongyang publicly denounced corruption inside the military and signaled a tough crackdown (Herald Business and Korea JoongAng Daily, August 30, 2026).
  • Accountability reaches the munitions sector as well. After the failed launch of the destroyer Kang Kon on May 21, 2025, three officials, including the chief engineer of the Chongjin Shipyard, were arrested, and Ri Hyong Son, vice director of the party's Munitions Industry Department, was detained; Kim called the accident a "criminal act" (KCNA, May 2025).
  • On foreign battlefields, too, the observed behavior has been loyalty rather than defection. On January 13, 2025, the NIS told the National Assembly Intelligence Committee of a North Korean soldier who, about to be captured, shouted "General Kim Jong Un" and tried to blow himself up with a grenade, and of a note recovered from a fallen soldier that stressed self-detonation before capture.
  • The regime's uncertainty stems less from the military than from succession. The NIS judges that Kim Ju Ae is in the process of being designated successor, though Pyongyang has never announced an official title for her (NIS briefing, September 1, 2026).

Capabilities

North Korea is estimated to field about 1.28 million troops and some 60 nuclear warheads. On the Korean Peninsula, the forces that matter most are the long-range artillery and multiple rocket launchers that put the Seoul metropolitan area within range and the short-range ballistic missiles Pyongyang touts as capable of carrying tactical nuclear weapons. Its intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) and hypersonic missiles, aimed at the U.S. mainland, are what Pyongyang holds up as leverage in negotiations with Washington. In December 2025, Pyongyang showed the construction site of an 8,700-ton nuclear-powered submarine; in 2026, it announced that two 5,000-ton destroyers had entered service, and it has declared its intent to arm the navy with nuclear weapons.

Proficiency

Proficiency · judgment estimate
Wide gaps across branches · Missile and artillery forces strong, navy and air force weak; troops deployed to Russia have gained combat experience Overall proficiency: low-moderate to moderate (judgment estimate)
  • The early phase of the deployment to Russia exposed a lack of experience in modern warfare. On January 13, 2025, the NIS reported more than 3,000 casualties and attributed them to futile long-range fire at drones and to assault tactics without rear-area fire support; in September 2025, it put the number killed at about 2,000.
  • Pyongyang is absorbing those lessons into its institutions. On September 1, 2026, the NIS reported that North Korea had expanded the Reconnaissance General Bureau into a Reconnaissance Intelligence General Bureau to strengthen counterintelligence, and had given its chief, Ri Chang Ho, a concurrent post in order to process operational intelligence from the war in Ukraine. Ri appeared at the February military parade as first deputy commander of the overseas special operations forces (Seoul Shinmun). Ukraine's military intelligence agency (HUR) said in August that the newly deployed troops included about 400 drone operators.
  • Missile units are training far more often. Japan's defense minister said that through September 2026 North Korea had fired at least 15 ballistic and other missiles on nine occasions, against five missiles on four occasions in all of 2025. On March 14, the JCS detected about 10 ballistic missiles, which Pyongyang claimed were a salvo fired by 12 super-large multiple rocket launchers (JCS and KCNA).
  • The navy's ability to operate its new ships remains unproven. The Kang Kon capsized during its launch on May 21, 2025, and was not deployed until September 6, 2026. South Korean officials and outside experts said the Choe Hyon was likely built with Russian help and questioned its readiness for active service (Stars and Stripes, March 4, 2026).

Deployment

The center of gravity of North Korea's forces is the forward area facing South Korea. The Ministry of National Defense assesses that about 70 percent of the ground forces, about 60 percent of the navy, and about 40 percent of the air force's fighters are deployed south of the Pyongyang–Wonsan line (2022 Defense White Paper). Since April 2024, North Korea has been pursuing a "border fortification" along the Military Demarcation Line, erecting walls and fences and laying mines; the JCS expects the work to be finished in the second half of 2028. Combat troops, engineers, and construction workers have gone to Russia in successive waves since the fall of 2024, and Ukraine puts the cumulative number deployed at about 25,000.
