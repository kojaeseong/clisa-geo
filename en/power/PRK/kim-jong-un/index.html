Worldview Assessment

For Kim Jong Un, international politics is a world of raw power in which regimes that give up their nuclear weapons collapse, and his goal appears to be to lock in North Korea's status as a nuclear power as an irreversible premise and then face the United States on that footing. He completes displays of capability and legal and institutional steps before entering negotiations; carries out announced deadlines and nonmilitary measures to the letter while holding back on steps that would be costly in relations with China and the United States; and leaves the pressure to Kim Yo Jong while making the decisions himself.

How he sees the world
Two days after the fourth nuclear test, a KCNA commentary wrote that the regimes of Saddam Hussein and Gaddafi, having given up the foundations of their own nuclear development, "could not escape the fate of destruction," and in 2022 Kim Jong Un declared that there would never be "denuclearization first," daring his adversaries to impose sanctions "for a hundred days, a thousand days" (E). Weeks after the U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran that began in late February 2026, he vowed to make North Korea's position as a nuclear state "absolutely irreversible" (E, based on English-language reporting), apparently reaffirming the same lesson. The decision of the plenary meeting1 at the end of 2019, which framed the U.S.–North Korea standoff after the Hanoi2 breakdown as a contest between self-reliance and sanctions, appears to spring from the same worldview. Basis: January 6, 2016 · December 31, 2019 · September 8, 2022 · March 23, 2026
What he wants
His goal appears to be not denuclearization talks but a relationship in which the United States "respects the present position of our state"—in other words, recognition as a nuclear power (September 2025, February 2026, E). At the same time, he has cut South Korea loose as the other party in "relations between two states hostile3 to each other" and excluded it permanently from "the category of compatriots" (December 2023, February 2026, E), putting survival and status as a separate state ahead of the reunification championed by his grandfather and father. Basis: December 30, 2023 · September 21, 2025 · February 21, 2026 · March 23, 2026
What he thinks works
In his 2018 New Year address, he packed the "nuclear button" threat and the offer to take part in the Olympics into a single speech (E), and he entered negotiations with a testing halt only after declaring the "completion of the state nuclear force." He announced a year-end deadline in April 2019 and enforced it to the letter that December (E), but in June 2020, right after blowing up the liaison office, he suspended the General Staff's plans for military action. He appears to carry out as announced the measures against which adversaries would find direct retaliation difficult, while keeping costly military measures in reserve as instruments of threat. Basis: November 29, 2017 · January 1, 2018 · April 20, 2018 · April 12, 2019 · December 31, 2019 · June 16, 2020
People and process
In 2013 he publicly executed his uncle Jang Song Thaek, Pyongyang's channel to Beijing, putting internal control ahead of external costs, and he set the date of the fourth nuclear test himself in a handwritten order. In 2020 and 2026, he sent out pressure and rebuffs in statements in Kim Yo Jong's name and set only the final level himself, and he wrote to President Trump proposing that the two talk directly, without going through President Moon Jae-in (B, not confirmed by North Korea). The structure appears to be one in which one man makes the big decisions while external signaling is split between proxies and direct leader-to-leader dealings. Basis: December 12, 2013 · January 6, 2016 · August 5, 2019 · June 16, 2020 · August 19, 2026
Where his words and decisions diverge
He signed the commitment "to work toward complete denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula" in the 2018 Singapore joint statement (A), but in 2022 he shut the door on denuclearization by law and broadened the conditions for nuclear use beyond his 2018 pledge not to use nuclear weapons first unless North Korea's sovereignty and interests were violated. On the deployment to Russia, he kept silent for more than six months before formally claiming it as his decision in April 2025 (A). Conciliatory words in negotiating phases have often gone unkept, but his words on nuclear possession have been borne out by actual decisions. With costly decisions like the deployment to Russia, he tends to act first and only later declare the decision his own. Basis: January 1, 2018 · June 12, 2018 · September 8, 2022 · October 8, 2024 · April 28, 2025

Relation to the country's strategic culture Assessment

Kim Jong Un inherited as his language of justification Juche4 and self-reliance, Kim Il Sung's anti-Japanese partisan5 tradition, and a small state's memory of surviving among great powers by refusing sadae6, or deference to the strong. Yet the direct rationale for nuclear weapons appears to come more from lessons of international politics—Iraq and Libya, and in 2026 Iran—and from the Party-centered Leninist7 organization. He has drawn on tradition selectively, stripping away in 2023–26 the cause of national reunification that his grandfather and father placed at the center of the regime's legitimacy and replacing his father's Songun8, or military-first politics, with Party-centered rule, which shows that opposite decisions can emerge from the same tradition. Of the three interpretations, this section judges that Andrei Lankov's account, which puts the rational calculation of regime survival first, fits best, and that B. R. Myers's account, which places pan-Korean racial nationalism at the core, conflicts with the decision to exclude South Koreans from the category of compatriots.

Inherited from the national tradition

He inherited the language of Juche and self-reliance, which rejects dependence on outsiders. In a speech on December 28, 1955, Kim Il Sung championed Juche while criticizing Soviet-style dogmatism and the sadaejuui, or toadyism, of serving great powers. After the Hanoi breakdown, Kim Jong Un told the December 2019 plenary meeting that the U.S.–North Korea standoff had "now boiled down to a clear stand-off between self-reliance and sanctions" and that North Korea would not count on sanctions relief (per the English version). On his ascent of Mount Paektu in October 2019, too, he called for holding high "the banner of self-reliance." Yet military and economic cooperation with Russia deepened over the same period, which suggests that self-reliance serves less as actual self-sufficiency than as a justification for refusing concessions to outsiders. Basis: December 31, 2019 · September 8, 2022 · October 2019 · December 28, 2019~31

The partisan tradition takes Kim Il Sung's experience in the anti-Japanese guerrilla units in Manchuria as the template for running the state. Wada Haruki characterized the Kim Il Sung system as a "guerrilla-band state" in which the whole population moves like guerrilla fighters. Right after working-level talks broke down in Stockholm, Kim Jong Un rode a white horse up Mount Paektu in October 2019; in December he returned to the revolutionary battle sites in the Mount Paektu area with military commanders in tow, and at a plenary meeting at the end of that month he announced the abandonment of the testing-halt pledge. His December 2015 remark about possessing the H-bomb was made at the Phyongchon Revolutionary Site, where Kim Il Sung set up the first arms factory right after liberation, and it too appears to justify nuclear development as a continuation of the revolutionary tradition of armed self-defense. Basis: December 10, 2015 · January 6, 2016 · December 31, 2019 · October 2019·December 2019

There is also a small state's survival instinct—wedged among China, Russia, Japan, and the United States—to avoid being bound to any one great power, and a rejection of sadae. Kim Jong Un executed Jang Song Thaek, the channel to China who had headed the joint North Korea–China committee, and a May 2017 KCNA commentary (issued under the name Kim Chol, not in Kim Jong Un's name) criticized China by name, declaring that the DPRK "will never beg for the maintenance of friendship with China" (per the English version). During Xi Jinping's visit to the North in June 2026, too, denuclearization was kept out of the readouts. Pompeo recalled Kim saying he needed U.S. forces in Korea to check China (B, unconfirmed), but the 2024 alliance with Russia and the troop deployment amount to leaning heavily on a single great power, which suggests that this tradition does not always produce balancing. Basis: December 12, 2013 · June 8, 2026 · 2018 · May 3, 2017

Roots outside the tradition

The direct rationale for nuclear weapons came less from tradition than from lessons of international politics drawn from cases of regime change. During the 2011 air campaign against Libya, North Korea's Foreign Ministry said that "Libya's nuclear dismantlement," as touted by the United States, had "turned out to be a mode of aggression," and two days after the fourth nuclear test a KCNA commentary wrote that the Saddam Hussein and Gaddafi regimes "could not escape the fate of destruction after being deprived of their foundations for nuclear development and giving up nuclear programs of their own accord" (per the English versions). His vow in March 2026, weeks after the strikes on Iran began in late February, to make nuclear status "absolutely irreversible" appears to carry the same lesson forward. This logic appears to point in the same direction as a small state's memory of survival among great powers, the two reinforcing each other. Basis: January 6, 2016 · September 8, 2022 · March 23, 2026 · January 8, 2016

The organizational culture of a Leninist party, which uses Party congresses and plenary meetings as the vehicle for decisions, also has roots distinct from the partisan tradition. In 2016 Kim Jong Un convened a Party congress for the first time in 36 years; he announced the 2018 testing halt, the 2019 abandonment of that pledge, and the 2023 definition of "two hostile states" all as plenary decisions; and the Party Rules9 as revised in 2021 replaced "Songun politics" with "people-first politics" as the basic mode of politics. He appears to have moved the stage of decision-making from the National Defence Commission and the military-centered rule of his father's era back to the Party. Basis: April 20, 2018 · December 30, 2023 · February 21, 2026 · January 2021

How history is used selectively

He kept his grandfather's Paektu-bloodline legitimacy while discarding the reunification legacy of his grandfather and father. In his policy speech10 of January 15, 2024, Kim Jong Un ordered the removal of the "eye-sore" Monument to the Three Charters for National Reunification, erected in 2001 under Kim Jong Il, and the same speech called for eliminating the concepts of "reunification," "reconciliation," and "fellow countrymen" from the national history and for no longer using expressions such as "80 million compatriots" in domestic life. Yet the same speech also raised the matter of occupying the Republic of Korea and incorporating it into North Korean territory in the event of war, which suggests that he stripped away the national narrative but kept the territorial claim. The "state-first principle," which first appeared in the Rodong Sinmun the day after the November 2017 declaration that the nuclear force was complete, appears to have foreshadowed the shift in the unit of legitimacy from the nation to the state. Basis: December 30, 2023 · February 21, 2026 · November 29, 2017 · January 15, 2024

He drew on his father's Songun and his grandfather's byungjin11, or parallel development, as needed. The line of developing the economy and nuclear forces in parallel, adopted at the plenary meeting of March 31, 2013, was presented as carrying the economy-and-defense parallel line of the Kim Il Sung and Kim Jong Il eras onto a higher stage, and in 2018 he declared that line's "victory" to justify the testing halt and the turn to the economy. In 2021 he dropped "Songun politics," his father's emblem, from the Party Rules, moving away from what Wada Haruki called the "regular-army state" of the Kim Jong Il era. The same ancestral line was used to justify both the nuclear buildup and the testing halt, which suggests that decisions choose the tradition more than the tradition determines the decisions. Basis: April 20, 2018 · September 8, 2022 · March 31, 2013 · January 2021

Checked against classics and scholarship

Bruce Cumings · North Korea: Another Country (The New Press, 2004) Conditional

Interpretation
Cumings argued that U.S. bombing in the Korean War devastated North Korea on a scale comparable to what the Soviet Union and Poland suffered, and, noting that the United States threatened to use nuclear weapons during the war and kept them in South Korea until 1991, he explained North Korea's garrison state and hostile behavior as a response to that memory and that threat. Emphasizing Kim Il Sung's career as an anti-Japanese guerrilla and his distance from the Soviet Union, he saw North Korea not as a satellite but as an anticolonial nationalist state.
Comparison
His justification of the nuclear forces as "self-defense" and deterrence (December 10, 2015; September 8, 2022), his objections to joint exercises (letter of August 5, 2019, B), and his remark locking in nuclear status right after the strikes on Iran (March 23, 2026) fit the interpretation that he is responding to external threats. Partisan legitimacy and the rejection of sadae also connect to the anticolonial nationalism Cumings emphasized.
Where it falls short
The 2024 deployment to Russia was not a defensive response but an expeditionary intervention, and the removal of the concept of "compatriots" in 2023–26 runs against the Korean nationalism at the center of Cumings's account. In August 2026, when Washington scaled back joint exercises, Kim held to his conditions and the following month observed a new missile test, so the implication that hostility recedes as threats recede has not been borne out.

Andrei Lankov · The Real North Korea: Life and Politics in the Failed Stalinist Utopia (Oxford University Press, 2013) Fits

Interpretation
Lankov argued that North Korea's leadership is not irrational but acts rationally to ensure regime survival and, because of the lesson of Libya, will not give up its nuclear weapons. He also contended that as long as a prosperous South Korea exists, Chinese-style reform and opening would expose the gap with the South and could bring down the regime, so the leadership avoids reform.
Comparison
The 2022 law's rejection of any "bargaining chip," the locking in of nuclear status in the 2026 constitution, and the decision not to offer anything beyond Yongbyon in Hanoi match the prediction that the regime will not give up its nuclear weapons. This section judges that the diagnosis that South Korea's existence is a weak point for the regime's legitimacy can serve as a framework for explaining the motives behind the 2024 ban on expressions such as "80 million compatriots" and the 2026 exclusion of South Korea from "the category of compatriots" (whether Lankov himself reads these decisions this way could not be confirmed).
Where it falls short
Contrary to Lankov's account of extracting aid through calculated escalation, Kim has taken nuclear weapons off the table entirely since 2022, did not respond to the scaled-back U.S. exercises in August 2026, and instead adopted a new mode of bargaining in the troop deployment to Russia. The forecast that the status quo is unsustainable in the long run has not been borne out in the 13 years since the book's publication.

B. R. Myers · The Cleanest Race: How North Koreans See Themselves and Why It Matters (Melville House, 2010) Conflicts

Interpretation
Myers argued that North Korea's ruling ideology is not Marxism-Leninism or Juche but a race-based nationalism inherited from Japanese fascism, with Juche closer to window dressing aimed at foreigners. In later writing, he held that the regime's legitimacy rests on a pan-Korean mission of reunification and that its nuclear program is meant to force the withdrawal of U.S. troops (2017), and he countered that the "two states" declaration of late 2023 was merely propaganda for external consumption, not an abandonment of reunification (2024).
Comparison
The theory's central proposition is that the regime cannot abandon the idea of "compatriots" and the mission of reunification. Yet Kim Jong Un banned expressions such as "80 million compatriots" not in external propaganda but in domestic political and ideological life, demolished the reunification monument, and erased reunification language from the constitution and the Party Rules in 2026. His raising of the occupation and incorporation of the Republic of Korea in wartime fits Myers's reading that territorial ambitions remain, but because the justification has shifted from the nation to war against a hostile state, this section judges that it conflicts with the central proposition.
Where it falls short
The Institute for National Security Strategy's finding that the demand for the withdrawal of U.S. forces was deleted in the 2026 revision of the Party Rules runs against the 2017 claim that the nuclear program aims to force a U.S. withdrawal, as does Pompeo's recollection that Kim said he needed U.S. forces in Korea to check China (B, unconfirmed). Whether "two states" is a long-term deception, however, is hard to disprove until a reunification phase returns, and in May 2026 Hyunseung Lee also read it as a tactical redefinition rather than an abandonment.

Strategic culture should be seen not as the cause of Kim Jong Un's decisions but as a condition that sets the range of options and the language of justification. On the same Paektu bloodline and Juche tradition, Kim Il Sung put forward the principles of independence, peace, and great national unity in the July 4 South-North Joint Statement of 1972, and Kim Jong Il went to the inter-Korean summit of 2000, whereas Kim Jong Un stripped away the cause of reunification with "two hostile states" in 2023—and he himself had signed the Panmunjom Declaration in 2018. The next decision should therefore not be predicted from the essentialist premise that North Korea is simply like that; cultural explanation appears to gain predictive power only when tradition and the lessons of international politics point the same way, as with nuclear weapons.

Domestic coalition and room to maneuver Assessment

Kim Jong Un controls a small elite at the Party center and the military and security services by drawing on the legitimacy of Paektu-bloodline succession and replacing and purging them at will. What actually narrows his room for decision appears to be less the demands of his support coalition than the logic of nuclear status and regime legitimacy that he himself has formalized. Because the elite's standing depends on him personally, they have virtually no means of sanctioning him for policy failures or reversals, so changes of direction such as resuming dialogue with Washington or deploying troops to Russia carry small domestic costs. Reversing the nuclear status and the exclusion of South Korea that he has written into the constitution and the Party Rules, however, would tear down the legitimacy he has built, a cost high enough that his room to do so appears narrow.

Coalition

The elite of the Party Political Bureau and Central Committee (core cadres who uphold Paektu-bloodline succession as the basis of legitimacy)Status and personal safety, the preservation of privileges, and the survival of the Kim Jong Un system that guarantees their positions
Formally the highest decision-making body, but as the Ninth Party Congress (February 2026) showed—with 161 of the 250 Central Committee members (64 percent) replaced, the Presidium of the Political Bureau reorganized into a five-member body, and veterans such as Choe Ryong Hae, Pak Jong Chon, and Ri Pyong Chol stepping down—its members appear to be pieces that Kim Jong Un swaps out rather than actors who constrain him. The 2013 verdict on Jang Song Thaek, which publicly listed among his crimes turning his department into a "little kingdom" and plotting a coup, served as a public declaration that building an independent power base within the elite is taboo.
The Korean People's Army and the security services (the Ministry of State Security and others)Institutional standing and a share of resources, nuclear and missile development and combat experience, and state rewards for meritorious service
As holders of coercive power, they have large potential influence, but after the dismissal of Chief of the General Staff Ri Yong Ho in July 2012, the military leadership was replaced in quick succession and Party control of the military through the Party Central Military Commission12 was reestablished (38 North). The security services appear mainly as instruments for carrying out purges, as when a special military tribunal of the Ministry of State Security handled Jang Song Thaek's trial. After the deployment to Russia, Kim Jong Un publicly expanded rewards for the military, conferring the title of Hero of the DPRK on soldiers killed in action and pledging support for bereaved families, and at the Ninth Party Congress the defense minister, the chief of the General Staff, and the director of the General Political Bureau were named to the leadership.
Residents of the capital, Pyongyang, and privileged groups (families of cadres, occupations the regime favors, and families of decorated veterans)Priority in housing and supplies, and keeping the right to live in the capital
Moderate, it appears. At the Eighth Party Congress in 2021, the regime made the construction of 50,000 homes in Pyongyang a top priority and announced their completion in February 2026, and it said it had built apartments for the bereaved families of soldiers killed in the Russia deployment across from the memorial museum in Pyongyang. Concentrating visible benefits in the capital even in hard economic times appears to be an allocation aimed at keeping this group from turning away.
Ordinary residents (provincial residents who depend on jangmadang market trading and on food supplies)Securing food, freedom to trade in the markets, and better living conditions
Weak. With no channel for collective organization, they cannot block decisions directly, but Kim Jong Un acknowledged at a June 2021 plenary meeting that the people's food situation was "getting tense" (per the English translation). Since January 2023, the regime has pushed a state monopoly on grain sales through state grain shops and tightened controls to steer market distribution back toward state-run stores, which suggests that residents' demands are managed through tighter control rather than by changing the direction of policy.

Decisions that strained the coalition

December 2013Jang Song Thaek—his uncle by marriage, his former guardian, and director of the Party's Administrative Department—expanded that department and built independent influence over the export of resources such as coal and over personnel appointments, which collided with Kim Jong Un's one-man rule. The verdict said he had massively increased his department's staff and converted it into a "little kingdom," had coal and other underground resources sold off at random, handed over land in the Rason economic and trade zone to a foreign country for 50 years, and plotted a coup using senior military officers.
How it was handled · Jang was stripped of all posts and expelled from the Party at an enlarged meeting of the Political Bureau on December 8 and executed after a special military tribunal of the Ministry of State Security on December 12, after which the verdict was published through KCNA. Around that time, a series of personnel moves purged networks tied to Jang Song Thaek and Kim Kyong Hui and installed loyalists (38 North analysis).
December 2019When the Hanoi summit broke down in February 2019 without sanctions relief, the expectation of sanctions easing that underpinned the April 2018 line of concentrating all efforts on economic construction collapsed. Sanctions, the COVID-19 border closure, and typhoon damage in 2020 then combined to leave him unable to deliver the economic results promised at home.
How it was handled · At the Fifth Plenary Meeting of the Seventh Central Committee on December 28–31, 2019, he framed the U.S.–North Korea standoff as a contest between self-reliance and sanctions and launched an "offensive for making a breakthrough head-on" with the economy as the main front, acknowledging stagnation across industry—metals, chemicals, power, and coal—and demanding a stepped-up struggle against anti-socialist and non-socialist practices. In his report to the Eighth Party Congress in January 2021, he admitted outright that "almost all sectors fell a long way short of the set objectives" of the five-year strategy and called the preceding five years "the worst of the worst" (per the English translation); as remedies, he put forward greater self-reliance and made the construction of 50,000 homes in Pyongyang a top priority.
December 2023Departing from the national reunification line that his grandfather Kim Il Sung and father Kim Jong Il held up as a pillar of the regime's legitimacy, Kim Jong Un defined inter-Korean ties at the December 2023 plenary meeting as "relations between two states hostile to each other" and called it a mistake to regard South Korea as a partner for reunification. In a system that relies on the continuity of its forebears' instructions and of Paektu-bloodline legitimacy, it was a decision to discard publicly a line laid down by his predecessors.
How it was handled · In his January 15, 2024, speech to the Supreme People's Assembly, he defined South Korea as an enemy rather than a partner for reconciliation and reunification and ordered the removal of the "eye-sore" Monument to the Three Charters for National Reunification, a symbol of inter-Korean reconciliation, which was demolished January 19–23 (per the English translation). After the abolition of the agencies handling South Korean affairs (January 2024) and the deletion of reunification language from the Party Rules (June 2024), the constitution as revised in March 2026 deleted the peaceful reunification clause and, for the first time, inserted a territorial clause that excludes South Korea; one analysis finds that the same constitution sharply pared back passages on Kim Il Sung and Kim Jong Il (KEI). Reports citing inside sources say cadres were told the change was about stabilizing the state system and making diplomatic strategy more realistic (Daily NK).
October 2024Combat units sent to Russia's Kursk13 front suffered battlefield deaths, but North Korea did not officially acknowledge the deployment itself until April 2025. South Korea's NIS estimated in September 2025 that about 2,000 had been killed; North Korea and Russia have not released casualty figures.
How it was handled · On April 28, 2025, he formally declared the deployment his own decision, called all who took part "heroes," and promised to build a monument; in late August, meeting bereaved families, he expressed sorrow at having failed to save precious lives and said that he, the state, and the army would take responsibility for the families (per the English translation). Construction of the Memorial Museum of Combat Feats in Overseas Military Operations began in October 2025, a homecoming ceremony for an engineer regiment and the conferral of the title Hero of the DPRK on the fallen followed in December, and the museum opened on April 26, 2026; it honors about 280 fallen soldiers, far fewer than the NIS estimate. North Korean media said apartments for bereaved families had been built across from the museum.

Room to maneuver

Because nuclear status has been placed at the center of domestic legitimacy by the 2022 law and by the 2026 Party Congress line and constitutional revision, there appears to be virtually no room for concessions toward denuclearization. Tactical adjustments—accepting a summit with Washington, a testing moratorium, a response to scaled-back exercises—by contrast carry small domestic costs because the elite has no means of sanctioning him, and he appears able to change direction by first declaring an achievement and packaging the shift as victory, as when he halted testing in 2018 after declaring the "completion of the state nuclear force" in 2017. On policy toward the South, having gone as far as rewriting the constitution, he has little room to swing back toward conciliation, but on the hard-line side, too, he has kept to institutional and nonmilitary measures. The casualties of the deployment to Russia have been absorbed through a narrative of heroism and compensation for bereaved families, with no visible domestic backlash, so domestic constraints on either further deployments or a withdrawal appear weak; a worsening food situation is more likely to be managed through tighter control and visible projects centered on the capital than to block decisions.

Words before and after a change of office

After Kim Jong Un's name first appeared in state media at the Party Conference of September 2010, with the rank of general and the post of vice-chairman of the Party Central Military Commission, no record of public remarks by him has been found before he took power in December 2011, so there is no basis for comparing what he said before and after the change in position. His first public speech was the address of April 15, 2012, marking the centenary of Kim Il Sung's birth, four months after he took power. Instead, his words changed sharply over time in the same position: in the 2012 speech he said the people would never again have to tighten their belts and that he would join hands with anyone who wanted reunification, but in 2021 he called the preceding five years "the worst of the worst," and in 2023 he defined the North and the South as "two states hostile to each other." This shift appears to reflect not a change of position but his management of his base amid changed conditions—the Hanoi breakdown, sanctions, and the border closure.

Earlier leaders who faced the same structure

After Kim Il Sung's death in July 1994, Kim Jong Il did not immediately take the post of Party general secretary and became head of the Party only in 1997; the 1998 constitutional revision enshrined Kim Il Sung as "eternal president" and made the chairmanship of the National Defence Commission the highest post. In the meantime, hundreds of thousands starved to death in the famine of the late 1990s, and the regime promoted the "Arduous March" and Songun politics (Britannica).

Checked against theory

Bruce Bueno de Mesquita, Alastair Smith, Randolph Siverson, and James Morrow · The Logic of Political Survival (MIT Press, 2003) Fits

Interpretation
The theory holds that leaders of systems with a small winning coalition and a large selectorate14 buy loyalty by distributing private goods to coalition members rather than providing public goods, and that because members stay loyal for fear of losing their places under the next leader, the leader keeps power despite policy failures or the costs of war.
Comparison
Kim Jong Un acknowledged economic failure himself in 2021 yet moved on by restating the line without holding the leadership accountable, and by replacing 64 percent of Central Committee members at the Ninth Party Congress he showed that coalition members can be replaced at any time. Resources are concentrated on core groups—Pyongyang housing, and apartments and hero titles for the families of soldiers killed in the deployment—while ordinary residents have received an admission of food shortages and controls on the markets. Hanhee Lee (Japanese Journal of Political Science, 2018), applying the theory to the Kim Jong Un system, identified frequent rotation and co-optation of close aides and rule by terror as survival tools, while arguing that rule by terror itself leaves open the possibility of a coup by the selectorate. Yet the regime is also pursuing programs aimed at the provinces, such as the "Regional Development 20×10" policy, so private goods alone do not explain it.

Jessica L. Weeks · Autocratic Audience Costs: Regime Type and Signaling Resolve (International Organization 62(1), 2008) Conditional

Interpretation
The argument is that authoritarian leaders can generate audience costs to signal resolve if domestic elites can coordinate to punish them, but that in personalist dictatorships—where the leader controls the security services, rotates elites frequently, and holds their fate in his hands—audience costs are low and threats are therefore less credible (personalist dictatorships are about 1.8 times as likely as other regimes to meet military reciprocation from their targets).
Comparison
Elite rotation and control of the security services, as in the execution of Jang Song Thaek (2013) and the replacement of 64 percent of the Central Committee (2026), match the features of personalist dictatorship, and the absence of any sign of domestic punishment for retreats and reversals—the 2018 testing halt, the June 2020 suspension of military action, the abandonment of his predecessors' reunification line—fits the prediction that domestic audience costs are low. Yet Kim Jong Un enforced to the letter the year-end deadline he announced in 2019 and wrote nuclear status into law and the constitution, making his external commitments irreversible by his own hand. This appears to be a way of creating credibility through the logic of his own legitimacy rather than through an elite audience, and it runs against the implication that a personalist dictator's threats are less credible.

How he decides: hypotheses and forecasts

Patterns drawn from the decision record. Each hypothesis carries a forecast that the next decision can confirm or refute.

1. Just before entering negotiations or summit diplomacy, Kim Jong Un first completes a hard-to-reverse display of capability or a legal and institutional step, then presents the result as a premise that is not up for negotiation.

Evidence and counterexamples
Support: the declaration of "completing the state nuclear force" on November 29, 2017, followed by the testing halt on April 20, 2018; the codification in law on September 8, 2022; the demand that Washington respect North Korea's "present position" after the February 2026 Party Congress and the March constitutional revision; and the reception of Xi Jinping (June 8, 2026) five days after the June 3, 2026, vow of an "exponential" buildup. Counterexample: the scrapping of Punggye-ri and the testing halt on April 20, 2018, were tangible steps conceded before negotiations.
What would confirm or refute it
If a U.S.–North Korea, North Korea–Russia, or North Korea–China summit is confirmed, the hypothesis holds if a new strategic weapons test or the unveiling of a nuclear facility comes within 60 days before the meeting, and fails if restraint in testing or acceptance of nuclear-related language comes first.

  1. Plenary meeting · A plenary meeting of the Central Committee of the Workers' Party of Korea, which sets the party's major lines and policies. ↩
  2. Hanoi summit · The second U.S.-North Korea summit, held in Hanoi, Vietnam, in February 2019. It ended without an agreement. ↩
  3. Two hostile states · The line Kim Jong Un set out in December 2023, defining inter-Korean relations not as ties between compatriots but as relations between two hostile states. ↩
  4. Juche · North Korea's official guiding ideology. It proclaims independence in thought, self-sufficiency in the economy, and self-reliance in defense. ↩
  5. Partisans · Irregular guerrilla fighters rather than a regular army. North Korea grounds the regime's legitimacy in Kim Il Sung's anti-Japanese guerrilla activity. ↩
  6. Sadae (事大) · Literally "serving the great": a small country deferring to a large one. North Korea condemns it and holds up independence instead. ↩
  7. Leninist party organization · The organizing principle under which a vanguard party of professional revolutionaries leads the state and the military, and within the party lower bodies obey the decisions of higher ones (democratic centralism). ↩
  8. Songun (先軍) · The Kim Jong Il-era line that put the military ahead of all other affairs. ↩
  9. Party Rules · The supreme code setting out a party's aims and organizing principles. In North Korea it is regarded as standing above the constitution. ↩
  10. Policy speech · A speech in which a national leader sets out the direction of state affairs before the legislature. In North Korea it is delivered at the Supreme People's Assembly. ↩
  11. Byungjin line · The line North Korea adopted in 2013 of building its economy and its nuclear forces simultaneously. In 2018 it gave way to a line of concentrating on economic construction. ↩
  12. Central Military Commission · China's supreme military command body. The party and the state each have a commission with identical membership, and its chairman holds final command authority. ↩
  13. Kursk incursion · The August 2024 operation in which Ukrainian forces pushed into Russia's Kursk region. ↩
  14. Selectorate · In the theory developed by Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and others, the set of people who can influence the choice of a leader. The core within it that actually keeps the leader in power is called the winning coalition. ↩
  15. Veto player · An institution or person that can block or delay a decision-maker's decisions (in the United States, Congress and the courts; in Taiwan, the Legislative Yuan). ↩
