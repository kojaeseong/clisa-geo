The current balance of power

State Duma (September 2026 election results)450 seats

Election held September 18–20, 2026; final results certified by the Central Election Commission on September 25 (as reported by Kommersant, The Moscow Times, and Meduza). With 95.18 percent of ballots counted, party-list vote shares stood at 57.83 percent for United Russia, 13.81 percent for the Communist Party, 8.98 percent for the Liberal Democratic Party, and 7.96 percent for New People.

With 349 seats, United Russia alone clears the two-thirds (300 seats) needed to amend the constitution and pass federal constitutional laws. The other parties in the Duma form the regime-tolerated "systemic opposition," which has not opposed the government on the war or on foreign treaties, as the 397–0 vote ratifying the treaty with North Korea showed. The balance of seats in the Duma is unlikely to stand in the way of the president's foreign-policy decisions.

Votes needed

Ordinary laws; treaty ratification lawsA majority of all State Duma members (226 seats), followed by review in the Federation Council
Overriding a presidential vetoTwo-thirds of all members of each chamber (300 seats in the State Duma)
Federal constitutional laws; amendments to Chapters 3–8 of the constitutionTwo-thirds of all State Duma members (300 seats) and three-quarters of all Federation Council members; constitutional amendments also require approval by the legislatures of two-thirds of the federal subjects
Threshold for party-list seats5 percent of the vote (225 party-list seats, 225 single-member districts)

Who drives decisions in the Kremlin and parliament

Foreign and security decisions are deliberated among the permanent members of the Security Council, which the president chairs. The leaders of parliament are also permanent members, but parliament is less a place where decisions are made than one where they are approved.

United Russia holds 20 of the 34 committee chairs in the Ninth State Duma, including those of the defense, international affairs, and security committees. The variables that shape decisions appear to lie less in parliament than in the information and judgment of the Security Council's permanent members and the heads of the security services, and in the interests at play among them.
