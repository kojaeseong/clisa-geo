Worldview Assessment

For Putin, international politics appears to be a process in which Western pressure, embodied in NATO expansion, closes in on Russia's borders, and Ukraine is not a foreign country but part of historical Russia that was "given away" when the Soviet Union broke up. His pattern has been to deny his intentions right before a decision, create a fait accompli, and then justify it in long-form speeches; and when the West crosses one of his stated red lines, to choose a calibrated response while avoiding direct hostilities with NATO.

How he sees the world
After calling the unipolar model "not only unacceptable but also impossible" and NATO expansion "a serious provocation" in his 2007 Munich speech (E), he said in 2008 that what mattered was not NATO's intentions but its capabilities (C). Right after the 2004 Beslan hostage crisis, he said, "We showed ourselves to be weak. And the weak get beaten," adding that some would like "to tear from us a 'juicy piece of pie'" (E). Through his 2014 Crimea speech, with its charge that the West was "trying to sweep us into a corner" (A), his framing of Russia as the encircled party appears to have barely changed in nearly 20 years. Basis: September 4, 2004 · February 10, 2007 · April 4, 2008 · March 18, 2014
What he wants
As early as a private meeting with President George W. Bush in June 2001, he described the breakup of the Soviet Union as a loss — "Ukraine, part of Russia for centuries, given away" (B; U.S. record declassified in December 2025, in English translation) — and in his 2021 article he declared that Russia would never accept an "anti-Russia" Ukraine (E). The 2022 annexation of four regions and the June 2024 demands for neutral, non-bloc, and non-nuclear status appear to give concrete form to this aim, which most likely combines gaining territory with denying Ukraine the right to choose the Western camp. Basis: June 16, 2001 · July 12, 2021 · September 30, 2022 · June 14, 2024
What he thinks works
Against Georgia (2008) and Ukraine (2014, 2022), neither of them NATO members, he created facts on the ground with large-scale force. Against NATO, by contrast, he has gone no further than nuclear threats such as "This is not a bluff" (2022, A) and calibrated demonstrations such as the single Oreshnik1 strike (2024). In negotiations, he appears to offer only partial or temporary truces, such as halts to strikes on energy facilities or on capitals, rather than a full ceasefire, using his advantage on the battlefield as leverage. Basis: August 8, 2008 · March 18, 2014 · February 24, 2022 · September 21, 2022 · November 21, 2024 · March 18, 2025 · September 5, 2026
People and process
The Security Council meeting of February 21, 2022, took a format in which participants declared their support one by one in a recorded broadcast, and the Federation Council's consent to deployments came on the day of the decision or just before. During the 2023 Wagner2 mutiny, on the same day he called it "treason," he chose a compromise brokered by Lukashenko over punishment. External negotiating channels are divided among Ushakov, Dmitriev, Medinsky, and Kostyukov (the GRU chief), each of whom appears to report directly to the president — an arrangement that funnels information and decisions to one man. Basis: September 30, 2015 · February 21, 2022 · June 24, 2023 · January 2026
Where his words and decisions diverge
His off-the-cuff denials just before military decisions have been reversed again and again. Crimea was annexed two weeks after "No, we do not" (March 4, 2014, C); the full-scale invasion came nine days after "Of course not" (February 15, 2022, C); and four regions were annexed seven months after "our plans do not include the occupation of Ukrainian territories" (February 24, 2022, A). Conversely, the retaliation he threatened for Western actions (the "all available means" warning of September 21, 2022, and the warning of September 12, 2024, that NATO countries would be "at war with Russia") was larger than his actual response. Both his denials about his own actions and his warnings about others' are weak indicators of his decisions; his long-form articles and speeches (E) appear to be better ones. Basis: March 4, 2014 · February 15, 2022 · February 24, 2022 · September 21, 2022 · September 30, 2022 · September 12, 2024 · November 21, 2024

Relation to the country's strategic culture Assessment

Putin appears to have inherited from Russian security culture, as a language of justification, the idea of a buffer zone3 beyond the border and the "besieged fortress4" mindset, the narrative of the "gathering of the Russian lands5" to recover lost territory, and the notion that great-power status and a strong state go together. But his methods of decision — denying intent and then creating a fait accompli, and acting through a small circle of aides and tightly held information — look closer to the professional culture of a KGB case officer than to state tradition, and he draws on Eurasianism6 and the Soviet legacy only selectively. Within the same tradition, Gorbachev withdrew force from Eastern Europe, Yeltsin recognized Ukraine's borders by treaty, and Putin himself in 2002 treated Ukraine's cooperation with NATO as that country's own business. The claim that tradition dictated the 2022 full-scale invasion therefore remains unproven.

Inherited from the national tradition

He inherited the idea that Russia, having suffered repeated invasions across a plain without natural borders, can be safe only with a buffer zone beyond its frontiers, along with the "besieged fortress" mindset, the belief that outsiders seek to encircle and weaken Russia. Kissinger wrote that Russia learned its geopolitics "from the hard school of the steppe," a terrain with few fixed borders. In his 2004 Beslan address, Putin said that "the weak get beaten" (слабых бьют) and that some would like "to tear from us a 'juicy piece of pie'"; in 2008 he said the problem was NATO's capabilities, not its intentions; and in June 2024 he made Ukraine's renunciation of NATO membership a condition for a ceasefire and neutral, non-bloc status a principle. Yet Finland's accession to NATO (2023), despite its long border with Russia, drew no military response, which suggests the buffer logic does not apply equally to every neighbor. Basis: September 4, 2004 · February 10, 2007 · April 4, 2008 · August 8, 2008 · June 14, 2024

He inherited the narrative of the "gathering of the Russian lands" (собирание земель), in which the Grand Duchy of Moscow built a state by gathering the scattered lands of Rus. In a private meeting with President Bush in June 2001, Putin described the Soviet breakup as "Ukraine, part of Russia for centuries, given away" (B; U.S. English-language record); in his 2014 Crimea speech he called Crimea "an inseparable part of Russia" (A); and in June 2022 he said that Peter the Great had taken nothing but returned what was Russia's — "Taking back and reinforcing. That's what he did" — and that "it looks like it fell on us to take back and reinforce, as well" (C). The annexation of four regions a little over three months later, including areas Russian forces did not control, appears to be a case in which this narrative ran ahead of military calculation. Basis: June 16, 2001 · March 18, 2014 · June 9, 2022 · September 30, 2022

He inherited the notion that binds great-power status to a strong state (державность, государственничество). In a programmatic article published as prime minister on December 30, 1999, the day before Yeltsin resigned, Putin wrote that Russia was and would remain a great power, that for Russians a strong state is "a source and guarantor of order," and that Russia would not become a second edition of the United States or Britain. His rejection of the unipolar order in 2007 and his response to U.S. sanctions in 2025 — that "no self-respecting country" makes decisions under pressure — draw on the same language. Hill and Gaddy see this as his "Statist" identity, which takes state builders such as Peter the Great and Stalin as its models. Basis: December 30, 1999 · February 10, 2007 · August 15, 2025

Roots outside the tradition

The professional culture of the KGB case officer has roots distinct from the national strategic tradition. Hill and Gaddy identify the "Case Officer" as central among Putin's six identities, arguing that techniques for handling people and information in order to persuade or coerce, the zero-sum calculus of the Cold War, and his experience of watching East Germany collapse from Dresden in 1989 all showed in how the 2014 Crimea operation was run. A pattern of buying time with denials and then revealing a fait accompli recurs: he called the armed men in Crimea "local self-defence units" and admitted six weeks later that Russian servicemen had stood behind them, and he dodged questions about North Korean troops before officially acknowledging them six months later. Former CIA Director Burns has written that Putin and his advisers, whom he warned in Moscow in November 2021, were "badly overestimating their own position," which suggests that reliance on a small inner circle and security-dominated handling of intelligence may have contributed to the misjudgment of 2022. Basis: March 4, 2014→April 17, 2014 · February 15, 2022 · October 24, 2024→April 28, 2025 · February 21, 2022

How history is used selectively

He appears to borrow only as much of the vocabulary of Eurasianism as he needs. Unveiling his plan for a Eurasian Union in a 2011 Izvestia article, Putin wrote that "the idea is not to recreate the Soviet Union in some form" (E), and at a February 2021 meeting with media editors he said he believed in the theory of passionarity (пассионарность), Lev Gumilev's concept (C). By contrast, Alexander Dugin, often called "Putin's brain," holds no official post, no record has been found of Putin quoting him publicly, and some analysts say it is unclear whether the two have even met. Moreover, the 2021 article's thesis that Russians and Ukrainians are "one people" looks closer to the imperial-era idea of an "all-Russian nation" than to Eurasianism, which champions a multiethnic Eurasian civilization, so the reading that treats Dugin's influence as a cause of Putin's decisions rests on weak grounds. Basis: October 4, 2011 · February 14, 2021 · July 12, 2021

He uses the Soviet legacy selectively as well, inheriting the size and power of the state while discarding Bolshevik nationalities policy. In his 2005 address to the Federal Assembly he called the collapse of the Soviet Union "a major geopolitical disaster of the century" (E), yet in 2016 he accused Lenin of having "planted an atomic bomb underneath the building called Russia" (C), and on February 21, 2022, he said modern Ukraine had been created by "Bolshevik, Communist Russia" (A). This framing — rejecting the Soviet national structure that gave the union republics a right to secede while counting the territory and great-power status the Soviet Union held as things lost — appears consistent with the selective use of history that Hill and Gaddy describe as his "History Man" identity. Basis: April 25, 2005 · January 21, 2016 · February 21, 2022

Checked against classics and scholarship

Henry Kissinger · World Order (Penguin Press, 2014), chapter 2, section "The Russian Enigma"; op-ed "To settle the Ukraine crisis, start at the end" (Washington Post, March 5, 2014) Conditional

Interpretation
Kissinger saw Russia as "a uniquely 'Eurasian' power, sprawling across two continents but never entirely at home in either," one that learned its geopolitics on the steppe, a terrain with few fixed borders, and has expanded across the Eurasian landmass since the 16th century, its identity shaped by tsarism, Orthodoxy, and the Byzantine legacy. In his 2014 op-ed he wrote that to Russia, Ukraine "can never be just a foreign country," argued that Ukraine should be a bridge rather than either side's outpost, and proposed as the solution a Finland-style status outside NATO.
Comparison
The 2008 Georgia operation, the 2021 article's refusal to accept an "anti-Russia" Ukraine, and the June 2024 demand for neutral, non-bloc status fit well with a reading of buffer-zone demands rooted in insecurity and of Ukraine as "not a foreign country." The same reading explains why Putin, in negotiations, has kept limits on Ukraine's choice of camp as a core condition alongside territory.
Where it falls short
Kissinger's prescription assumed that a neutral Ukraine would satisfy Russia's security demands, but the annexation of September 30, 2022, which included areas Russian forces did not control, went beyond buffer logic. Finland's accession to NATO, despite its long border with Russia, drew no military response, so the account in which insecurity breeds expansion did not fit every case; and Kissinger himself changed his view at Davos on January 17, 2023, saying that the idea of a neutral Ukraine was "no longer meaningful."

Dmitry (Dima) Adamsky · The Russian Way of Deterrence: Strategic Culture, Coercion, and War (Stanford University Press, 2024); 「From Moscow with coercion: Russian deterrence theory and strategic culture」(Journal of Strategic Studies, 2018) Conditional

Interpretation
Adamsky argued that Russia's "strategic deterrence" fuses deterrence, compellence, and coercion, which Western theory treats separately, into a single concept for preserving or changing the status quo and shaping the strategic environment through threats, sometimes accompanied by limited use of force. In his account, it puts concrete action ahead of verbal threats and manages escalation across the nuclear, conventional, and informational domains, and its distinctiveness stems less from universal principles than from Russia's strategic culture, national mentality, military customs, and intelligence traditions.
Comparison
The nuclear threat issued with the mobilization order on September 21, 2022, the nuclear doctrine revision and single Oreshnik strike on November 21, 2024, and the avoidance of direct hostilities with NATO members fit well with his model of managing escalation by mixing threats with limited demonstrations of force. The pattern of offering only partial or temporary truces in the 2025–2026 negotiations also looks like a way of adjusting coercion step by step.
Where it falls short
The full-scale invasion of February 24, 2022, was a choice for large-scale war rather than "calibrated coercion," and Charap's 2020 forecast, citing Adamsky, that Moscow would keep a low-intensity conflict going indefinitely also missed at that point. In a book review roundtable, Kofman wrote that as the West crossed one warned-of threshold after another (permission for long-range strikes, the Kursk7 incursion), Russia's coercive measures could be seen as "partially successful at best," and Roberts noted that nuclear restraint may be better explained by battlefield conditions or fear of retaliation than by culture.

Fiona Hill and Clifford Gaddy · Mr. Putin: Operative in the Kremlin (Brookings Institution Press, 2013; expanded edition, 2015) Fits

Interpretation
Hill and Gaddy analyzed Putin as a figure whose six identities — the Statist, the History Man, the Survivalist, the Outsider, the Free Marketeer, and the Case Officer — interlock according to circumstance to produce policy. At the center are the techniques for handling people and information he learned in the KGB; his goals are the stability of the state and the restoration of great-power status; and underneath lies a distrust that sees Western civil society groups as a foreign-funded "fifth column." The authors warned against either underestimating or overestimating Putin.
Comparison
The denial and later admission about the armed men in Crimea (2014), the evasion and later official acknowledgment of North Korean troops (2024–2025), and the invasion nine days after "Of course not" (February 15, 2022) fit the case officer's pattern of moving from denial to fait accompli. Drawing on history to justify decisions, as in the 2021 article and the remarks on Peter the Great, fits the "History Man" identity, and language that puts a strong state and great-power status first fits the "Statist" identity.
Where it falls short
The book's portrait of a risk-calculating pragmatist is at odds with the intelligence failure exposed by the 2022 full-scale invasion (Burns's account). The Survivalist's emergency-reserve thinking, born of the 1990s, also showed its limits when the 2022 freeze of foreign exchange reserves held abroad sharply eroded the reserves' value as insurance. A framework in which six identities shift from situation to situation has strong explanatory power after the fact but little power to anticipate the next decision.

Strategic culture should be treated not as the cause of Putin's decisions but as a condition that sets the range of options and the language of justification. Within the same tradition, Gorbachev, in his 1988 UN speech, called freedom of choice "a universal principle to which there should be no exceptions" and withdrew tank divisions from Eastern Europe; Yeltsin, in Article 5 of the 1991 Minsk Agreement, recognized the inviolability of existing borders, Ukraine's included; and Putin himself did not stand in the way of cooperation between Ukraine and NATO in 2002. That Gorbachev nonetheless endorsed the annexation of Crimea in 2014 shows that even when attachment to the land is shared, the decision to use force has differed from leader to leader. Putin's next decision should therefore not be predicted from the essentialist claim that "this is simply how Russia is."

Domestic coalition and room to maneuver Assessment

Putin appears to stay in power, behind the forms of elections, by relying on the loyalty of a small coalition of the security services, the military command, state companies, and the economic elite; after he contained the 2023 Wagner mutiny and purged the Defense Ministry in 2024, the independent forces within this coalition capable of standing up to him have most likely dwindled further. The pressure that narrows his choices therefore appears to come less from elite vetoes than from public opinion. Most Russians favor ending the war but oppose returning annexed territory, and they pushed back hard against the 2022 mobilization. Both extremes — returning territory and mobilizing again — thus appear blocked, leaving the options in between as the real ones: freezing the front, partial truces, and intensified strikes.

Coalition

The security services (the siloviki8, including the FSB) and the prosecutorial and investigative agenciesThey appear to want regime security, the power to suppress opposition, and a share of the assets seized in wartime. Stanovaya argues that because the security services' performance is measured by the number of arrests and asset seizures, repression is growing from the bottom up, and that arrests have reached the network of former Defense Minister Shoigu, the Defense Ministry leadership, regional deputy governors, and even senior judges.
High (judgment). Holding the means to punish other elites, and having carried out the suppression of the mutiny and the purge, they appear to be the most influential group in practice. Whether they are pushing to continue or to end the war in the peace negotiations, however, cannot be confirmed from public sources.
The military command (the Defense Ministry and the General Staff)They appear to want troops, budgets, and latitude on the battlefield, and to escape blame for failures of command. In June 2023 Prigozhin launched his mutiny demanding the dismissal of Defense Minister Shoigu; Deputy Defense Minister Ivanov was arrested on April 23, 2024, Shoigu was replaced on May 12 by the economic official Belousov, and in May generals including Popov and Shamarin were charged with bribery one after another.
Medium to low (judgment). Since the 2024 shake-up, the Defense Ministry has been run by an economic official and its generals have become targets of corruption investigations, making it look more like an executive agency than an independent political force. A single-source report that the military command's optimistic battlefield reports shaped negotiating decisions in September 2026, however, suggests it retains influence as a supplier of information.
Economic elites of state companies and big business (including oligarchs)They appear to want sanctions relief, recovery of assets abroad, relief from heavy taxes and high interest rates, and protection of property rights. Stanovaya argues that, with business squeezed by successive tax increases and high interest rates, Central Bank Governor Nabiullina has become the least popular figure among part of the elite, and that large-scale asset seizures targeting Russians living abroad, critics of the war, and underperforming companies are under way.
Low (judgment). Because they could themselves become targets of seizure, open opposition is rare, and they have no visible collective means of blocking decisions. Their interests overlap, however, with the agenda of sanctions relief and Russia-U.S. economic cooperation pursued through special envoy Dmitriev, and they appear to be used in defining what Russia would gain from a deal.
Public opinion: hard-line nationalist bloggers ("turbo-patriots") and the general publicHard-liners have demanded victory without concessions and escalation (general mobilization, strikes on government buildings in Kyiv, and, for some, nuclear use). As for the general public, in a Levada9 Center survey in August 2026, 68 percent supported the military operations while 59 percent said it was time to move to peace talks; in September 2025, 80 percent said they would support Putin ending the war immediately, but that fell to 33 percent if the condition was returning annexed territory; and in January 2026, if no agreement could be reached, 59 percent chose intensifying strikes and 21 percent further concessions.
Medium (judgment). Hard-liners moved the agenda in 2022 — the replacement of commanders, mobilization, and strikes on infrastructure — but were punished when they targeted Putin directly. The public has no institutional means to replace him but reacts when costs reach daily life, as when his approval rating fell from 83 percent to 77 percent after the 2022 mobilization; this reaction appears to be one reason the Kremlin has avoided another mobilization.

Decisions that strained the coalition

September 2022When he announced a partial mobilization of reservists on September 21, protests broke out in which more than 1,300 people were detained that day (according to OVD-Info), and large numbers of men crossed the border to avoid the draft. Even the pro-government RT editor-in-chief Margarita Simonyan criticized how it was carried out, and Putin's approval rating in Levada Center surveys fell from 83 percent in August to 77 percent in September.
How it was handled · At a Security Council meeting on September 29, Putin, citing the wrongful call-up of fathers of large families and people with chronic illnesses, said "it's necessary to correct all the mistakes," but he held neither Defense Minister Shoigu nor regional officials to account. On October 28 Shoigu reported that the mobilization of 300,000 had been completed; from then on, the ranks were filled by sharply raising signing bonuses for contract soldiers, and in June 2026 Putin did not publicly back the hard-liners' calls for general mobilization.
October 2022After the collapse of the Kharkiv front in September and the withdrawal from Lyman on October 1, Chechen leader Ramzan Kadyrov publicly denounced Central Military District commander Alexander Lapin as "a mediocrity," and military bloggers10 pointed to troop shortages and delays in striking infrastructure. The withdrawal from the city of Kherson on November 9 was announced on television by Defense Minister Shoigu and the overall commander, Sergei Surovikin.
How it was handled · Lapin was reported to have been removed from command in late October but returned in January 2023 as chief of staff of the Ground Forces. The Kremlin tolerated criticism aimed at generals and adopted measures the hard-liners had demanded, such as mobilization (September) and strikes on infrastructure (October), and it was the military command, not Putin, that announced the Kherson withdrawal. By contrast, Igor Girkin, who denounced Putin directly as a "cowardly mediocrity," was arrested in July 2023 and on January 25, 2024, sentenced to four years in prison for inciting extremism.
June 2023On June 23–24, Prigozhin of the Wagner Group, claiming the Defense Ministry had attacked a Wagner camp, demanded the dismissal of Defense Minister Shoigu and sent armed units marching to within about 200 kilometers of Moscow.
How it was handled · In a June 24 address, Putin branded the mutiny "treason," but the same day, through President Lukashenko's mediation, he agreed that Prigozhin would move to Belarus, that participants would be spared prosecution, and that fighters who had not joined the mutiny could sign contracts with the Defense Ministry; he did not dismiss Shoigu at the time. On August 23 the plane carrying Prigozhin crashed; the next day Putin described him as "a talented man" who had "made serious mistakes in life," and on May 12 of the following year Shoigu was moved from defense minister to secretary of the Security Council.
June 2026After the Anchorage summit in August 2025, pro-war bloggers called a meeting on U.S. soil a "humiliation" and argued that any exchange of territory would violate the constitution. After Ukrainian drones struck a Moscow oil refinery in June 2026, the nationalist businessman Konstantin Malofeyev asked why nuclear weapons were not being used, and bloggers demanded an end to talks with Washington and general mobilization.
How it was handled · According to a Reuters analysis, Putin neither broke off talks with Washington nor publicly endorsed nuclear use or general mobilization; the response went no further than the Defense Ministry publishing the addresses of sites it identified as European drone factories and the Foreign Ministry warning of systematic strikes on Kyiv. On September 5, 2026, too, he agreed only to a 72-hour halt to strikes on capitals and conceded nothing on core issues such as territory. He appears to have managed the hard-liners by continuing to negotiate while withholding the concessions they object to.

Room to maneuver

In ending the war in Ukraine, his room for concession appears narrowest on the legal status of the four annexed regions. Support for ending the war immediately stands at 80 percent but falls to 33 percent if that requires returning annexed territory (Levada Center, September 2025), and since hard-liners spoke of "humiliation" over the Anchorage summit alone, giving territory back would likely carry a heavy cost with both the elite and the public. By contrast, a deal that combines a freeze of the front in Zaporizhzhia and Kherson, partial or temporary truces, and sanctions relief tied to economic cooperation appears to leave relatively wide room, since it fits public support for negotiations (59 percent in August 2026) and the interests of the economic elite. In the hard-line direction, there is wide room for intensifying long-range strikes (59 percent favored more strikes if talks fail, January 2026), but even under hard-liner pressure in June 2026 he did not choose another mobilization or nuclear use, given the backlash against the 2022 mobilization and the risk of direct conflict with NATO. On the Korean Peninsula and Iran, no clear demands from his domestic coalition have been identified, so his latitude appears to depend more on external calculations than on domestic ones.

Words before and after a change of office

The gap between what he said before and after taking power runs in the opposite direction from the usual case. In a BBC interview on March 5, 2000, as acting president and a candidate, he said of Russian membership in NATO, "Why not? Why not?" and did not rule out the possibility "in the case that Russia's interests will be reckoned with, if it will be an equal partner." In a speech to the German Bundestag on September 25, 2001, he declared that "the Cold War is done with" and presented Russia as "a friendly European nation." From his 2007 Munich speech onward, however, he cast NATO expansion as "a serious provocation," and in 2021–2022 he made Ukraine's approach to NATO the justification for invasion. Because his rhetoric hardened while he held the same office, the gap did not arise from a change of position. The condition of being "an equal partner" in 2000 and the 2001 complaint that "decisions are often taken, in principle, without our participation, and we are only urged afterwards to support such decisions" were already there, so what changed most appears to be not the substance of his demand (an equal status that takes Russia's interests into account) but his judgment of whether that condition was being met, and his tone. That his early audiences were Western (the BBC, the German parliament) and that his support coalition tilted toward the security services from the mid-2000s are also candidate explanations, but no material directly supporting them was found.

Earlier leaders who faced the same structure

On August 31, 1996, Security Council Secretary Alexander Lebed signed the Khasavyurt11 Accord with Chechnya's Aslan Maskhadov, postponing a decision on Chechnya's status until the end of 2001; President Yeltsin reportedly approved it through Prime Minister Viktor Chernomyrdin without making any public statement. In August 1991, the heads of the security services and the military, including KGB Chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov and Defense Minister Dmitry Yazov, placed Gorbachev under house arrest at his dacha in Crimea and tried to seize power, but failed within three days.

Checked against theory

Bruce Bueno de Mesquita, Alastair Smith, Randolph Siverson, and James Morrow · The Logic of Political Survival (MIT Press, 2003) Conditional

Interpretation
Leaders survive by satisfying the winning coalition that sustains them within the nominal selectorate12. When the winning coalition is small and the selectorate large, leaders hand out private benefits to coalition members rather than public goods, and members, fearing they will be pushed out of the coalition, become more loyal, so leaders tend to hold on for a long time even when their policies fail.
Comparison
The structure — a small coalition of the security services, the military command, and the economic elite sitting atop a large nominal group, the whole electorate — and the facts that his hold on power did not waver after the 2022 retreats from Kharkiv and Kherson or the 2023 mutiny, and that the coalition did not crumble when he replaced Shoigu's network and the Defense Ministry leadership in 2024, fit the theory's loyalty norm. But the biggest threat in 2023 came not from the public but from inside the coalition (Wagner), and the backlash against the 2022 mobilization — a public cost to which the leader of a small coalition should be relatively insensitive — appears to have kept him from mobilizing again, so the public's effect on his latitude is likely larger than the theory predicts. Clarke and Stone have criticized the book, arguing that its statistical findings on war do not hold once democracy is controlled for.

Jessica Weeks · Autocratic Audience Costs: Regime Type and Signaling Resolve (International Organization 62(1), 2008) Fits

Interpretation
She argued that authoritarian leaders, too, can incur audience costs, but only if domestic elites have the ability and incentive to coordinate to punish the leader, if a backdown is observable, and if the audience views backing down unfavorably. She held that audience costs are large in single-party and military regimes and small in personalist dictatorships, where the leader controls the security services and can punish dissent.
Comparison
When his stated red lines were crossed — "This is not a bluff" (September 21, 2022) and permission for long-range strikes as NATO's entry into the war (September 12, 2024) — Putin did not use the threatened means, yet he was not punished at home. Hard-liners called the retreats and summits a "humiliation," but Girkin, who targeted Putin directly, was jailed, and the Wagner mutiny, the only organized challenge, was contained within a day and its leader died two months later. This comes close to the conditions of a personalist dictatorship, in which the channels for elites to coordinate punishment are blocked. His public warnings should therefore be read as weak signals not backed by domestic costs, which also fits the earlier observation, in "Where his words and decisions diverge," that his warnings were larger than his actual responses.

How he decides: hypotheses and forecasts

Patterns drawn from the decision record. Each hypothesis carries a forecast that the next decision can confirm or refute.

1. Putin will not accept a full ceasefire along the current line of contact until Russia controls all of the Donetsk region (or Ukraine agrees to withdraw from it); he offers only partial or temporary truces as bargaining tools.

Evidence and counterexamples
Supporting: March 18, 2025 (accepted only a 30-day halt to strikes on energy facilities); May 11, 2025 (counterproposed talks instead of a ceasefire); August 15, 2025 (demanded withdrawal from Donetsk and Luhansk without a ceasefire); September 5, 2026 (accepted only a 72-hour halt to strikes on capitals). Against: on August 15, 2025, he reportedly softened his demands on Zaporizhzhia and Kherson, so his territorial demands are not fixed, and he has pulled back when the battlefield turned against him, as in the withdrawal from the Kyiv axis in March 2022.
What would confirm or refute it
Holds if, from October on, only partial truces (covering energy or the air, for example) are agreed at the trilateral talks in the UAE or elsewhere, or if Russia keeps demanding withdrawal from Donetsk as a condition for a ceasefire. Fails if Russia signs a full ceasefire along the current line of contact while Ukrainian forces still hold part of the Donetsk region.
85%By December 31, 2026, no ceasefire covering the entire front (lasting 30 days or more, or open-ended) and officially confirmed by both the Russian and Ukrainian governments will have taken effect

2. When the West crosses one of his stated red lines, Putin responds not with direct hostilities against NATO members but with calibrated, asymmetric moves: demonstrations of new weapons, doctrinal revisions, and gray-zone incursions.

Evidence and counterexamples
Supporting: November 21, 2024 (he had called permission for long-range strikes "war," but responded only with a single Oreshnik strike and a doctrinal revision); no nuclear use after Ukraine's August 2024 entry into Kursk, despite his "all available means" warning of September 21, 2022; and the drone incursion into Polish airspace on September 10, 2025 (Russia denied intent).
