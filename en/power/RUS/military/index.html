Power Structures · Armed forces

The armed forces of Russia

Command · Capabilities · Deployment · Posture changes · As of October 8, 2026

← Power Structures · RussiaDecision-maker · Vladimir PutinRussia's Security Council and Federal AssemblyCountry profile · Russia

Russia's armed forces answer to President Vladimir Putin, the commander-in-chief under the constitution, who alone decides on the use of nuclear weapons. Four presidential decrees in 2026 alone have raised the military's authorized strength, setting the number of service members at 1,550,500. Estonia's Foreign Intelligence Service estimates that Russia produced more than 7 million artillery shells, mortar rounds and rockets in 2025, and according to Ukrainian Air Force data, Russia launched 8,161 Shahed-type drones, including decoys, in May 2026 alone. The bulk of the force is tied down on the Ukrainian front. Along NATO's borders, Moscow is laying the groundwork for the period after a ceasefire by reestablishing the Leningrad Military District and deploying the Oreshnik missile to Belarus.

Command and control

Under Article 87 of the constitution, the president is supreme commander-in-chief and directs the military through the defense minister and the chief of the General Staff. Using forces abroad requires the consent of the Federation Council (Article 102), but that check has proved hollow: on February 22, 2022, the chamber granted it unanimously within a day. Under the nuclear deterrence principles revised in November 2024, the president decides on nuclear use, and the National Guard, under his direct command, protects the regime separately from the regular military. Since the Wagner mutiny of June 2023, the military leadership has remained firmly under presidential control, and the defense ministry purge under way since 2024 appears to reflect that control at work rather than any pushback from the military.

Does the military follow the decision-maker?

Loyalty · judgment estimate
Loyal to the decision-maker · Collective defection unlikely About 5 percent (range 2–10 percent) that military or paramilitary forces break with the president by force within the next 12 months
  • No military-led change of government has occurred in Russia since 1991, and in the constitutional crisis of October 1993 the army sided with the president.
  • During the Wagner Group's armed mutiny on June 23–24, 2023, the regular military command did not join the revolt, which collapsed within a day (NPR, June 24, 2023). Still, the mutineers occupied central Rostov-on-Don and the area around the Southern Military District headquarters without resistance, and local law enforcement was reportedly ordered not to interfere with Wagner (Meduza, June 27, 2023).
  • Starting with the arrest of Deputy Defense Minister Timur Ivanov in April 2024, the ministry's senior ranks have been hit by a wave of corruption arrests, and in May 2024 Shoigu was replaced as defense minister by Andrei Belousov, a career economic official. Prominent cases include former deputy minister Timur Ivanov (arrested April 23, 2024; sentenced to 13 years in July 2025), former deputy minister Dmitry Bulgakov (arrested July 2024), former deputy minister Pavel Popov (arrested August 2024; sentenced to 19 years on April 10, 2026), and former first deputy minister Ruslan Tsalikov (arrested March 4, 2026) (Meduza, Kyiv Independent).
  • On September 30, 2026, Major General Sergei Nikitushkin, head of the training and logistics department of the defense ministry's Main Directorate of Combat Training, was reported to have been placed in pretrial detention (Charter97, October 1, 2026, citing an unnamed Telegram channel [Reported]).
  • The National Guard, established in 2016 under Federal Law No. 226-FZ and commanded directly by the president (Article 6), is a regime-protection force that counters armed domestic threats independently of the regular military (law as published in Rossiyskaya Gazeta).
  • Pointing the other way, ISW in October 2026 relayed a Russian Telegram channel's claim that at least 8,000 soldiers had deserted the Western Group of Forces' area of responsibility. Individual desertions may well rise, but there is no evidence that they have translated into collective defection by commanders.

Capabilities

Russia holds the world's largest nuclear arsenal and runs a war economy, with defense spending at 7.5 percent of GDP. Four presidential decrees in 2026 alone have raised the military's authorized strength, and mass production of artillery shells and long-range drones, together with North Korean shells and troops, gives Moscow the means to sustain a war of attrition. Since March 2026, however, that capacity has not produced significant gains on the front.

Proficiency

Proficiency · judgment estimate
Battle-hardened but weak at large-scale maneuver · Rapidly improving drone and long-range strike operations Moderate (likely low-to-mid range relative to major NATO militaries; medium confidence)
  • With full-scale war now in its fifth year, Russian commanders and troops rank among the most combat-experienced in the world.
  • ISW assessed on September 26, 2026, that Russian forces had advanced west of Kostiantynivka but had made almost no net gains across the front since March. Their ability to convert mass manpower and firepower into territory remains limited.
  • Ukraine's commander-in-chief claimed that 183,500 Russian troops were killed or seriously wounded between January 1 and late June 2026, while Russia mobilized roughly 180,500 over the same period (interview with The Times, via LIGA.net, June 26, 2026). That implies most new recruits go to replacing losses, leaving little room to build a trained reserve.
  • Russia has built a system for the mass use of long-range drones, launching 8,161 (including decoys, per Ukrainian Air Force data) in May 2026 alone, and its specialized drone unit, the Rubicon Center, develops new tactics and spreads them across the military (ISIS, FPRI).
  • The Russian defense ministry said its May 19–21, 2026, nuclear forces exercise involved more than 200 launchers, more than 140 aircraft, and 13 submarines, eight of them strategic missile submarines. Strategic nuclear exercises continue on a regular schedule.
  • In October 2023, British defence intelligence assessed that the Black Sea Fleet was likely to move a larger share of its forces from Sevastopol to Novorossiysk to escape Ukrainian drone and missile strikes, exposing the vulnerability of the navy's surface ships.

Deployment

The Russian military's center of gravity is the Ukrainian front. Ukraine estimated in June 2026 that 721,300 Russian troops were inside Ukrainian territory. Facing NATO, Russia reestablished the Leningrad and Moscow military districts on February 26, 2024, and is building a new base in Karelia near the Finnish border, but Lithuanian military intelligence and a former Finnish intelligence officer (Yle) expect any large-scale buildup to come only after the war in Ukraine ends or is frozen. Abroad, Russia's footprint is shrinking: Moscow has agreed to convert its Syrian bases into joint training centers and has pulled out of northern Mali.
