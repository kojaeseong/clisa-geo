The security vacuum the Korean Peninsula can expect in the 2030s

October 1, 2026 · Clisa Geopolitics

Summary

  • Around 2028–2030, the United States will hand roles over to South Korea through the transfer of wartime operational control, a realignment of U.S. Forces Korea, and a shift of primary responsibility for deterring North Korea.
  • Offsetting a shrinking force with career soldiers and unmanned systems, and acquiring the means to counter North Korea's nuclear weapons, will not come to fruition until around 2040. In between, South Korea will be in a security vacuum, having taken on the roles without yet having the means.
  • In this period, South Korea is likely to be pushed toward becoming an "armed middle power," with fewer troops, a growing defense budget, and mounting latent nuclear capability.
  • The paths that would widen the vacuum are a U.S.-North Korean deal that leaves out South Korea and moves by South Korea and Japan toward nuclear armament. The joint communiqué of the November 2026 SCM is the first test.

This essay assumes that the trends that will shape the Korean Peninsula's future continue on their present course, and it sets out the configuration they form as they overlap and the events likely to occur within it. Where the scenarios for the Korean Peninsula issue address which futures could arrive, this essay addresses when the risks grow.

Trends pointing in one direction

  • Transfer of wartime operational control (OPCON)1 · The Security Consultative Meeting (SCM) in November 2026 will take up verification of the Future Combined Forces Command's full operational capability (FOC)2 and a target year for the transfer. The South Korean government has floated 2027–2028 and the commander of U.S. Forces Korea the first quarter of 2029, so the timing of the transfer is converging on 2028–2030 OPCON transfer completed by 2030 50%.
  • China's rise · China has set as its national goals "the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation" and becoming a "great modern socialist country" by the middle of the century. After Xi Jinping's visit to Pyongyang in June 2026, denuclearization was missing from both sides' statements, and Beijing has in effect tilted toward tacit acceptance of North Korea's nuclear weapons.
  • Public opinion in each country · In South Korea, majorities in several polls favor an independent nuclear arsenal, and young people's interest in unification is waning. In the United States, support for defending allies splits sharply along partisan lines, and the administration makes cost sharing a condition of alliance. In China, a 2025 Tsinghua University survey found that 79.2 percent of respondents expected China's international standing to rise over the next decade.
  • North Korea's nuclear weapons · The number of warheads is growing, and the delivery systems for tactical nuclear weapons aimed at South Korea and Japan are effectively in place. Reentry technology for intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) that could reach the U.S. mainland, however, has not yet been demonstrated. North Korea's nuclear weapons are pointed at South Korea, while its ICBMs serve as a shield against U.S. intervention.
  • U.S. policy and military posture · The 2026 U.S. National Defense Strategy says South Korea is capable of taking primary responsibility for deterring North Korea with "critical but more limited" U.S. support. The National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA)3 for fiscal year 2026 restored the 28,500-troop floor for U.S. Forces Korea (USFK) but allows reductions if the secretary of defense certifies them, and the 2026 Ulchi Freedom Shield combined exercise was cut to five days USFK falls below 28,500 35%.
  • A shrinking pool of young people · The number of 20-year-old men will fall from about 240,000 in 2024 to about 130,000 in 2045. The number of conscripted soldiers could drop to around 150,000 by 2040, and even with officers and NCOs included, total troop strength is projected to fall short of a quarter of North Korea's estimated forces. Defense spending will rise to about 3.5 percent of GDP, but the population that must sustain it is shrinking as well Military service overhaul formally pursued 25%.

The gap between handing over and filling in

Taken together, these trends point to one core problem: a difference in speed. The U.S. handover of roles—the transfer of wartime operational control, the realignment of U.S. Forces Korea, and the shift of primary responsibility—is concentrated around 2028–2030. South Korea's effort to fill those roles—offsetting a shrinking force with career soldiers and unmanned systems, and acquiring the means to counter North Korea's nuclear weapons—is aimed at 2040 even in the Ministry of National Defense's own plans. In between, from around 2028 and throughout the 2030s, South Korea will have taken on the roles without yet having the means to carry them out. Clisa Geopolitics calls this stretch a "security vacuum." It may be the longest period during which the U.S. defense commitment looks shaky. Because the Mutual Defense Treaty and U.S. Forces Korea remain in place, the situation differs from 1950, when the United States publicly left South Korea outside its defense perimeter. But a transition in which roles are being handed over is when North Korea has the most room to discount Washington's willingness to intervene.

In this period, three things are likely. The U.S.-South Korean alliance will endure but become a division of labor in which the United States backstops from behind; North Korea, China, and Russia, though not formal allies, will band together and shield one another; and South Korea will be pushed toward becoming an armed middle power4·5, with fewer troops, a growing defense budget, and mounting latent nuclear capability.

Paths to add to the scenarios

Among the scenarios for the Korean Peninsula issue, a deal without South Korea (F5) and nuclear dominoes (F3) are paths along which this vacuum widens, and sudden upheaval (F4) is a path that would upend every calculation. To these, this essay adds the following paths.

  • The alliance closes ranks · A crisis in the Taiwan Strait or a major North Korean provocation draws U.S. attention back to the Korean Peninsula, extended deterrence6 becomes institutionalized, and the vacuum is filled.
  • Two fronts in a Taiwan contingency · As a crisis unfolds in the Taiwan Strait, North Korea simultaneously raises tensions on the Korean Peninsula, pinning down South Korean forces and U.S. Forces Korea on the peninsula. South Korea is caught between its ally's demands and North Korea's nuclear threat The Taiwan Strait.

Signals that this assessment is wrong

If any of the following is confirmed, the assessment of a security vacuum is wrong or has been weakened.

  • An SCM joint communiqué includes both a commitment to maintain the size of U.S. Forces Korea and joint planning for nuclear operations.
  • The agreement on the OPCON transfer also institutionalizes extended deterrence.
  • Unmanned systems to offset the shrinking force are deployed faster than planned.
  • U.S.-North Korean negotiations cover short-range missiles, and South Korea takes part as a party.

Key terms

  1. Wartime operational control (OPCON) · The authority to command South Korean military units and conduct operations in the event of war. Since 1978 it has been exercised by the ROK-U.S. Combined Forces Command (CFC), whose commander is a U.S. four-star general (peacetime operational control passed to the South Korean military in 1994). Seoul and Washington agreed on the transfer in 2007, but it was postponed once in 2010, and in 2014 the approach shifted from a fixed date to a transfer once set conditions are met. ↩
  2. Full operational capability of the Future Combined Forces Command · The Future Combined Forces Command is the new combined command that, after the OPCON transfer, will be led by a South Korean four-star general, with a U.S. four-star general as deputy commander. Before the transfer, whether it works properly is verified in three stages—initial operational capability (IOC), full operational capability (FOC), and full mission capability (FMC). Full operational capability is the second of these. ↩
  3. National Defense Authorization Act · The basic law on defense policy and budgets that the U.S. Congress passes every year. For several years it has included a provision barring the use of funds to reduce U.S. Forces Korea below a set level, with an exception if the secretary of defense certifies to Congress that certain conditions are met, such as that the reduction is in the U.S. national security interest. ↩
  4. Middle power · A country that is not a great power but has enough economic, military, and diplomatic weight to play a meaningful role in its region and on the international stage. Australia, Canada, and South Korea are commonly cited; in 2013, South Korea joined Mexico, Indonesia, Turkey, and Australia in founding MIKTA, a consultative group of middle powers. Middle powers are often characterized by mediation between great powers, multilateral diplomacy, and security that relies on alliances. ↩
  5. Armed middle power · A term used in this essay. Unlike middle powers that entrusted their security to alliances and expanded their role through diplomacy and economics, it describes a country that, as the United States hands over primary responsibility for deterring North Korea, raises its defense spending, builds up latent nuclear capability (the technology and materials to build nuclear weapons quickly), and arms itself to hold its ground. It is an in-between state: neither becoming a great power nor relying on an alliance as before. ↩
  6. Extended deterrence · A U.S. commitment to treat an attack on an ally as an attack on itself and to deter it with all its military power, including nuclear weapons. It is often called the "nuclear umbrella." Seoul and Washington consult on carrying out this commitment through the Extended Deterrence Strategy and Consultation Group (EDSCG) and the Nuclear Consultative Group (NCG), created by the 2023 Washington Declaration. ↩
