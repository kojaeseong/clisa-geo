Trump's last two years: a president in a hurry, strongmen who can wait

Summary

  1. Having lost both the House and the Senate in the midterms, President Trump is a lame duck in the eyes of Xi Jinping, Vladimir Putin and Kim Jong Un. They will deal with him only when they can claim a big win for their own countries.
  2. For Trump, by contrast, foreign policy, where he still has wide latitude, becomes his biggest stage for the two years he has left. He is the one racing the clock, and the strongmen will come to believe that the longer they wait, the more he will give.
  3. The deals both sides can agree on are ones the strongmen can sell as major U.S. concessions. Allies such as South Korea need to prepare so that the cost or risk of such deals does not end up falling on them instead.

How the strongmen see Trump: a lame duck

President Trump's term ends on January 20, 2029, and he cannot run again. The November 3 midterms put Democrats in a position to control the budget and the legislative agenda in both the House and the Senate starting in January 2027. For the rest of his term, Congress can be expected to check his power.

His camp's chances of keeping the White House have also fallen. Since 1958, the president's party has lost a second-term midterm election five times (1958, 1974, 1986, 2006 and 2014), and it kept the White House in the next presidential election only once, in 1988, when Republican George H. W. Bush succeeded Ronald Reagan.

The strongmen's burden: legitimacy

Strongmen who rule more or less for life rest part of their claim to power on the idea that their systems outperform democracies, which change course with every election. Being seen actively building something with a counterpart who leaves office in two years and is constrained by Congress undercuts that claim.

So even when they meet President Trump, the strongmen will agree only to deals they can present as major gains for their own countries. In November 2014, eight days after Obama's party lost the Senate, Xi Jinping hosted President Obama in Beijing, where the two announced a climate agreement. Under that agreement, however, China could keep increasing its carbon emissions until around 2030.

Trump's calculation: diplomacy as his final stage

A president who has lost Congress can decide few things on his own, but as head of state and commander in chief he has wide latitude over diplomacy, including summits, and over military action. If he delivers foreign-policy achievements that Americans give him credit for, he can serve out his last two years on firmer footing and give a lift to whoever carries his camp's banner in 2028. Trump is now short on time, and he needs results.

Where the two sides can agree

For the two sides to strike a deal, Trump has to be able to claim that he gave up a little and won a lasting peace, and the strongmen have to be able to claim that they protected their core interests and secured U.S. commitments. The late-term diplomacy of past U.S. presidents offers precedents for such deals.

  • Bush and North Korea (2007–2008). Hit in quick succession by North Korea's nuclear test in October 2006 and Republican losses in the midterm elections that November, the Bush administration arranged for $25 million in North Korean funds frozen at Macau's Banco Delta Asia to be transferred to North Korea in 2007, as Pyongyang had demanded, and in October 2008 removed North Korea from the list of state sponsors of terrorism. The Bush administration claimed progress on denuclearization; North Korea claimed that the United States had caved. Talks on verification broke down in December 2008.
  • Obama and Cuba (2014). After secret talks with Cuba that had begun in 2013, the Obama administration announced on December 17, shortly after Democrats lost the Senate, that it would move to restore diplomatic relations. President Obama said he was ending a failed policy of isolation, and Cuba, without changing its system, won the release of its intelligence agents held in U.S. prisons.
  • Obama and Iran (2015). The United States said the Iran nuclear deal had cut off Iran's paths to a nuclear weapon, and Iran's leaders said they had preserved their right to enrich uranium. Three years later, the next president pulled out of the deal.
  • Clinton and North Korea (2000). In his final year in office, Clinton pushed to conclude missile talks, and North Korea invited him to Pyongyang. On December 28, he decided against the trip, saying there was "not enough time."

These cases share a pattern. The U.S. president was racing the clock, and where a deal was struck, the other side could say it had kept what it considered essential and that the United States had backed down.

A president in a hurry, strongmen who can wait

In a negotiation, the side facing a deadline gives up more. President Trump faces a deadline in January 2029; the strongmen face no such limit on their tenure. They may, however, face an economic deadline.

President Reagan was already something of a lame duck when he signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty in 1987. His party had lost the Senate in the 1986 midterms, and the Iran-Contra affair had driven his approval rating from 63 percent down to 47 percent. Yet it was the Soviet Union that gave up more. On top of its economic troubles, Moscow was saddled with the cleanup from the April 26, 1986, explosion at the Chernobyl nuclear plant and a loss of faith in the Soviet system, and it needed to cut military spending. Eighteen days after the accident, General Secretary Mikhail Gorbachev proposed a meeting with Reagan on a nuclear test ban. The side under greater pressure gave up more.

The strongmen are not free of pressure either. Iran and Russia in particular are under fiscal strain, which could bring them to the table before Xi Jinping or Kim Jong Un. The possibility that the next U.S. president, who takes office in 2029, will take a harder line is another deadline for them. But with only two years to show results, Trump is likely to pursue diplomacy that is more transactional than ever and breaks with convention. So the strongmen will wait for better offers, while trying to lock them in before 2029.

What it means for allies such as South Korea

The cost or risk of deals President Trump rushes into could fall on U.S. allies. If a U.S.-North Korea summit is held without South Korea at the table, any resulting agreement is likely to leave out provisions on inter-Korean relations or short- and medium-range missiles (Clisa Geopolitics puts the probability at 75 percent, assuming such a summit takes place) No inter-Korean provisions in a U.S.-North Korean deal 75%. The new Congress, however, can block concessions in which the law gives it a say, such as lifting sanctions on Russia or North Korea. What Trump can give away on his own is therefore likely to be limited to concessions that need no congressional approval.

So allies will have little choice but to work every channel in Washington to protect their interests before the terms of any deal are set. A president who prizes deals is hard to sway through opposition alone. The more realistic course for allies would be to offer him something he can tout as a win and, in return, get their core interests written into the terms of the deal. Allies also need to deepen cooperation among themselves, so that it is harder for Washington to trade away one ally's interests in a deal with someone else.

Signs this assessment is wrong

  • The November 19 summit in Shenzhen or another meeting produces a concrete written agreement with Xi Jinping or Putin without new U.S. concessions
  • President Trump stays the course in 2027 without putting new deals on the table
  • North Korea agrees to a summit without U.S. concessions
