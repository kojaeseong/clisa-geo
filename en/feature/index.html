South Koreans' well-being hangs by a thread

Summary

  1. Even in the best-case scenario, South Koreans' well-being index will be hard to keep at its current level, and geopolitical shifts could send it sharply lower.
  2. The geopolitical shifts of this period (the transfer of primary responsibility for deterring North Korea from U.S. to South Korean forces, the transfer of wartime operational control, China's rise and growing influence in East Asia, a Taiwan Strait crisis, and the de facto formalization of North Korea's nuclear status) are converging, and South Korea urgently needs to examine them carefully and prepare its response.

South Koreans' well-being index

The Clisa Geopolitics well-being index is a modified version of the UN Development Programme's (UNDP) Human Development Index1. It scores South Koreans' lives on two dimensions, safety and living conditions, each from 0 to 100, and combines the two into a single score the same way the Human Development Index does, by taking their geometric mean. Safety is calculated from life expectancy and exposure to war and state violence; living conditions from per capita income, the dependency burden, the old-age poverty rate, and the youth employment rate, with the worst-performing OECD member set at 0 and the best at 100. Unlike the World Happiness Report2 (published by the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network and others), which asks respondents how satisfied they are with their lives, the index is built from measurable living conditions, so projected changes in population and the security environment can be built into it.

South Koreans' well-being index currently stands at 58.1 (safety 71.1, living conditions 47.5). Weighted by the probabilities of the scenarios the peninsula could follow through 2030, the index falls to 54.5 in 2030, 41.0 in 2040, and 20.9 in 2050. In no scenario does the 2030 index exceed today's level, and even if the best scenario, in which inter-Korean tensions ease, holds, the index reaches only 24.2 in 2050. Safety varies widely from one scenario to another, but living conditions deteriorate in every scenario as the working-age3 population shrinks and the number of older people grows.

These calculations assume that old-age poverty and youth employment stay at current levels. Even in the best case, with old-age poverty falling to the OECD median, youth employment rising to the OECD median, and people aged 65 to 69 continuing to work, living conditions score 49.5 in 2050, below today's score on the same basis (about 61, with 65- to 69-year-olds counted as working age). Even if 65- to 69-year-olds work, the number of people supported by every 100 working-age people more than doubles, from about 29 today to 68.5 in 2050. On the well-being index, the best case yields 59.5 in 2050, short of today's 65.9 on the same basis. With the current level hard to hold even in the best case, the geopolitical shifts described below come on top.

Geopolitical shifts converging around 2030

The United States is handing its role on the Korean Peninsula over to South Korea. The 2026 U.S. National Defense Strategy states that South Korea is capable of taking primary responsibility for deterring North Korea4 with "critical but more limited" U.S. support. With the South Korean government citing 2027–2028 and the commander of U.S. Forces Korea the first quarter of 2029, expectations for the transfer of wartime operational control5 are converging on 2028–2030 OPCON transfer completed by 2030 50%. The National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2026 sets a floor of 28,500 U.S. troops in Korea but allows a reduction if the secretary of defense certifies it USFK falls below 28,500 35%.

North Korea's nuclear status has, in effect, been formalized. In September 2023, Pyongyang wrote its policy of strengthening its nuclear forces into its constitution, and it has been adding warheads and has effectively fielded the means to deliver tactical nuclear weapons against South Korea and Japan. After Chinese President Xi Jinping's visit to Pyongyang in June 2026, denuclearization was absent from both sides' statements, a sign that Beijing has tilted toward tacitly accepting North Korea's nuclear weapons. China has made "the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation" its national goal and is extending its influence in East Asia.

The Taiwan Strait is a risk that bears directly on South Korea's economy and security. Clisa Geopolitics puts the likelihood of a Chinese invasion of Taiwan and U.S. intervention by 2030 at 2.5 percent The Taiwan Strait. The likelihood is low, but if it happened, South Korea's trade with China, its shipping, and its semiconductor supply chains would be cut off even if South Korea itself were not a battlefield. Applying Bloomberg Economics' estimates, South Korea's per capita income would fall by about a quarter in the first year. If North Korea raised tensions on the peninsula during a crisis over Taiwan, both U.S. Forces Korea and the South Korean military might have to devote themselves entirely to defending the peninsula.

These shifts threaten a well-being index that is precarious to begin with. In 2030, the index ranges from 52.3 to 57.7 in the scenarios without war, but it falls to 50.3 if a limited clash breaks out and to 42.6 in a full-scale war on the peninsula. Clisa Geopolitics puts the likelihood of a full-scale war on the peninsula by 2030 at 2 percent. The likelihood is low, but if it happened, applying Bloomberg Economics' estimates, income would fall to two-thirds of its no-war level and, even after recovering to its prewar level, would remain about 12 percent below the no-war path as late as 2050.

A shrinking military around 2040

The number of 20-year-old men will fall from about 240,000 in 2024 to about 140,000 in 2040 and about 130,000 in 2045. Those who turn 20 in 2040 were born in 2020 and are about six years old today. According to material the Ministry of National Defense submitted to the National Assembly in September 2025, if the current system is maintained, the armed forces will shrink from about 450,000 in July 2025 (250,000 enlisted soldiers and 200,000 officers and NCOs6) to about 270,000 in 2040 (150,000 enlisted soldiers and 120,000 officers and NCOs). North Korea's military is estimated at about 1.28 million (2022 Defense White Paper).

Keeping enlisted strength at 250,000 by lengthening service would require, assuming about 69 percent of 20-year-old men enlist as they do now, about 31 months of army service in 2040 and about 33 months in 2045, nearly double the current 18 months. Even then, the decline in officers and NCOs would leave total strength below a third of North Korea's, and young men on the verge of starting careers and families would serve more than a year longer. Longer service cannot make up for the shrinking force Military service overhaul formally pursued 25%.

In its June 2026 force-restructuring plan, the Ministry of National Defense said it would keep defense manpower, meaning active-duty troops plus military civilian employees and standing reservists, at 500,000 in 2040, raise the share of officers and NCOs (career soldiers) among active-duty personnel to 63 percent, and expand drone forces to about 30 times their current size. Unlike the figure of 270,000 above (active duty only), the 500,000 includes military civilian employees and standing reservists. The plan works only if the number of career soldiers rises sharply, yet the army's NCO fill rate fell from 95 percent in 2020 to 42 percent in 2024 (data submitted to the National Assembly's National Defense Committee). Career soldiers, too, must be drawn from the same population of young people. How much of the shortfall around 2040 can be made up depends on how far unmanned systems can substitute for troops.

North Korea's total fertility rate is 1.78 (2024, UN estimate), and about 340,000 to 350,000 babies are born there each year. Projected from births, North Korea will match or overtake South Korea in the number of people in their 20s around 2043, at 3.46 million against 3.3 million (the North Korean figures are UN estimates with wide margins of error). South Korea's military is already only a little over a third the size of North Korea's, so this crossover does not open a new gap, but under the current system the troop ratio widens from about 1 to 2.8 today to about 1 to 4.7 in 2040. That said, the North Korean figure has been the same estimate for years and the North's equipment is aging, so the real gap in military strength is smaller than the troop ratio suggests.

Because of the U.S.-South Korean alliance and U.S. Forces Korea, a shrinking military does not translate directly into a gap in the country's defenses. But as noted above, troop numbers are falling just as South Korea's share of deterring North Korea grows, and without fundamental measures a defense gap will be hard to avoid.

Fewer workers and more older people through 2050

Statistics Korea (now the Ministry of Data and Statistics) publishes several population projections under differing assumptions about fertility, life expectancy, and migration; its baseline is the medium variant7. Actual births (254,000) and the total fertility rate (0.80) in 2025 came closer to the high variant, which assumes the highest fertility, so the table below, which shows the number of children and older people supported by every 100 working-age people (ages 15 to 64), uses the medium variant as its baseline and also gives the high-variant totals. Raising fertility alone to the high-variant level puts the 2050 burden at 94.4, almost the same as the high variant.

The working-age population shrinks 32 percent, from 35.91 million in 2025 to 24.45 million in 2050; the population aged 65 and over grows 80 percent, from 10.51 million to 18.91 million; and the population aged 0 to 14 falls from 5.26 million to 3.75 million (medium variant). What sets South Korea apart is the speed of the change and old-age income. The increase in the burden from 2025 to 2050 (46.6) is the largest in the OECD, eight more than the next-largest, Spain's (38.6). The poverty rate among people aged 66 and over (39.8 percent) is the highest in the OECD, double Japan's and triple those of Italy and Spain. If that rate holds, about 7.5 million of the 18.91 million people aged 65 and over in 2050 will be poor. The rate may come down somewhat as generations with longer National Pension contribution records reach old age. Per capita income at purchasing power parity8 rises from $64,210 in 2025 to $82,195 in 2050 (applying the Korea Development Institute's potential growth projections), but the number of people each working-age person supports doubles.

The limits of a fertility rebound

The total fertility rate fell below the replacement level (2.1) in 1983, to 2.06, and kept falling to 0.72 in 2023 before rebounding to 0.75 in 2024 and 0.80 in 2025.

But the older people of 2050 have all been born already. The rebound cannot change the old-age burden through 2050, and by adding children it actually enlarges the dependent population slightly. Even if the rebound continues, the burden falls below the medium variant only in the late 2050s, and even then it merely grows more slowly rather than shrinking (109 in 2065). Where the rebound pays off before 2050 is the young adult population: children born from 2026 on begin turning 20 in 2046, adding to the pool of workers and conscripts.

Conclusion

Around 2030, the transfer of primary responsibility for deterring North Korea, the transfer of wartime operational control, China's rise and growing influence, the risks in the Taiwan Strait, and the de facto formalization of North Korea's nuclear status are expected to arrive at once. Over the same period, falling births will shrink the military, and by 2050 the working-age population will make up only a little over half of the total. Even in the best case, South Koreans' well-being index will be hard to keep at its current level, and a single geopolitical shock could send it sharply lower.

As South Korea's share within the alliance grows, Seoul needs to secure the U.S. commitment to extended deterrence9 in more clearly institutionalized form. The Ministry of National Defense's plan to offset a shrinking force with career soldiers and unmanned systems faces problems that must be solved, as the difficulty of recruiting officers and NCOs shows. South Korea also needs to prepare in advance for how it would handle both the defense of the peninsula and the alliance's demands during a Taiwan Strait crisis. South Korea is far ahead of North Korea economically and technologically, so if this review and response come in time, technology and the alliance can make up for the decline in troop numbers.

Paths to add to the scenarios

Adding demographic change to the Korean Peninsula scenarios means the following paths also need to be watched The Korean Peninsula.

  • A prolonged manpower gap · NCO recruitment shortfalls persist and unmanned systems arrive late, pushing back the Ministry of National Defense's 2040 targets. If the manpower gap extends into the 2040s, public support for South Korea's own nuclear weapons could grow South Korea or Japan formally goes nuclear 20%.
  • A locked-in division of labor · U.S. Forces Korea is reorganized around air power, missile defense, and reconnaissance, and the South Korean military takes on nearly all of ground defense USFK falls below 28,500 35%. South Korea's share would be largest just when its ground forces shrink the most.
  • The shock of war · A full-scale war breaks out on the Korean Peninsula or in the Taiwan Strait. The likelihood is low, but if it happened, applying Bloomberg Economics' estimates, per capita income would fall by roughly a quarter to a third in the first year and would not regain its no-war level even by 2050, pushing an already declining well-being index down once more The Taiwan Strait.

Signals that this assessment is wrong

If any of the following is confirmed, this essay's assessment is wrong or has been weakened.

  • The army's NCO fill rate climbs back to 80 percent or higher.
  • A new Statistics Korea population projection raises the working-age share of the population in 2050 to 60 percent or more in the medium variant.
  • A Security Consultative Meeting joint communiqué or an agreement on the transfer of wartime operational control includes both a commitment to maintain the size of U.S. Forces Korea and the institutionalization of extended deterrence.
  • North Korea returns to denuclearization talks and removes its nuclear force policy from its constitution.

Data and calculations

  • Population · Based on Statistics Korea's population projections (December 2023, medium and high variants). Working age follows the 15–64 definition of the working-age population used by Statistics Korea and the OECD. For cross-country comparisons, World Bank population projections are used to apply a common standard.
  • Income · Per capita income starts from the World Bank's per capita gross national income at purchasing power parity and applies the Korea Development Institute's (KDI) potential growth projections (1.5 percent a year through 2030, 0.7 percent in the 2030s, and 0.1 percent in the 2040s), adjusted for population change.
  • Old-age poverty · Poverty rates for people aged 66 and over from the OECD Income Distribution Database (2023; 2021 for Japan). With no official projections available, current levels are assumed to persist and are applied to the population aged 65 and over.
  • If people aged 65 to 69 work · The dependent and working-age populations are recalculated with ages 65 to 69 counted as working age.
  • Population in their 20s and troop strength · The population in their 20s is estimated from annual births; for North Korea, UN estimates and World Bank projections are used. Length of service is calculated for the army, assuming about 69 percent of 20-year-old men enlist, as they do now.
  • Well-being index · A modified index that adds other indicators to the life expectancy and income components of the UNDP's Human Development Index. Safety averages life expectancy with a 0–100 rating of exposure to war and state violence in each scenario (a judgment estimate by Clisa Geopolitics). Living conditions averages per capita income, the dependency burden, the poverty rate for people aged 66 and over, and the employment rate for people aged 15 to 29, each rescaled so that the worst-performing OECD member scores 0 and the best 100. The well-being index is the geometric mean of the two scores, the same method as the Human Development Index. Values for 2040 and 2050 assume the 2030 scenario persists. Because the best case counts ages 65 to 69 as working age, it is compared with the current score on the same basis.
  • Scenario probabilities · The 2030 scenario probabilities are taken from the Korean Peninsula issue in Strategic Analysis. In this essay, inter-Korean détente (13 percent) corresponds to managed coexistence and North Korean regime transition in Strategic Analysis; continued standoff (63 percent) to armed stalemate, a deal without South Korea, and a North Korean leadership succession that leaves the regime intact; and nuclear arms race (19 percent) to nuclear dominoes. Sudden upheaval (10 percent) is split into full-scale war on the peninsula (2 percent), limited clash (3 percent), leadership succession (2 percent), and North Korean regime transition (1 percent), with the remaining 2 percent added to armed stalemate. Where a case falls under more than one scenario, its score is the probability-weighted average of those scenarios' scores.
  • Security environment and war · Likelihoods through 2030 are Clisa Geopolitics estimates. The 2 percent probability of full-scale war on the peninsula takes into account that, since the armistice, the only inter-Korean clash with 100 or more deaths was the DMZ conflict of the late 1960s; the share of such clashes since 1945 that escalated into war (37 percent); and forecasts on prediction platforms. Income losses from full-scale war apply Bloomberg Economics' first-year estimates (37.5 percent for the Korean Peninsula, 23 percent for Taiwan) and assume that 10 percent of the first-year loss is recovered each year from the second year on.

Key terms

  1. Human Development Index · An index the UNDP has published every year since 1990 that combines life expectancy, education, and per capita income to measure each country's level of development. The Clisa Geopolitics well-being index borrows its methods for calculating life expectancy and income. ↩
  2. World Happiness Report · An annual report that ranks countries by the life satisfaction respondents assign themselves (0 to 10) in the Gallup World Poll. It is published jointly by the Wellbeing Research Centre at the University of Oxford, Gallup, and the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network. In the 2026 report, South Korea scored 6.040, ranking 67th of 147 countries. ↩
  3. Working age · Ages 15 to 64, which Statistics Korea and the OECD define as the working-age population. It is a statistical category that applies whether or not people actually work; this essay's "best case" assumes that people aged 65 to 69 also work. ↩
  4. Primary responsibility for deterring North Korea · A division of roles, set out in the 2026 U.S. National Defense Strategy, under which the South Korean military bears primary responsibility for deterring and repelling a North Korean attack and U.S. forces provide support. ↩
  5. Wartime operational control · The authority to command South Korean military units in operations if war breaks out. Since 1978 it has been exercised by the ROK-U.S. Combined Forces Command, headed by a U.S. four-star general. ↩
  6. Officers and NCOs · Career soldiers, namely commissioned officers and noncommissioned officers, as distinct from enlisted soldiers doing compulsory service. ↩
  7. Medium and high variants · Statistics Korea publishes several population projections assuming low, medium, and high levels of fertility, life expectancy, and international migration. The medium variant is the baseline projection calculated from the medium assumptions; the high variant uses the assumptions under which the population stays largest. ↩
  8. Purchasing power parity · A conversion that adjusts for differences in price levels across countries so that what the same amount of money actually buys can be compared. ↩
  9. Extended deterrence · The U.S. commitment to treat an attack on an ally as an attack on itself and to deter it with all its military capabilities, including nuclear weapons. It is often called the "nuclear umbrella." ↩
