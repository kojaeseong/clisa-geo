The Russia-Ukraine war: why the attrition goes on

Summary

  1. Even if the war of attrition continues, Russian society will look much as it does today, while Ukrainian society will be worse off.
  2. The side for which fighting on is cheaper has no reason to scale back its demands, so Putin can afford to keep up the war of attrition and wait.
  3. Ukraine's drone strikes and the strain on Russia's budget are unsettling that dynamic, but higher oil prices driven by the Iran war and Washington's easing of sanctions on Russia are cushioning their impact on Russia.

Same war, different costs

The People's Lives tab on Clisa Geopolitics plays out the years to 2030 a hundred times and shows, each time, whether people's lives would be better or worse than today1. For the Russia-Ukraine war, the result splits cleanly in two. In the 60 futures where the fighting stops, life improves for both peoples. In the 40 where the war goes on, life for Russians stays about the same while only Ukrainians are worse off. Thirty-three of those 40 are a war of attrition like today's The Russia-Ukraine War.

On the classification alone, the gap between the two countries is not large. If attrition continues, Ukraine is rated "worse" for one reason only: the share of people displaced from their homes rises from 23 percent to 24 percent. In fact, relative to population, more Russians die than Ukrainians. Violent deaths run at 62.1 per 100,000 people a year in Russia and 47.0 in Ukraine, and most of the Russian dead are soldiers at the front.

The real difference lies less in the size of the cost than in where it falls. In Russia, soldiers die but the rest of society is barely shaken; in Ukraine, the war is felt in communities and the economy, and in food, health and the environment as well.

The difference exists because the front lies entirely on Ukrainian soil. Russian missiles and drones target Ukraine's cities and power plants, and about a quarter of Ukrainians have been driven from their homes. Russia, by contrast, has loaded the cost of the war onto its soldiers and their families. As Robert Pape observed in his work on economic sanctions, those who hold power can shift pain onto the powerless2 Lecture 9.

Why Putin can afford to wait

The bargaining model of war shows why this matters. Because war is costly, there is a bargaining range of deals that both sides should prefer to fighting3 Lecture 5, and the side that pays less to keep fighting can demand a correspondingly larger share of the deal. Russia, able to keep the war's impact confined to the front, is in exactly that position.

Russia's behavior fits the model. Since the start of 2026, Moscow has repeatedly demanded that Ukrainian forces withdraw from all of Donetsk Oblast as a condition for a cease-fire. Putin has rejected proposals to stop the war along the current front line, and on October 16 he again called the "liberation" of Donetsk one of his main goals.

Public opinion narrows the bargaining range as well. Surveys by the Levada Center, an independent Russian pollster, show that most Russians want peace talks, but only a minority would accept talks that hand occupied land back. Most Ukrainians support a cease-fire as such, but they reject giving up land they still hold or legally recognizing the occupation, according to the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology (KIIS). In short, the deals the two publics would accept do not overlap.

An end to the fighting would clearly benefit Russians too, as the rise in their personal-safety score from 40 to about 65 shows. The war goes on anyway because the winning coalition4 Lecture 6 that keeps Putin in power, groups such as the security services and big business, bears almost none of its cost, and the deaths at the front have little bearing on them.

On the battlefield, neither side is likely to win easily. On September 26, the Institute for the Study of War (ISW), a Washington think tank, assessed that Russian forces had gained almost no ground since March, and in a September KIIS survey 62 percent of Ukrainians said they could endure the war for as long as necessary. With Russia able to wait despite making little headway and Ukraine determined to hold its ground, the grinding war of attrition is likely to drag on.

When the bleeding spreads beyond the front

Russia's strategy rests on the premise that it can keep the war's impact confined to the front, and Ukraine's long-range drones seek to break that premise. Striking deep behind enemy lines once required bombers or long-range missiles; cheap drones have sharply lowered that bar. In September and October, Ukraine struck Russian refineries, export terminals and data centers belonging to Yandex, Russia's largest internet company, one after another. Reuters reported on October 8 that, as a result, fuel was running short in many parts of Russia, lines were forming at gas stations and consumer prices were climbing.

Russia's budget is growing tighter. The 2026 deficit forecast has doubled from the original plan to 3.2 percent of GDP, and the liquid assets of the National Wealth Fund have fallen below half their pre-invasion level. Even so, the government has decided to raise defense spending in 2027 and cover the gap with tax increases and cuts to social spending. A cost once confined to the front has begun to shift onto society as a whole.

Then the Iran war intervened The Iran war and Hormuz. Russia's Urals crude, which sold for about $45 a barrel before the United States and Israel struck Iran on February 28, reached $113.89 on April 8 and still averaged more than $90 in September. The Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA) in Finland estimates that higher oil and gas prices added $35.9 billion to Russia's seaborne export revenue over six months. Moscow is spending much of that windfall to hold down domestic fuel prices: in September alone it paid refiners 305.5 billion rubles in price compensation, about two-thirds of that month's oil and gas tax revenue of 452.4 billion rubles. In effect, Moscow is using its oil-price windfall to keep the fuel shortages caused by Ukraine's strikes from spilling over into everyday life.

In the United States, meanwhile, as fuel prices rose, the Trump administration decided on October 9 to import Russian diesel. It also pressed Ukraine to stop striking Russian refineries, and on October 16 it waived the tariffs it had planned to impose on countries buying Russian oil. President Donald Trump himself pointed to the burden of diesel prices ahead of the November midterm elections. As far as the war in Ukraine is concerned, the Iran war has brought Russia extra revenue while leading Washington to give up some of its own leverage over Moscow.

Higher fuel prices from the Iran war are also shaking European societies. In France, protests that began in mid-September, when diesel topped €2.37 a liter, merged with anger over schools, living costs and public services; on October 17, about 550,000 people took to the streets nationwide, according to the authorities. The protesters have not demanded cuts to aid for Ukraine, but the more frustration grows over living costs and public debt, the harder it becomes for European governments to spend money on Ukraine. European aid fatigue is precisely what Russia is counting on most. Russia can keep the war's impact confined to the front; Europe's democracies cannot shield their citizens from the shock of rising living costs.

As a result, Putin's strategy has become even harder to shift. For that to happen, the cost of the war would have to go beyond inconveniences such as gas station lines and reach the war budget and the winning coalition. There is no evidence yet that it has reached that stage, and higher oil prices and U.S. imports of Russian diesel, among other things, have made it less likely to do so. Still, Washington's easing of sanctions has not upended the situation entirely: existing sanctions on Russia remain in place, nothing confirms that Washington has pressed Ukraine to cede territory, and Europe is keeping up its pressure on Russia.

What to watch

This assessment would change in light of any of the following.

  • Whether Russia formally proposes stopping the war along the current front line, or accepts armed foreign troops stationed in Ukraine—a sign that Putin no longer has a reason to wait.
  • Whether Russia declares a new mobilization—a sign that a cost once confined to the front has spread to society as a whole.
  • How far Russia's fuel supply deteriorates and its budget deficit widens this winter—a gauge of how fast the cost is spilling beyond the front.
  • Whether Congress moves to reverse the sanctions waiver after the November 3 midterm elections—lawmakers from both parties have already objected to the Russian diesel imports and the waiver, and if Congress revives pressure on Russia, Moscow's costs will rise again.
  • Whether Washington revisits the tariff and sanctions waivers in mid-December, when the 60-day grace period cited by Secretary of State Marco Rubio runs out.
  • Whether Europe settles its support after France's presidential election in the spring of 2027—the EU's €90 billion loan covers 2026–2027, and if Russia concludes that support will continue indefinitely, time will no longer be on its side.

Glossary

  1. 100 futures · A chart that divides the scenarios that could unfold by 2030 into 100 dots in proportion to their probability and colors each dot by whether people's lives get better, stay about the same or get worse under that scenario. ↩
  2. Burden shifting · When those in power push the pain of sanctions or war onto opponents or powerless groups. Pape cited it as one reason economic sanctions rarely work (Clisa Academy, Introduction to International Politics, Lecture 9). ↩
  3. Bargaining range · The range of agreements that two risk-averse states should both prefer to war, which exists because fighting is costly. The concept comes from James Fearon (Lecture 5). ↩
  4. Winning coalition · The group whose support a leader needs to stay in power; it is large in democracies and small in authoritarian systems (Lecture 6). ↩
