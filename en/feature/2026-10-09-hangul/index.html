Hangul Day feature · The Korean Peninsula

What it took for a country to invent an alphabet

October 9, 2026 · Clisa Geopolitics

Summary

  1. Slow work with no immediate payoff, such as inventing an alphabet, was possible only when no one at home threatened the throne and no one abroad threatened the state. Sejong's 32 years had both conditions: a royal authority his father had secured in blood, and an international order Ming China held steady.
  2. That stability rested less on institutions than on the will and action of a single monarch, and it did not outlast a generation. A coup came three years after Sejong's death, and within six the Hall of Worthies, the academy that had produced the book explaining the alphabet, was abolished. Hangul survived not by the will of the state but because ordinary people used it.
  3. The Korean Peninsula in 2026 faces an unsettled external order, but its domestic stability rests on democratic institutions rather than, as in Sejong's day, on a single ruler. Democracy entrusts the succession of power to a constitution and elections rather than to one person's temperament or lifespan; what it does not supply by itself is the consensus needed to carry a task across changes of government. What is the task this peninsula must take on for the next generation, and is there a national consensus to carry it forward whoever holds power? Those are questions worth asking on Hangul Day.

An alphabet that came with its own manual

In December 1443, King Sejong of Korea's Joseon dynasty (r. 1418–1450) devised a set of 28 letters. In September 1446 he completed the Hunminjeongeum, "the correct sounds for instructing the people," a book that set out the principles behind the letters and how to use them. The royal annals record both events without a date, noting only that each happened "this month," because the alphabet was not a project debated at court; the king drove it himself, and quietly. Scholars still differ over how much the Hall of Worthies, the royal academy, contributed, but the annals and the Hunminjeongeum both credit Sejong with the invention, and that remains the mainstream view. The preface the king wrote himself, at the head of the book, opens with a plain statement: "The speech of this country differs from that of China and does not match its characters, so that among the ignorant people there are many who wish to say something but cannot express their meaning." A different language needs a different script. The logic has held for 580 years, and Hangul is now the one script that South and North Korea share.

Hangul was not the first script a state had invented. The Khitan rulers of Liao created their scripts around 920 and 925; Li Yuanhao, founder of the Tangut state of Western Xia, issued a Tangut script in 1036, on the eve of declaring his empire; the Jurchen rulers of Jin promulgated their scripts in 1119 and 1138; and in 1269 Kublai Khan proclaimed the 'Phags-pa script the official writing of the Mongol Yuan empire. Choe Mal-li, deputy director of the Hall of Worthies, pointed to exactly these precedents when he petitioned against the new alphabet in 1444. Only the Mongols, the Tanguts, the Jurchens, the Japanese and the Tibetans had scripts of their own, he wrote, and those were the affairs of barbarians; to invent one was to abandon China and make Korea barbarian too. Sejong knew the precedents his critics named and went ahead anyway. Every one of those state-made scripts, from Khitan to 'Phags-pa, has since vanished. The Khitan scripts were banned by the Jin in 1191; 'Phags-pa fell out of use after the Yuan collapsed; the Tangut and Jurchen scripts lingered for roughly two centuries beyond their dynasties and then died. A script made by a state declined when the state lost its power.

Hangul differs from them in two respects. The first is survival. Among scripts invented outright rather than borrowed or adapted, those that serve today as the de facto sole official script of a nation come down to Hangul and the Armenian alphabet. The second is the manual. Other scripts have a known inventor and date; what no other script has, as far as is known, is a book in which its makers systematically explained the design. The Hunminjeongeum explains that the five basic consonants depict the shape of the speech organs as each sound is made, and that the three basic vowels stand for heaven, earth and humanity. The book was rediscovered in 1940. Hangul Day falls on October 9 because a postscript by the scholar Jeong In-ji at the end of the volume is dated to the first 10 days of the ninth lunar month of 1446, and the last of those days, converted to the solar calendar, is October 9. UNESCO inscribed the book on its Memory of the World register in 1997.

Survival, though, did not mean that Hangul was the script of the state all along. It became the default script of official documents only in 1894, under the Gabo Reforms, 448 years after its invention. In the interval, Joseon's official documents were written in classical Chinese, and Hangul was the script of women and children, of Buddhist texts and popular fiction. The Joseon state did not keep Sejong's alphabet alive. Through the centuries in which the state declined to make it official, it was kept alive and handed down by the people who wrote in it.

The two conditions behind a 32-year reign

Once the letters existed, the book explaining them (the Hunminjeongeum) took another three years. Before and after that work Sejong re-established and enlarged the Hall of Worthies (1420), published a manual of Korean farming (1429), built six garrisons along the Tumen River frontier (1434–1449), devised a rain gauge (1441), calculated a calendar fitted to Korea's own sky (1442), and, after 14 years of debate, settled a land tax graded by soil quality (1444). Each took about a decade from start to finish, and each required that the king not change his mind along the way. That is why the usual account of these 32 years dwells on Sejong's character and intelligence. The account is not wrong, but the conditions that allowed Sejong to use those gifts lay elsewhere.

The first condition was stability at home. Sejong's father, Taejong (Yi Bang-won), took up arms in August 1398 and killed the chief minister Jeong Do-jeon and his own half-brother, the crown prince Yi Bang-seok. In 1400 he fought and defeated his elder brother Yi Bang-gan in the streets of the capital, Kaesong, and that same year abolished the private armies kept by princes and by the merit subjects, the officials rewarded for their part in founding the dynasty. As king he had his wife's four brothers put to death in turn (1410 and 1416). After passing the throne to his third son in August 1418 he kept military affairs in his own hands, and at the end of that year he had the new king's father-in-law, Sim On, executed; the pretext was that Sim's brother had criticized the retired king's grip on the army. When Taejong died in 1422, Joseon had no royal kinsman, no merit subject and no in-law left who could stand against the king. Many ministers opposed Sejong's policies, but in 32 years no one challenged his throne, and that security was not something Sejong built. He inherited it from his father.

The second condition was stability abroad. Sejong's reign coincided with a settled stretch of Ming rule under the Yongle, Xuande and Zhengtong emperors, and Joseon kept to the tributary relationship the Ming had defined, sending envoys every year and receiving investiture for each new king. The Ming left Korea's position undisturbed, and Korea had no reason to fight the Ming. One purpose of the Hall of Worthies was to train officials who could manage relations with the Ming smoothly; the Ming order was the premise of Sejong's government. There was fighting with the Jurchens on the northern frontier and a punitive expedition against Japanese pirates in the south, but no war that put the state's existence at stake. Sejong died in 1450, the year after the Tumu crisis, in which Mongol forces from the northern steppe captured a Ming emperor.

With no rival to the throne at home and no threat to the state's existence abroad, a king could spend 10 years at a stretch on work that showed no immediate result. The alphabet was one such piece of work, and the one that lasted longest. Without those conditions, even the most gifted king would have struggled to do the same.

After the conditions were gone

From 1445 Sejong delegated the business of government to his heir, and in 1450 he died. The heir was Munjong, who had been crown prince for 29 years, since 1421. Munjong reigned a little over two years before dying of illness in 1452, and his son Danjong became king at 12. Sejong had spent 29 years preparing the succession. But the heir died young, and in the meantime the princes to whom Sejong had entrusted various tasks in his final years had built up followings of their own.

Taejong had abolished the princes' private armies and kept royal kinsmen out of politics. Sejong, late in life, charged his second son, Prince Suyang, with translating and publishing Buddhist scriptures and with compiling musical scores, while his third son, Prince Anpyeong, moved among the scholars of the Hall of Worthies and became a central figure in learning and the arts. Publishing and pious works were not politics, but through them the princes acquired followers and standing. In October 1453 Prince Suyang led troops to kill Kim Jong-seo and Hwangbo In, the ministers guarding the boy king, and exiled his brother Anpyeong to Ganghwa Island, then had him put to death. In 1455 he took the throne from his nephew as King Sejo. In 1456 he executed the officials who had tried to restore Danjong, abolished the Hall of Worthies and suspended the royal lectures. In 1457 he had Danjong killed. All of it came within seven years of Sejong's death.

Sejo did not stop everything. He began compiling a code of law and reformed the land system; the code was completed in 1485 as the Gyeongguk daejeon, Joseon's Great Code. What he cut off were the Hall of Worthies and the royal lectures: the institutions at the king's side that produced books and could tell him no, among them the very body that had explained the Hunminjeongeum. Sejong's tax law, his frontier and his calendar survived; the institution that had helped produce them could not withstand a single coup.

Hangul, for its part, did not owe its survival to the protection of the Joseon state. Within six years of Sejong's death, Joseon abolished the body that had explained the alphabet, and in 1504 King Yeonsangun, seizing on an anonymous denunciation written in Hangul, went so far as to ban the teaching and learning of the script. For nearly 400 years afterward the documents of the state were written in classical Chinese. Hangul survived not through official recognition or protection but through use. It lived in mothers' letters to their daughters, in the Buddhist translations the court printed, and in the novels that passed from hand to hand.

The question for the Korean Peninsula in 2026

Hold the two conditions behind Sejong's 32 years up against the Korean Peninsula of 2026, and the external one is being shaken. The international order is strained by rivalry between the United States and China; Washington is moving to reduce the role of U.S. forces in Korea; and in February 2026 North Korea's party congress formalized its status as a nuclear-armed state and its "two states" doctrine toward the South The Korean Peninsula. The domestic condition differs in kind from Sejong's. Where Joseon's progress depended heavily on the temperament and lifespan of one monarch, South Korea's stability rests on democratic institutions: a constitution, elections, a National Assembly and courts. That the country holds steady when its president changes every five years is the work of those institutions, a system that South Koreans rebuilt in blood after the tragedy of dictatorship. In that respect South Korea has what Sejong never had.

Under absolute monarchy, the direction and pace of progress depend heavily on the disposition of one ruler, and a change of ruler often halts or reverses them. That is why Sejong's 32 years ended with his generation. Democracy entrusts the succession of power not to a monarch's lifespan or an heir's quality but to a constitution and elections. What democracy does not supply by itself is continuity of policy: a change of government normally brings a change of policy, and for any undertaking to outlast a government there must be a separate consensus that it should. South Korea's large infrastructure projects and long-term reforms have carried across governments because such a consensus existed. Its approach to North Korea, by contrast, has not. The alliance with the United States has remained the foundation of South Korea's foreign policy under every government; policy toward the North has reversed course each time power has passed between conservatives and progressives. Dealing with the North is strategic and in part philosophical work, and its results are slow to appear; no consensus, it seems, has formed around it, and policy has hinged instead on the judgment of each president in turn.

Of everything Sejong left behind, what lasted longest and survives to this day is an alphabet, and what preserved it for 450 years was not the state or those who held power but the people who wrote in it. It may be that what ultimately sustains work that is strategic and philosophical, invisible yet important, is the steady attention and will of the people themselves. The 450 years of Hangul after Sejong suggest as much.

Glossary

  1. Hunminjeongeum · "The correct sounds for instructing the people": the original name both of Sejong's alphabet and of the 1446 book that explained it. The section setting out the design principles is called the haerye, so the volume is known as the Haerye edition. Rediscovered in Andong in 1940, it is held by the Kansong Art Museum in Seoul. ↩
  2. Tributary relationship (sadae) · Literally "serving the great": the term for Joseon's relationship with Ming China. Korea sent envoys every year and received investiture for each new king; the Ming did not touch Korea's throne or its borders. ↩
  3. Hall of Worthies (Jiphyeonjeon) · The royal academy Sejong re-established and enlarged in 1420. It compiled books, lectured the king on the classics and answered his requests for policy advice. Sejo abolished it in 1456. ↩
  4. Royal lectures (gyeongyeon) · Sessions at which the king read the classics with his ministers and discussed government: the formal channel through which officials could disagree with the king. ↩
  5. The coup of 1453 (Gyeyu jeongnan) · The seizure of power in 1453 by Prince Suyang, who killed the senior ministers guarding his young nephew, King Danjong. Two years later Suyang took the throne as King Sejo. ↩
