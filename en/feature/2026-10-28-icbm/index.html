Why North Korea is holding off on ICBM test launches

October 28, 2026 · Clisa Geopolitics

Summary

  1. Conventional analysis and reporting holds that North Korea lost interest in negotiating with the United States after aligning with Russia. Clisa Geopolitics, however, sees North Korea using ICBM test launches as a bargaining chip in talks with Washington and in pursuit of its geopolitical goals. One reason is that its pauses in ICBM testing have coincided with periods when U.S.-North Korea summitry was under way or under discussion.
  2. Halting ICBM launches serves as a bargaining chip with Washington, while also helping to pry South Korean and U.S. interests apart and suiting the interests of China, North Korea's patron. Since 2022, as U.S.-China rivalry has deepened, China has stepped back from holding North Korea to account and has instead blamed South Korea and the United States.
  3. North Korea keeps firing the short- and intermediate-range missiles that neither China nor the United States much cares about. If Washington and Pyongyang strike a deal that limits only ICBMs, the short- and intermediate-range nuclear missiles aimed at South Korea and Japan are likely to be left out of it and to harden into a North Korean lever that could reshape the security landscape of Northeast Asia.

North Korea's silent ICBMs

According to Japan's Ministry of Defense, North Korea has launched 16 ICBM-class missiles since 2017 (Figure 1): three in 2017, seven in 2022, five in 2023 and one in 2024, with none in 2018–2021 or since 2025. The last was a Hwasong-19 on October 31, 2024, five days before the U.S. presidential election.

Other ballistic missile launches continued in the meantime. The ministry counted five in 2025 and at least 15 in 2026 through September 20, and North Korea fired ballistic missiles again on October 3 and 12. What North Korea has halted is not missile launches as such, but only test launches of the ICBMs aimed at the U.S. mainland.

Nor has ICBM development stopped: in place of test launches, North Korea has kept displaying its ICBM capability through ground tests and public events (Figure 2).

Why only the ICBMs are held back

Conventional analysis and reporting holds that North Korea no longer needs to negotiate with the United States now that it has expanded military cooperation with Russia. The Associated Press reported in July 2025 that North Korea, focused on its cooperation with Russia, showed no near-term interest in diplomacy, and many experts took the same view. On this reading, the absence of ICBM tests has nothing to do with negotiations and is explained instead by other factors, such as the Hwasong-20's development schedule or restraint urged by Russia and China.

Clisa Geopolitics takes a different view: North Korea is using its ICBM tests as a bargaining chip with the United States and for its geopolitical goals.

First, the points at which North Korea halted and then resumed ICBM test launches have tracked the course of U.S.-North Korea summitry:

  • 2017: In President Trump's first year in office, North Korea fired three ICBMs.
  • April 2018: North Korea announced a halt to nuclear tests and ICBM test launches, and the Singapore summit followed in June.
  • 2020–2021: North Korea declared at the end of 2019 that it was no longer bound by its moratorium, but it soon sealed its borders against the COVID-19 pandemic, and its ballistic missile launches overall fell sharply during this period.
  • January–February 2022: In January, North Korea said it would consider resuming the activities it had suspended, and in late February it resumed test launches, firing 13 ICBMs through October 2024.
  • Since 2025: While President Trump has again floated a meeting with Kim Jong Un, there has not been a single ICBM test launch.

Halting test launches that directly threaten the U.S. mainland is the easiest concession North Korea can offer Washington before negotiations even begin. And since test launches can be resumed at any time, North Korea gives up little by making it.

North Korea stands to gain a good deal from this bargaining chip. In a September 21, 2025, speech to the Supreme People's Assembly, Kim Jong Un said he could talk with the United States if it dropped its demand for denuclearization, but that he would not negotiate any trade-off to obtain sanctions relief. The ruling Workers' Party's Ninth Congress likewise set respect for its current status (recognition as a nuclear-armed state and withdrawal of the hostile policy) as the condition for talks. This suggests North Korea needs a negotiation in which it first secures de facto recognition as a nuclear-armed state and then builds on that to win sanctions relief as well.

Second, the ICBM pause is not only about negotiations with Washington: Clisa Geopolitics judges that a calculation aimed at separating South Korean and U.S. interests is also at work. If North Korea offers to lift the threat to the U.S. mainland while continuing to hold and display a regional nuclear strike capability through its short- and intermediate-range missiles, Washington becomes more likely to weigh the cost of defending South Korea and Japan separately from its own interests, and the room for U.S. and South Korean interests to diverge grows accordingly.

Third, the ICBM pause also suits the interests of China, North Korea's patron. Test launches aimed at the U.S. mainland give Washington a rationale for its Golden Dome missile defense plan and for a U.S. military buildup, which also burdens China. By halting them, North Korea stays within the bounds China will tolerate and preserves the conditions for China's continued protection at the UN Security Council.

Why China does not stop it

North Korea's nuclear missiles are a burden for China too, because the threat they pose fuels calls for nuclear armament in South Korea and Japan and gives grounds for Golden Dome (the U.S. missile defense plan) and for military cooperation among the United States, South Korea and Japan. That is why China voted for all six UN Security Council sanctions resolutions against North Korea in 2016–2017, and in February 2017 it also halted imports of North Korean coal.

Yet the same ICBM launches drew opposite responses from China at different times (Figure 3). China backed sanctions in 2017, but since 2022 it has stepped back from holding North Korea to account:

  • May 26, 2022: The day after North Korea fired an ICBM, China joined Russia in vetoing a draft resolution imposing additional sanctions.
  • March 28, 2024: China abstained in the vote on extending the mandate of the UN panel of experts on North Korea sanctions.
  • Since 2024: China began dropping the phrase “denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula” from official statements, and did not use it in its November 2025 arms control white paper or during President Xi Jinping's June 2026 visit to Pyongyang.

What changed in between was not North Korea's behavior but U.S.-China relations. In 2017 President Trump linked North Korea to trade, saying a trade deal would be far better for China if it solved the North Korean problem, and China's cooperation on sanctions was likely driven in part by the expectation of such a reward on trade. But the United States labeled China a strategic competitor in December 2017 and launched a trade war in July 2018. In 2022, Russia's invasion of Ukraine and U.S. House Speaker Nancy Pelosi's visit to Taiwan deepened the U.S.-China confrontation further. With nothing left to gain from cooperating on the North Korean nuclear issue, China began to treat that issue not as a problem to be solved but as leverage against the United States.

The threat that remains for South Korea

If North Korea offers to halt ICBM test launches in negotiations and the United States accepts, the threat to the U.S. mainland will shrink. But the short- and intermediate-range missiles aimed at South Korea are easily left out of a deal; in 2019, when North Korea fired a string of short-range missiles, President Trump indicated that short-range missiles were not a problem.

In March 2023, North Korea unveiled its Hwasan-31 tactical nuclear warhead alongside a chart showing it could be mounted on a range of short- and intermediate-range missiles, including the Hwasong-11 family. Its frequent recent launches are therefore closer to operational deployment and unit training aimed at South Korea than to new development. In January 2024, the U.S. government also said Russia had struck Ukraine with North Korean ballistic missiles; through these sales, North Korea is gaining both combat performance data and revenue.

If a U.S.-North Korea summit takes place without South Korea formally involved in the preparations, Clisa Geopolitics puts the chance that the agreement contains no provisions on inter-Korean relations or on short- and intermediate-range missiles at 75 percent No inter-Korean provisions in a U.S.-North Korean deal 75%.

North Korea's halt to ICBM test launches is not a sign that the threat is shrinking, but a sign that the party bearing the risk and cost of the North Korean threat is changing. North Korea holds the threat to the U.S. mainland as a bargaining chip while it keeps building its nuclear strike capability aimed at South Korea and Japan, and this widens the room for Washington to weigh the cost of defending its allies separately from its own interests.

Rather than restraining North Korea, China, for its part, has held South Korea and the United States responsible for the costs of the North Korean threat, and has thereby kept U.S. military influence on the Korean Peninsula from growing. Its economic retaliation over the 2017 THAAD deployment, and the “Three Nos” position the South Korean government subsequently stated, are the clearest examples1·2. In Clisa Geopolitics' judgment, North Korea's strategic aim is that, even though South Korea faces the risk from North Korea's nuclear weapons head-on, the negotiations and deals over those weapons should take place among the United States, North Korea and, behind them, China, without South Korea.

What to watch

  • The chance that North Korea resumes ICBM test launches by June 2027 is 13 percent North Korea resumes ICBM flight tests 13%, and it rises if a Trump-Kim meeting (35 percent) Trump-Kim meeting in first half of 2027 35% falls through.
  • The chance that North Korean state media announce an ICBM test moratorium tied to dialogue with the United States is 12 percent North Korea ties an ICBM test moratorium to talks with Washington 12%.
  • How any U.S.-China leaders' document issued at the APEC summit in mid-November refers to the Korean Peninsula.
  • How the December plenary meeting of the Workers' Party Central Committee describes the United States in its report, and whether it mentions the Hwasong-20.

Signs this assessment is wrong

  • A summit takes place, but North Korea refuses to include an ICBM test moratorium, or equivalent language, in the agreement.
  • North Korea demands sanctions relief first, rather than recognition as a nuclear-armed state, in return for a test moratorium or an equivalent step.
  • A U.S.-North Korea summit falls through, yet ICBM test launches do not resume (in which case the development schedule may be the bigger reason).
  • North Korea conducts an ICBM test launch while summit diplomacy is still under way.
  • China explicitly says it “opposes” North Korean missile launches, or votes for additional Security Council sanctions, even though U.S.-China relations have not eased.
  • North Korea conducts a seventh nuclear test or an ICBM test launch during a major Chinese diplomatic event.

Glossary

  1. THAAD · Terminal High Altitude Area Defense, a missile defense system. In July 2016, South Korea and the United States decided to deploy THAAD with U.S. Forces Korea against North Korean missiles, and it was deployed in Seongju, North Gyeongsang Province, in 2017. China objected that the THAAD radar could monitor Chinese territory and retaliated economically: it restricted group tours to South Korea, forced stores run by South Korean companies to suspend operations and curbed South Korean pop culture. ↩
  2. The “Three Nos” · Three positions stated by the South Korean government in October 2017: no additional THAAD deployment, no participation in the U.S. missile defense system, and no development of South Korea-U.S.-Japan security cooperation into a military alliance. The South Korean government explained that this was a statement of existing positions, not an agreement with or commitment to China, and on October 31 that year Seoul and Beijing agreed to improve relations. ↩
