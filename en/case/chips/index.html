The U.S.-China contest over AI and chips: how long South Korea can use its leverage, and where

As of October 5, 2026

The question
Caught between U.S. tariffs and controls on one side and China's market and minerals on the other, when and where should South Korea use the leverage of memory?
Assessment
Through 2027, while the HBM shortage lasts, Seoul should lock in the terms of U.S. chip-tariff exemptions in return for recognition of its U.S. investment, and keep front-end memory fabrication at home. Multiyear supply contracts with Nvidia and other U.S. buyers would create a constituency in Washington with a stake in the supply of Korean memory. On the Chinese plants, Seoul should offer no new concessions and ask Washington for written license-renewal criteria, while the companies manage the share of production in China themselves. For rare earths and materials, it should rely on dialogue with China and stockpiles in the short term and on diversified sourcing in the long term. Large-scale adoption of Chinese equipment, or joining the China-led World AI Cooperation Organization (WAICO), would put the entire U.S. equipment license at risk.
Most likely outcome
M1 Managed separation 40%
U.S. controls stay in place, and the annual licenses are renewed each year. Korea's plants in China make only limited process transitions with no expansion, and their share of total output declines gradually; chip tariffs settle into exemptions tied to investment in the United States. Washington and Beijing keep extending the tariff truce and leave rare-earth controls suspended. CXMT steadily gains share in commodity memory.
Probability by outcome
M1 Managed separation 40% · M3 Intensified pressure from both sides 25% · M4 Accelerated Chinese substitution 20% · M2 Transactional easing 15%
The other side’s best move
China · Lead with co-optation to keep South Korea close (J1) and selective conciliation (J0), and show the threshold with targeted sanctions (J3) on individual companies that cooperate with U.S. investigations and controls. This is the move that serves China's goals of technological self-reliance (G1), self-reliant supply chains (G2), and attracting foreign investment (G3) at once, without damaging relations with South Korea as a whole.
The likely outcome
Washington and Beijing both prefer that the Chinese plants of Samsung Electronics and SK hynix keep running without expansion rather than shut down. The value of this "limited coexistence" will be reset at two points: when the annual U.S. licenses expire at the end of 2026, and when the U.S.-China tariff truce ends in January 2027.
What it means for people
This issue could shake South Koreans' livelihoods. Semiconductors made up about a quarter of South Korea's exports in 2025 and close to half of monthly exports during the boom, so stronger U.S. and Chinese pressure would lower the income outlook for South Koreans as a whole. The first to be shaken are local staff and expatriate managers at the Chinese plants, and small and midsize manufacturers that use rare earths and materials.

Key assessments

South Korea's window for using its memory chips as leverage to lock in the terms of U.S. chip tariffs and of licenses to bring equipment into its plants in China runs through 2027, while the shortage of high-bandwidth memory (HBM) lasts, and those terms are more likely to be secured through long-term supply contracts with American buyers than through trade-offs between governments.

ReasoningBecause a ban on expanding or upgrading the plants in China is already a condition of U.S. licenses, a pledge to refrain from doing so cannot serve as a bargaining chip; even TSMC's Nanjing plant, which accounts for only about 3 percent of the company's capacity, received no more than a one-year license. What has actually moved export controls, by contrast, has usually been commercial interests. In 1957, when Britain unilaterally abolished the stricter controls on trade with China, the so-called China differential, citing its own trading interests, other European countries followed; in 2025, lobbying by Nvidia got the GAIN AI Act, which would have restricted AI chip exports so that U.S. demand was met first, dropped from the National Defense Authorization Act.
