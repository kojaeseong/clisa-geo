The Russia-Ukraine War: How should Ukraine end the war?

As of October 5, 2026

The question
How should Ukraine end the war?
Assessment
Build the capacity to outlast a stalemate through defense-industrial self-reliance, expanding domestic production of drones and ammunition, and aim for a ceasefire that does not legally cede the occupied territories, on condition that it is backed by great-power troops. Any ceasefire agreement should impose no caps on the size of Ukraine's armed forces or the range of its missiles.
Most likely outcome
U1 Continued war of attrition 33%
Short truces come and go, but the front line keeps inching back and negotiations stall on territory. European support keeps Ukraine going, but population loss and casualties mount.
Probability by outcome
U1 Continued war of attrition 33% · U3 Peace on Russia's terms 20% · U6 Unguaranteed ceasefire (Minsk-style) 18% · U2 Guaranteed ceasefire (Korean-style) 12% · U5 A favorable end to the war 10% · U4 Collapse of the front 7%
The other side’s best move
Russia · For now, the best course is to keep the bilateral deal with Washington alive while taking the rest of Donetsk Oblast through a war of attrition. In 2026 Russia has repeatedly demanded withdrawal from all of Donetsk Oblast as a precondition for a ceasefire, and Putin has rejected advice to freeze the current front line.
The likely outcome
Russia has made the withdrawal of Ukrainian forces from the rest of Donetsk Oblast a precondition for a ceasefire, so for now the point where the two sides' best moves meet is a war of attrition. Even if a ceasefire is reached on the current front line, it is more likely to lack the backing of great-power troops and to break down again, as the 2014–2015 Minsk agreements did.
What it means for people
The longer the war of attrition lasts, the more casualties mount and the later the children forcibly deported to Russia will come home. The first item on the ceasefire agenda should be the return of prisoners of war and deported children; this is the only channel through which talks have continued even amid the stalemate.

Key assessments

A ceasefire on the current front line in the Russia-Ukraine war is unlikely until Russia concludes that it can no longer bear the cost of taking the rest of Donetsk Oblast.

ReasoningEven if a ceasefire is reached, without guarantees backed by great-power troops it is likely to be no more than a pause before the fighting resumes, as with the Minsk agreements of 2014–2015. Yet Russia rejects the very presence of Western troops.
