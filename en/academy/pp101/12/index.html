The question for this session

If one person earns 10 times as much as another, does taxing away part of the gap restore justice or violate it? In A Theory of Justice (1971), John Rawls argued that the principles of justice are those people would agree to if they did not know where they would stand in society, and that such principles permit inequality only when it benefits the worst off. Three years later his Harvard colleague Robert Nozick replied in Anarchy, State, and Utopia that whatever is justly acquired and freely exchanged is just, however unequal the result.

This session sets out the core arguments of both books and the points where they collide. It then brings both frameworks to bear on a question the U.S.-South Korea alliance is facing again as of November 6, 2026: who should pay for protection, and how much? The question fits because Nozick traced the origin of the state to a market in protective associations, and Rawls, in The Law of Peoples (1999), worked out the fair terms of cooperation among peoples separately.

1. The original position and the veil of ignorance

Rawls opens with a bold claim: "Justice is the first virtue of social institutions." Laws and institutions, however efficient, must be reformed or abolished if they are unjust (section 1). Society, in his phrase, is "a cooperative venture for mutual advantage" (section 1). Cooperation produces gains no one could achieve alone, and people's interests clash over how to divide them. The principles of justice govern that division, and their primary subject is not individual conduct but the "basic structure" of society: the constitution, the system of property, the market, and the family (section 2).

To find those principles, Rawls proposes a thought experiment. Imagine that the people choosing society's basic rules do not know whether they will be born rich or poor, talented or not, or what values they will hold. This is the "original position," and the device that screens out knowledge of one's own circumstances is the "veil of ignorance" (sections 3 and 24). As Rawls puts it, "the principles of justice are chosen behind a veil of ignorance" (section 3). Since no one can tailor the principles to favor himself, the agreement is fair, which is why Rawls called his theory "justice as fairness." In effect, he recast the social contract of Locke, Rousseau, and Kant (sessions 6, 7, and 10) as a hypothetical agreement.

His first target was utilitarianism (session 9). If justice means maximizing total happiness, then sacrificing a few for a large gain to the many can be justified. "Utilitarianism does not take seriously the distinction between persons," Rawls objects (section 5). One person's loss cannot be offset by another's gain. This "separateness of persons" is where A Theory of Justice begins, and, as section 3 of this session shows, Nozick starts from the same premise and reaches the opposite conclusion.

2. The two principles and the difference principle

Rawls holds that the parties in the original position would agree to two principles. The summary below draws on the first statement in section 11 and the final statement in section 46.

Principle Content Priority
First principle (equal liberty) Each person has an equal right to the most extensive system of basic liberties compatible with a like liberty for all (political liberty, freedom of speech, conscience, and thought, freedom of the person, the right to hold personal property, and so on) Takes priority over the second principle
Second principle (a): the difference principle Social and economic inequalities are to be arranged to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (b) takes priority over (a)
Second principle (b): fair equality of opportunity Those inequalities are to be attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity

The priority is "lexical": the earlier principle must be satisfied first, so no economic gain, however large, can justify curtailing basic liberties. Nor does the difference principle demand strict equality. If high pay for doctors draws more people into medicine and the poorest end up with better care as a result, that inequality is just (this lecture's example; in section 13 Rawls himself uses entrepreneurs and unskilled workers).

The natural lottery. For Rawls, the family one is born into and the talents one is born with are, morally speaking, matters of chance, the results of a "natural lottery" (section 12). The distribution of talents is neither just nor unjust. "These are simply natural facts"; what is just or unjust is the way institutions deal with them (section 17). The difference principle amounts to an agreement to treat the distribution of natural talents as "a common asset," so that the gifted may benefit from their good fortune on terms that also improve the lot of the least advantaged (section 17).

Maximin. Parties who know neither their place nor the odds of landing in any place, Rawls argues, should "rank alternatives by their worst possible outcomes" and choose the one whose worst outcome is best: the maximin rule (section 26). The rule fits, he says, under three conditions: there is no basis for estimating probabilities, the chooser cares little for gains above the guaranteed minimum, and the rejected alternatives carry outcomes one could hardly accept (section 26). The Going deeper section returns to how well this argument holds up.

3. Nozick: from protective association to minimal state

Nozick's book opens with the words "Individuals have rights" and announces its conclusion at once. A minimal state, limited to protecting people against force, theft, and fraud and to enforcing contracts, is justified; any more extensive state violates people's rights (preface).

Rights as side constraints (chapter 3). For Nozick, rights are not goals to be maximized but "side constraints" that no pursuit of any goal may cross. They rest on the Kantian principle that "individuals are ends and not merely means" (session 10). There is no social entity that undergoes a sacrifice for its own greater good, only different individuals with their own lives; to use one person for the benefit of others is simply to use him. Rawls invoked the separateness of persons against utilitarianism. Nozick invokes it against redistribution.

The state arises by an invisible hand (chapters 2–5). Nozick begins from Locke's state of nature (session 6). People form mutual-protection associations, and with the division of labor, businesses spring up to sell protection. Where several agencies compete in one area, clients drift to the agency that keeps winning, or the agencies divide the territory, or they agree on a common procedure for settling disputes. Either way, each area ends up with a single "dominant protective association" (chapter 2). Because no one designed this outcome, Nozick calls it an "invisible-hand" explanation.

The dominant association becomes an "ultraminimal state" when it stops "independents" from punishing on their own and so gains a de facto monopoly on force, and a "minimal state" when it protects even those who have not paid (chapter 3). The second step looks like redistribution, using some people's money to protect others. Nozick's answer is the principle of compensation: having forbidden independents to enforce their own rights, the dominant association owes them compensation, and the cheapest form is to supply protection (chapters 4 and 5).

Unrequested benefits create no obligation (chapter 5). Nozick also attacks the "principle of fairness" advanced by H. L. A. Hart and Rawls (the fair-play theory of session 1; A Theory of Justice, section 18), which holds that those who benefit from a cooperative scheme must share its burdens. Suppose neighbors set up a public-address system and take turns running it, one day each. You enjoy the broadcasts but never agreed to the plan. When your day comes, must you give it up? Nozick says no. One cannot confer benefits on people and then demand, or seize, payment.

4. The entitlement theory: acquisition, transfer, rectification

In chapter 7, which opens part II of the book, Nozick first questions the very term "distribution." Goods are not handed out by someone; people make them and exchange them. His "entitlement theory" rests on three principles (chapter 7, section 1).

Principle Content
Justice in acquisition How unheld things may justly come to be held (including a weak reading of Locke's proviso, session 6)
Justice in transfer How holdings may justly pass from one person to another, through voluntary exchange or gift
Rectification of injustice How to remedy past violations of the first two principles, such as theft, fraud, or seizure

The core claim is brief: "Whatever arises from a just situation by just steps is itself just" (chapter 7, section 1). Justice for Nozick is historical. Whether a distribution is just depends not on its shape but on how it came about. Principles that allot shares according to merit, need, or some other criterion are "patterned," and Rawls's difference principle is one of them. Nozick's own maxim is "From each as they choose, to each as they are chosen."

The Wilt Chamberlain argument. Take whatever distribution the reader thinks most just, say perfect equality, and call it D1. The basketball star Wilt Chamberlain signs a contract under which 25 cents from each home-game ticket goes into a box for him. Over a season, a million fans gladly drop in their quarters, and Chamberlain ends up with $250,000, far richer than anyone else. Is the new distribution, D2, unjust? D1 was just by hypothesis, the fans parted with their shares voluntarily, and no third party's holdings were touched. So D2 must be just too (chapter 7, section 1). Maintaining any pattern, Nozick concludes, requires continual interference with voluntary exchange; a socialist society would have to "forbid capitalist acts between consenting adults."

Taxation and forced labor. "Taxation of earnings from labor is on a par with forced labor," Nozick declares (chapter 7, section 1). Taking a share of someone's earnings amounts to making him work that many hours for others. The theory has a third principle, however. If existing holdings were shaped by conquest, slavery, or plunder, rectifying them could require substantial redistribution, and Nozick allowed that, lacking a precise method of rectification, a rough rule such as the difference principle might serve (end of chapter 7, section 2; this lecture's summary). The entitlement theory does not simply ratify existing inequality.

5. The debate between them

Nozick's critique of Rawls (chapter 7, section 2). The original position, Nozick charges, treats goods as if they fell like manna from heaven, with no one entitled to them. Its parties never ask who made what; they choose only the shape of the outcome, so the setup can yield nothing but patterned principles. Nozick also rejects the natural-lottery argument: even if people's talents are morally arbitrary, people can be entitled to their talents and to what those talents produce. Treating talents as a "common asset," he argues, ignores the very separateness of persons that Rawls invoked.

Rawls's reply. In Political Liberalism (1993), lecture 7, "The Basic Structure as Subject," Rawls gives his main answer (this lecture's summary). Even if each transaction is fair, their cumulative effect can erode background fairness, so principles of justice must apply first to the basic structure within which transactions occur. Chamberlain's quarters, too, change hands within a framework set by contract and property law. Liam Murphy and Thomas Nagel later developed the point in The Myth of Ownership (2002): pretax income is no natural baseline of what is "mine."

Beyond borders: The Law of Peoples. A Theory of Justice assumed a closed society, isolated from others (section 2). In The Law of Peoples, which takes up justice across borders, the parties to the original position are representatives of peoples, and they do not know the size of their territory, their population, or their relative strength (part 1, section 3). The eight principles they adopt are: peoples are free and independent; they keep treaties; they are equal; they do not intervene in one another's affairs; they have a right of self-defense but no right to wage war for other reasons; they honor human rights; they observe limits on the conduct of war; and they have a duty to assist peoples living under unfavorable conditions (part 1, section 4; Introduction to International Relations, session 9).

Rawls rejects the proposal of Charles Beitz and Thomas Pogge to extend the difference principle worldwide (part 3, section 16). The duty of assistance aims to help "burdened societies," which lack the institutions and resources to become well ordered, to stand on their own, and it ends once that aim is met (part 3, sections 15–16). His example: if two peoples start from the same point but make different choices about saving and industrialization, the resulting gap in wealth is no injustice to be corrected.

6. Classics and theory: after Rawls and Nozick

Most later theories of distributive justice have taken the form of revising or attacking one of the two books.

Thinker Argument Relation to Rawls and Nozick
Ronald Dworkin, "Equality of Resources" (1981) People should bear the consequences of their choices but be compensated for brute bad luck in their endowments Builds on Rawls's natural lottery but draws a sharper line between choice and luck
G. A. Cohen, Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality (1995) Grant self-ownership and Nozick's conclusions are hard to escape, so the premise itself must be attacked Takes on Nozick's starting point; later also criticizes Rawls for allowing inequality as an incentive
Amartya Sen, "Equality of What?" (1979) The measure of equality should be capabilities, what people can actually do and be, not primary goods Criticizes Rawls's use of primary goods to define disadvantage
Michael Sandel, Liberalism and the Limits of Justice (1982) The self behind the veil is an "unencumbered self," detached from community and ends Criticizes Rawls's conception of the person; a starting point of communitarianism (session 13)

7. Applying it to 2026: who should pay for protection?

The facts. U.S. Forces Korea (USFK) numbers about 28,500 troops. The U.S. National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal 2026 bars the use of funds to reduce USFK below 28,500 or to complete the transfer of wartime operational control (OPCON) in a way that departs from the plan the two allies agreed, though the restriction lapses 60 days after the secretary of defense certifies the move and submits an assessment. On July 22, 2026, the House passed a bill extending the same conditions through fiscal 2027. Meanwhile, the 2026 U.S. National Defense Strategy handed South Korea primary responsibility for conventional deterrence of North Korea, and in August 2026 the Trump administration unilaterally scaled back the Ulchi Freedom Shield combined exercise (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Korean Peninsula issue).

The joint communiqué of the annual Security Consultative Meeting (SCM) included a pledge to maintain USFK at its "current level" in 2019, 2021, and 2024 but not in 2020, and the 57th communiqué, in November 2025, said only that force and posture levels would be maintained. This year's 58th meeting is reportedly slipping to around December because of disagreement over the conditions for the OPCON transfer (record behind Clisa Geopolitics forecast f77), a disagreement that surfaced publicly in mid-October in remarks by the USFK commander and a South Korean defense ministry spokesman (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, October 16, 2026, via The Star).

Cost is a further point of contention. South Korea's share of the cost of stationing U.S. troops is set by the 12th Special Measures Agreement, signed on November 4, 2024. Seoul pays 1.5192 trillion won in 2026, 8.3 percent more than in 2025; from 2027 through 2030, the amount rises with consumer prices, capped at 5 percent a year (reported by The Korea Herald and The Korea Times). On October 15, 2024, then-candidate Donald Trump said that if he were president, South Korea would be paying $10 billion a year (reported by Bloomberg). In a joint fact sheet released on November 14, 2025, South Korea said it planned to raise defense spending to 3.5 percent of GDP, buy $25 billion of U.S. military equipment by 2030, and provide $33 billion in comprehensive support for USFK (reported by Asia Economy and Korea Pro).

Through Nozick's lens. A client of a protective association pays a fee and buys protection. On this view, whether the cost of protection is just depends on procedure, not on the shape of the outcome. Terms both sides freely accepted define the just share, and the 12th agreement, in force through 2030, is a commitment to be kept. A demand for payment beyond the contract is merely an offer of a new deal, and the protector, for its part, owes no protection the contract does not cover (this lecture's interpretation). The argument that South Korea has long enjoyed protection and should therefore pay more has the same structure as the principle of fairness that Nozick attacked with his public-address example. Yet the alliance rests on explicit consent, the 1953 Mutual Defense Treaty, so South Korea is not in the position of the neighbor who never agreed to the plan. The principle of compensation is suggestive as well: South Korea's reliance on U.S. extended deterrence instead of its own nuclear weapons resembles a party that has laid down the means of self-help and receives protection in return (this lecture's interpretation). South Korea did abandon its weapons program in the 1970s under heavy U.S. pressure, but it formally took on the obligation itself by ratifying the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) in 1975, so the case differs from a dominant association unilaterally forbidding independents to enforce their own rights.

Through Rawls's lens. The representatives in The Law of Peoples do not know whether their people is strong or weak. Fair terms of defense cooperation, then, should not be set by differences in bargaining power. By that standard, both raising cost-sharing through threats of troop cuts and dodging a fair share in the belief that the other side cannot leave are suspect (this lecture's interpretation). Carry Rawls's principle of fairness for individuals (A Theory of Justice, section 18) over to peoples, and South Korea, as a beneficiary of the alliance, owes a fair share; but since Rawls refused to apply the difference principle among peoples, no rule that richer countries should pay more follows directly from his theory (this lecture's interpretation). The "primary responsibility" for conventional deterrence of North Korea that the National Defense Strategy assigns, and that Seoul has signaled with its plan to raise defense spending, fits instead with the first principle of The Law of Peoples: peoples are free and independent. South Korea is no burdened society, so the comparison is loose; but just as the duty of assistance ends when a burdened society can stand on its own, an alliance's support can be understood as aimed at the partner's self-reliance, and the OPCON transfer is a test of it (this lecture's interpretation). If one side alone set the pace and conditions of that self-reliance, however, the arrangement would fall short of fair cooperation.

The limits of the analogy. First, states are not individuals; a country's choices bind citizens who opposed them and generations not yet born. Second, an alliance is not a market. South Korea has, in practice, only one possible protector, and the United States gains from USFK interests of its own, in countering China and in regional influence. Insofar as the alliance looks more like joint production than a sale, the public-goods logic of Olson and Zeckhauser, discussed in Introduction to International Relations, session 3, fits parts of it better. Third, A Theory of Justice is a theory for a closed society, and Rawls himself adopted different principles for relations among peoples.

Check your understanding

  1. What does the veil of ignorance conceal, and why does Rawls think it yields fair principles?
  2. How does the difference principle differ from strict equality? Give an example.
  3. What are the three principles of Nozick's entitlement theory, and how do "historical" principles differ from "patterned" ones?
  4. Nozick denies that a dominant protective association redistributes when it protects independents who have not paid. On what grounds?

Discussion questions

  1. How would Rawls and Nozick each assess South Korea's progressive income tax and its inheritance tax?
  2. Do you agree with Rawls that even achievements earned by effort owe much to inborn talent and family background? Apply the question to South Korea's debates over fairness in university admissions and hiring.
  3. What should be the fair standard for dividing the cost of an alliance's protection: ability to pay, benefits received, proximity to the threat, or the terms originally agreed?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows key passages of both books, works through maximin and the Chamberlain argument with numbers, and looks at research that has tested or criticized the two theories.

Reading the original

Because the works of Rawls and Nozick are under copyright, they are not quoted at length here. The passages below are close summaries of the sections cited, not translations; only the short phrases in quotation marks are the authors' own words. Section numbers in A Theory of Justice are the same in the 1971 first edition and the 1999 revised edition.

Passage 1. The veil of ignorance (A Theory of Justice, sections 3 and 24; summary with short quotations)

Behind the veil, the parties know nothing of their social position or class, of how well endowed they are with abilities such as intelligence and strength, or of what they take a good life to be. "The principles of justice are chosen behind a veil of ignorance." No one, therefore, is advantaged or disadvantaged in choosing principles by natural chance or social circumstance (section 3). The parties also do not know their society's economic or political situation or which generation they belong to, but they do know general facts: political affairs and economic theory, and the laws of human psychology (section 24).

How to read it. The veil is not a real assembly but a picture of how to reason impartially (section 4). Because the parties know general facts, including that incentives raise output, they can arrive at a difference principle that permits some inequality.

Passage 2. The natural lottery and the common asset (A Theory of Justice, section 17; summary with short quotations)

Neither the natural distribution of talents nor being born into a particular social position is unjust. "These are simply natural facts." What is just or unjust is the way institutions deal with these facts. The difference principle amounts to an agreement to regard the distribution of natural talents as "a common asset" and to share in the benefits it makes possible. Those favored by nature may gain from their good fortune only on terms that improve the situation of those who have lost out. Neither greater natural ability nor a better start in life is something anyone has earned.

How to read it. What is to be regarded as a common asset is not talent itself but its distribution. The talents remain the person's own; society sets the rules for sharing the benefits they generate. The 1999 revised edition qualifies the phrase to read "in some respects" a common asset.

Passage 3. Entitlement and Wilt Chamberlain (Anarchy, State, and Utopia, chapter 7, section 1; summary with short quotations)

Justice in holdings has three topics: original acquisition, transfer, and the rectification of injustice. Anyone who acquires something under the rule of just acquisition, or receives it from its rightful owner under the rule of just transfer, has a right to it, and there is no other route to a rightful holding than repeated use of these two rules. "Whatever arises from a just situation by just steps is itself just." Now start from the reader's favored distribution, D1. Chamberlain signs a contract for 25 cents of each ticket price, and a million people watch his games, each freely giving him a quarter out of what was justly held under D1. If the resulting D2 is unjust, what is unjust about it?

How to read it. The argument draws its force from letting the reader pick the starting point: whatever pattern one chooses, voluntary exchange upsets it. The objections ask whether the fans also accepted the result that one man would accumulate great wealth and power (Cohen), and whether the "just holdings" of D1 were not defined from the outset within institutions (Rawls, as in section 5 of this session).

Working it out

Problem 1. The veil of ignorance and maximin. A society consists of three groups of equal size. Their incomes are as follows: society A (10, 10, 10), society B (12, 18, 30), society C (5, 30, 61). You must choose a society without knowing which group you will belong to. (1) What is the average income in each society? Which society would be chosen by a rule that maximizes average income, by the maximin rule, and by a rule that insists on strict equality? (2) If society D (11, 60, 100) is added to the options, which does the maximin rule choose? What problem does this reveal, in light of Rawls's three conditions for maximin?

Problem 2. Chamberlain's quarters. (1) If a million fans each pay 25 cents per ticket over a season, how much does Chamberlain earn? (2) If that income is taxed at 40 percent and the proceeds are returned equally to the million fans, how much does each receive? (3) If Chamberlain works 2,000 hours a year, how many hours of "forced labor," in Nozick's terms, does a 40 percent tax represent? What are the objections to this calculation?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. Average income is 10 in A, (12 + 18 + 30) ÷ 3 = 20 in B, and (5 + 30 + 61) ÷ 3 = 32 in C. The average-maximizing rule picks C; the maximin rule compares worst outcomes (A 10, B 12, C 5) and picks B; the strict-equality rule picks A. B is unequal, but its worst-off group (12) is better off than everyone in egalitarian A (10). That is why the difference principle prefers B to A.
  2. D's average is (11 + 60 + 100) ÷ 3 = 57, nearly three times B's, but its worst outcome, 11, is below B's 12, so maximin still picks B. The rule rejects a society in which the worst-off group gives up 1 so that the other two gain 42 and 70: this is John Harsanyi's objection. Measured against Rawls's third condition, D's worst outcome of 11 is better even than egalitarian A's 10 and can hardly be called unacceptable, so the case for maximin weakens. Rawls replied that the choice is not a single gamble but a choice of social structure that fixes lifetime prospects and basic liberties, which makes a cautious rule rational.

Problem 2.

  1. 1,000,000 × $0.25 = $250,000.
  2. The tax is $250,000 × 0.4 = $100,000; divided among a million fans, it comes to 10 cents each. Returning fully to D1 would require taxing away all of Chamberlain's earnings, which is the "continual interference" Nozick warned of, expressed in numbers.
  3. 2,000 × 0.4 = 800 hours. There are two objections. Unlike forced labor, an income tax leaves the worker free to work less and pay less. And, as Murphy and Nagel argue, pretax income arises within contract law, property law, and public order, all sustained by taxes, so taxes cannot be treated simply as a seizure from what is wholly "mine."

Evidence and counterarguments

Frohlich and Oppenheimer (1987): the veil of ignorance in the laboratory. Norman Frohlich, Joe A. Oppenheimer, and a colleague had participants who did not yet know their income class choose a principle of distribution through group discussion. Few groups chose maximin. The most popular choice was to maximize average income subject to a floor, a compromise between Rawls and utilitarianism (American Journal of Political Science 31, no. 3). Laboratory subjects are not the parties to the original position, but the weight they gave to a guaranteed minimum supports part of Rawls's intuition.

Harsanyi (1975) and Rawls (1974): the maximin debate. The economist John C. Harsanyi argued that even without known probabilities it is rational to assign equal probabilities to each outcome and maximize average utility, and that maximin leads people to forgo very large gains to avoid very small risks (American Political Science Review 69, no. 2). Rawls defended the rule not as a general principle of choice under uncertainty but as suited to the special case of choosing a society's basic structure (American Economic Review 64, no. 2). Society D in Problem 1 is the debate in miniature.

Cohen (1977) and Nozick's second thoughts. G. A. Cohen countered that although each fan paid a quarter willingly, the fans did not thereby consent to the outcome, in which Chamberlain acquires power over other people's lives (Erkenntnis 11, no. 1). Nozick himself, in "The Zigzag of Politics," a chapter of The Examined Life (1989), conceded that the libertarian position he had once advanced gave too little weight to the need for politics to express shared bonds of solidarity (this lecture's summary). He did not, however, retract the arguments of Anarchy, State, and Utopia one by one.
