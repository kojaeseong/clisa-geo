The question for this session

Hobbes argued that escaping the terror of the state of nature requires people to hand their rights to an absolute sovereign (session 5). John Locke (1632–1704) begins in the same place and arrives at the opposite conclusion. People hold rights to life, liberty, and property before any government exists. Government is a power they grant by consent so that those rights are better protected. And when a government betrays that trust, power returns to the people.

This session follows the Second Treatise of Government to see how Locke's three claims, about natural rights, property, and resistance, fit together into a single argument. It then applies his framework to Taiwan, which holds local elections on November 28, 2026, and a presidential election in January 2028, and asks whose consent makes a government legitimate. It also asks where Locke's framework can answer that question and where it cannot.

1. The state of nature: liberty, not license

The Second Treatise appeared anonymously in late 1689. It was long read as a justification of the Glorious Revolution of 1688, but Peter Laslett argued in his 1960 edition that much of it was written earlier, around 1679–1683, amid the struggle to exclude the Catholic Duke of York, later James II, from the succession. Locke's theory of resistance, on this account, was not an apology written after a revolution but a dangerous argument made before one.

Like Hobbes, Locke starts from a condition without government, the state of nature. It is a state of perfect freedom for people to order their actions and dispose of their possessions "within the bounds of the law of nature, without asking leave, or depending upon the will of any other man," and a state of equality in which no one stands above another (chapter 2, §4). "But though this be a state of liberty, yet it is not a state of licence." The state of nature has a law, and reason, which is that law, teaches that "no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions" (§6). Locke grounds this in theology: all human beings are the workmanship of one maker, so none may treat another as an instrument for his own use.

In the state of nature, everyone holds the executive power of the law of nature and may punish those who break it (§7). The trouble is that people judging their own cases are lenient with themselves and harsh with others, so Locke grants that "civil government is the proper remedy for the inconveniencies of the state of nature." In the same section, however, he turns on Hobbes: if the remedy is absolute monarchy, it is no improvement, because "absolute monarchs are but men" (§13). For Locke, the state of nature is inconvenient but is not the same as a state of war, which arises when force is used against another without right (chapter 3, §19). And to the objection that no state of nature has ever existed, he replies that "all princes and rulers of independent governments all through the world, are in a state of nature" (§14). The point that no common judge stands over states will matter again in section 7.

2. Property: labor makes the common one's own

God gave the world to humanity in common. How, then, can any one person come to own part of it? In chapter 5, Locke's answer is labor.

...every man has a property in his own person ... The labour of his body, and the work of his hands, we may say, are properly his. Whatsoever then he removes out of the state that nature hath provided, and left it in, he hath mixed his labour with, and joined to it something that is his own, and thereby makes it his property. (§27)

Two conditions attach to such acquisition. First, there must be "enough, and as good, left in common for others" (§27; see also §33), the condition now known as the Lockean proviso. Second, a person may take only as much as he can use before it spoils: "whatever is beyond this, is more than his share, and belongs to others" (§31). The same logic extends to land. Locke adds that cultivated land yields, on a conservative estimate, ten times what the same land yields lying waste, so whoever encloses and tills land does not shrink the common stock of humanity but enlarges it (§37).

Money undoes these limits. Gold and silver do not spoil, so a person can exchange his surplus for them and accumulate without end. Locke concludes that by accepting money, people "have agreed to a disproportionate and unequal possession of the earth," having found, "by a tacit and voluntary consent," a way to possess more land than they can use (§50). Inequality of property, in this account, comes not from nature but from people's consent to money.

"Property" for Locke is not wealth in the narrow sense. He uses it as "the general name" for people's "lives, liberties and estates" (chapter 9, §123). To say that government exists to protect property is to say that it exists to protect people's bodies, their freedom, and the fruits of their labor. Critics have charged, though, that Locke slides conveniently between the broad meaning and the narrow one (see Macpherson in section 6).

3. Consent: how government begins

Chapter 8 is the heart of the book.

Men being, as has been said, by nature, all free, equal, and independent, no one can be put out of this estate, and subjected to the political power of another, without his own consent. (§95)

Once people agree to unite into a community, that community must move in one direction, so the decision of the majority binds the whole (§§96–97). A demand for unanimity would mean that the community could not outlast the day it was born (§98). What begins a political society, then, is "nothing but the consent of any number of freemen capable of a majority to unite and incorporate into such a society. And this is that, and that only, which did, or could give beginning to any lawful government in the world" (§99).

But when do people born under an existing government consent? Locke first insists that a father's promise cannot bind his children, because a son, once grown, is as free as his father (§116). He then distinguishes express consent from tacit consent. Anyone who possesses or enjoys any part of a government's territory gives tacit consent, whether the possession is land held forever, "a lodging only for a week," or simply "travelling freely on the highway" (§119). The two kinds of consent differ in effect. The duty of obedience that flows from tacit consent ends when a person gives up the possession and leaves, whereas one who has become a member by express consent is "perpetually and indispensably obliged" to remain one unless the government is dissolved (§121).

Tacit consent is widely regarded as the weakest link in Locke's theory. If walking down a highway counts as consent, almost no one has failed to consent, and consent loses its force as a standard for limiting government. Hume's analogy, discussed in session 1, takes aim at exactly this point: a man carried aboard a ship while asleep does not consent to the captain's rule merely by declining to jump overboard.

4. The ends of government and the limits of legislative power

People enter society for "the mutual preservation of their lives, liberties and estates" (§123). The state of nature lacks three things: an established, known law agreed to by common consent, an impartial judge to apply it, and the power to enforce the judge's sentences (§§124–126). Government therefore cannot reach beyond the public good, and it must rule "by established standing laws, promulgated and known to the people, and not by extemporary decrees" (§131).

Chapter 11 sets out the limits on legislative power. The legislature is the supreme power, yet it cannot be arbitrary over people's lives and fortunes (§135). "The supreme power cannot take from any man any part of his property without his own consent" (§138). Taxes, too, require "the consent of the majority, giving it either by themselves, or their representatives chosen by them" (§140), the principle that American colonists would invoke some seven decades later as "no taxation without representation."

Behind these limits lies the idea of trust. Locke calls the legislature "only a fiduciary power to act for certain ends" and holds that "there remains still in the people a supreme power to remove or alter the legislative, when they find the legislative act contrary to the trust reposed in them" (chapter 13, §149). Government is a trustee, not an owner.

5. Prerogative and resistance: the appeal to heaven

Locke concedes that law cannot foresee everything, so he leaves discretion to the executive. "This power to act according to discretion, for the public good, without the prescription of the law, and sometimes even against it, is that which is called prerogative" (chapter 14, §160). His example is pulling down an innocent man's house to stop a fire from spreading from the house next door (§159). Whether prerogative is used rightly depends on whether it serves or harms the people (§161).

Who decides when prerogative has been abused? Between the executive and the people there is no judge on earth, Locke answers, and so "the people have no other remedy in this, as in all other cases where they have no judge on earth, but to appeal to heaven" (§168). He had introduced the phrase in chapter 3 with the biblical judge Jephthah, who, disputing land with the Ammonites and lacking any earthly court to settle it, declared, "The Lord the Judge ... be judge this day between the children of Israel and the children of Ammon," and led his army into battle (§21; Judges 11:27). To appeal to heaven is to resist by force, on one's own judgment, when no earthly judge exists. Locke adds a limit: this right "operates not, till the inconveniency is so great, that the majority feel it, and are weary of it, and find a necessity to have it amended" (§168).

Chapter 19 develops the argument. Locke first separates the dissolution of society from the dissolution of government. Society, he holds, is almost never dissolved except by foreign conquest (§211). Yet conquest cannot create a government: "conquest is as far from setting up any government, as demolishing an house is from building a new one in the place," and "without the consent of the people, can never erect a new one" (chapter 16, §175). Governments can also dissolve from within. When legislators try to seize people's property or subject them to arbitrary power, they put themselves into a state of war with the people, and power reverts to the people. An executive who bribes representatives or uses threats and promises to dictate whom the electors choose commits the same betrayal (§§221–222).

To the objection that his theory invites rebellion, Locke offers three replies. People do not readily abandon their old institutions (§223). They bear great mistakes and bad laws, but when "a long train of abuses, prevarications and artifices, all tending the same way," makes a design visible, it is no wonder that they rise (§225). And the true rebels are those in power who break the law by force (§226). To the final question of who shall judge, he answers: "The people shall be judge" (§240). Whether a trustee keeps faith is for the one who granted the trust to decide.

7. Applying it to 2026: whose consent makes Taiwan's government legitimate?

The facts. Taiwan elects 22 mayors and county magistrates on November 28, 2026, and a president in January 2028. The Kuomintang (KMT) currently holds 13 or 14 of the 22 posts (Clisa Geopolitics, record behind forecast f72). On April 10, KMT chair Cheng Li-wun met Chinese President Xi Jinping in Beijing and said the Taiwan Strait should become a symbol of peace safeguarded jointly by "Chinese people on both sides"; both leaders voiced opposition to Taiwan independence and foreign interference. Taiwan's Mainland Affairs Council criticized her "one family" framing for treating the sovereignty dispute as an internal matter (Al Jazeera, April 10, 2026). In his National Day address on October 10, President Lai Ching-te said Taiwan is "open to exchanges, but will not accept subordination," and, citing the first direct presidential election in 1996, said that Taiwan's future will be decided by its people. Beijing's Taiwan Affairs Office denounced the speech as "separatist thinking" (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, October 11, 2026, via Focus Taiwan and Al Jazeera). On October 20, a China Coast Guard ship facing off with a Taiwanese patrol vessel in restricted waters off the Pratas Islands broadcast that "the so-called Republic of China does not exist" (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, October 21, 2026, via the Taipei Times).

Three positions. Beijing's position is codified in the Anti-Secession Law of 2005. Article 2 declares that "both the mainland and Taiwan belong to one China" and makes safeguarding China's sovereignty and territorial integrity the common obligation of all Chinese people, Taiwan compatriots included. Article 8 provides for "non-peaceful means" if secession occurs, if "major incidents entailing Taiwan's secession" occur, or if the possibilities for peaceful reunification are "completely exhausted." The Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) government holds that the Republic of China and the People's Republic of China are not subordinate to each other and that Taiwan's future is for its people to decide. The KMT, citing the 1992 Consensus, opposes independence and favors dialogue with Beijing but rejects "one country, two systems." Public opinion can be gauged from the Election Study Center at National Chengchi University, whose combined surveys for January–June 2026 (4,807 respondents) found 63.1 percent identifying as Taiwanese, 30.7 percent as both Taiwanese and Chinese, and 2.5 percent as Chinese. On unification and independence, 34.4 percent favored maintaining the status quo indefinitely (up from 25.8 percent in 2020), 25.5 percent the status quo with a decision later, 21.7 percent the status quo moving toward independence, 4.5 percent independence as soon as possible, and 1.3 percent unification as soon as possible (Clisa Geopolitics, Taiwan Strait analysis, public-opinion data; figures read from the center's published charts, with a possible error of about one percentage point).

Through Locke's lens. A lawful government begins only in the consent of free people (§99). Locke allows consent to taxes to be given through elected representatives (§140) and counts the rigging of elections as a breach of trust (§222). Taiwan's legislators have been chosen in direct elections since 1992 and its presidents since 1996, so the power Taiwan's present government exercises over its residents can be said to rest on consent in Locke's sense. The same standard applies to the Republic of China's own past: from 1945 until the lifting of martial law in 1987 and the democratization that followed, its rule over Taiwan could hardly claim the consent of the governed. And by §175, conquest cannot establish a legitimate government without the consent of the people (this lecture's interpretation).

Locke's standard, however, does not settle the outcome in advance for either side. First, consent cuts both ways. If Taiwanese voters elect a government that puts dialogue with Beijing first, that too is an expression of consent. What Locke's framework asks is not which result emerges but whether it emerges free of coercion. Beijing regards U.S. arms sales and political support as foreign interference; Taipei regards China's military and gray-zone pressure as election meddling. Second, Locke does not say who the consenting people are. His majority rule operates among those who have already united into one community (§§96–97). Beijing holds that the people entitled to decide Taiwan's status are all Chinese; Taipei holds that they are Taiwan's residents (see Problem 2 in Going deeper). Third, §121 says that one who has become a member by express consent cannot leave the community unless its government is dissolved, which leans toward preserving existing communities rather than toward separation. Yet the fact that Taiwan has never been under the effective control of the People's Republic, founded in 1949, leaves open whether Taiwan's residents ever consented to belong to the community Beijing describes. Beijing, for its part, identifies that community with "one China" rather than with the government of the People's Republic, and so rejects the question itself. Locke's text alone cannot resolve either point (this lecture's interpretation).

The limits of the analogy. Locke wrote about why government within a country is legitimate, not about secession or the recognition of states. He treated independent governments as standing toward one another in a state of nature (§14). Whether cross-strait relations are relations between states is itself disputed, but on either view no common judge stands over the two governments. A dispute with no common judge resembles Jephthah's quarrel with the Ammonites, an "appeal to heaven" that can be settled by force (§21). The most accurate lesson Locke offers here may be not a verdict on who is right but a warning that such disputes slide easily into a state of war (this lecture's interpretation). Self-determination and the recognition of states developed in twentieth-century international law (for example, Article 1(2) of the UN Charter) and cannot be drawn straight from Locke.

Check your understanding

  1. Why is Locke's state of nature "a state of liberty" but "not a state of licence"? What is the biggest difference from Hobbes's state of nature?
  2. What two conditions does Locke attach to acquiring property through labor, and how does money neutralize one of them?
  3. How do express and tacit consent differ in how long they bind (§121)?
  4. When Locke calls legislative power a "fiduciary" power held in trust, what follows from it?
  5. What are Locke's three replies to the objection that his theory of resistance invites rebellion?

Discussion questions

  1. When, and in what way, can someone born and raised in South Korea be said to have consented to its government? Which of voting, paying taxes, or performing military service comes closest to Locke's "express consent"?
  2. Article 77 of South Korea's constitution empowers the president to declare martial law but requires the president to lift it when a majority of the National Assembly's members demands it. How does this arrangement resemble, and differ from, Locke's relationship between prerogative and legislative power? Consider the declaration of martial law on December 3, 2024, the National Assembly's vote demanding that it be lifted, and the Constitutional Court's ruling removing the president from office on April 4, 2025.
  3. South Korea's constitution establishes popular sovereignty in Article 1(2) and defines the national territory in Article 3 as "the Korean peninsula and its adjacent islands." How do legitimacy based on consent and a claim to territory coexist within the Korean constitution? In what ways is this question like and unlike the Taiwan question in section 7?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages of the Second Treatise in the original, works through Locke's proviso and the unit of majority rule with numbers, and looks at research and counterarguments on consent and Locke's theory of property.

Reading the original

The quotations from the Second Treatise in this session, both below and in the main text, are taken from Project Gutenberg eBook 7370, a transcription of C. B. Macpherson's Hackett edition (1980), with its spelling retained and omissions marked by ellipses. References are to chapter and section numbers.

Passage 1. Labor and the proviso (Second Treatise, chapter 5, §27)

Though the earth, and all inferior creatures, be common to all men, yet every man has a property in his own person: this no body has any right to but himself. ... for this labour being the unquestionable property of the labourer, no man but he can have a right to what that is once joined to, at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others.

How to read it. The argument runs in three steps: my body is mine, so my labor is mine, so whatever my labor is mixed with is mine. Nozick exposed the weakness of the "mixing" metaphor by asking whether pouring a can of tomato juice into the sea makes the sea one's own. The proviso in the final clause was easily met when land was plentiful; how to read it once land grows scarce became the central question in later debates about property.

Passage 2. Tacit consent (Second Treatise, chapter 8, §§119 and 121)

The difficulty is, what ought to be looked upon as a tacit consent, and how far it binds ... And to this I say, that every man, that hath any possessions, or enjoyment, of any part of the dominions of any government, doth thereby give his tacit consent, and is as far forth obliged to obedience to the laws of that government, during such enjoyment, as any one under it; whether this his possession be of land, to him and his heirs for ever, or a lodging only for a week; or whether it be barely travelling freely on the highway ... (§119)

...whereas he, that has once, by actual agreement, and any express declaration, given his consent to be of any commonwealth, is perpetually and indispensably obliged to be, and remain unalterably a subject to it, and can never be again in the liberty of the state of nature; unless, by any calamity, the government he was under comes to be dissolved ... (§121)

How to read it. Section 119 stretches consent nearly as far as it can go; section 121 splits its effects in two. Locke never says how many people have actually given express consent. On what basis most people are "members" of a political community therefore remains an open question within his theory.

Passage 3. Breach of trust (Second Treatise, chapter 19, §222)

...whenever the legislators endeavour to take away, and destroy the property of the people, or to reduce them to slavery under arbitrary power, they put themselves into a state of war with the people, who are thereupon absolved from any farther obedience ... He acts also contrary to his trust, when he either employs the force, treasure, and offices of the society, to corrupt the representatives, and gain them to his purposes; or openly preengages the electors, and prescribes to their choice, such, whom he has, by sollicitations, threats, promises, or otherwise, won to his designs ...

How to read it. Locke grounds the right of resistance in two wrongs: the invasion of property and the corruption of elections. The first concerns what a government does; the second, how it obtains consent. Consent that survives in form but is dictated by coercion is not consent at all.

Working it out

Problem 1. The proviso and money. An island has 100 households and 1,000 hectares of arable land. A household can use the produce of eight hectares without letting any of it spoil. One hectare left in common yields one unit; one hectare under cultivation yields 10 units (the tenfold ratio of §37). (1) Before money exists, if every household takes as much land as it can use without spoilage, how much land is left? Is the proviso met? (2) After money arrives, the first 20 households take 40 hectares each. How many hectares are left for each of the remaining 80 households, and is the proviso met? (3) Compare a world in which all land stays common and the 100 households share its produce equally with the situation in (2), in which latecomers cultivate their share. Is Nozick's weaker proviso satisfied?

Problem 2. The unit of majority rule. A country of 100 million people contains a region, R, of 5 million. A proposal to expand R's autonomy is supported by 80 percent of R's residents and 30 percent of residents elsewhere. (1) If the whole country votes at once, what share supports the proposal? (2) If only R's residents vote? (3) Do §§95–99 of the Second Treatise settle which of the two majorities is legitimate?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. 100 households × 8 hectares = 800 hectares, leaving 200 hectares. Everyone has as much as they can use and land remains, so the proviso is met.
  2. 20 households × 40 hectares = 800 hectares, leaving 200 hectares for 80 households, or 2.5 hectares each. That is less even than the spoilage limit of eight hectares, so the proviso, read literally, is not met. The first households also escape the spoilage limit by exchanging their surplus for gold and silver (§50).
  3. If all land stays common, total output is 1,000 units, or 10 units per household. In (2), each latecomer cultivates 2.5 hectares × 10 units = 25 units, better than under the commons. Nozick's weaker proviso, which requires only that no one be left worse off than before the appropriation, is therefore satisfied. But each early household gets 40 hectares × 10 units = 400 units, 16 times as much as a latecomer. The logic of §37 shows that everyone can end up better off than before; whether that justifies the gap is a separate question, and it is precisely where Macpherson's critique aims.

Problem 2.

  1. Supporters in R number 4 million (5 million × 0.8) and supporters elsewhere 28.5 million (95 million × 0.3), for a total of 32.5 million, or 32.5 percent. The proposal fails.
  2. It passes with 80 percent.
  3. They do not. Locke's majority rule operates inside a community that individuals have already agreed to form (§§96–99). Majority rule presupposes that the set of voters has been fixed in advance, so that set cannot itself be fixed by majority rule. Frederick G. Whelan called this the "boundary problem" of democratic theory in 1983 and argued that democratic procedures alone cannot solve it. That the same territory can yield opposite results depending on how the unit is drawn is what makes the question in section 7 so hard.

Evidence and counterarguments

Anderson and colleagues (2005): losers' consent. In Losers' Consent, Christopher J. Anderson and four coauthors compared surveys across many countries and found that supporters of parties that lose elections are less satisfied with how democracy works, and less trusting of government, than supporters of the winners, with the gap generally larger in newer democracies. Locke's principle that the majority decides can work in practice only if the losing side accepts the result. In an election like Taiwan's in 2028, in which voters are divided over cross-strait policy, whether the losers accept the outcome will matter as much as who wins in determining the substance of consent (this lecture's interpretation).

Counterargument: Locke's consent and America. James Tully and Barbara Arneil have argued that Locke's theory of property served alongside arguments justifying English colonization of America. Locke repeatedly cites American land as the example of uncultivated land and writes that "in the beginning all the world was America" (§49). If land used for hunting and gathering does not count as property, taking it without the inhabitants' consent becomes legitimate. Locke served as secretary to the Lords Proprietors of Carolina and invested in the Royal African Company, which traded in enslaved people [secondary source]. Jeremy Waldron counters that Locke's egalitarianism rests on universal equality before God and so stands in tension with such uses. The debate asks whom the "people" of consent theory have historically included and whom they have left out.
