The question for this session

The Social Contract opens with a famous sentence: "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains." Jean-Jacques Rousseau draws two questions from it. How did people come to be in chains? And what, if anything, could make those chains legitimate? The Discourse on the Origin of Inequality (1755) answers the first; the Social Contract (1762) answers the second.

Hobbes and Locke told a story in which people in the state of nature create the state by contract (sessions 5 and 6). Rousseau gives that story a darker ending. The contract actually struck in history, he argues, was a swindle by which the rich persuaded the poor to accept their own subjection, and its result was inequality fixed in law. Yet he does not abandon the idea of a contract. If everyone gave themselves equally to the community and submitted to the "general will," he believed, people could live together and still obey no one but themselves.

This session follows the key passages of both books to see what the general will is and why it has drawn so much admiration and so much alarm. It then applies Rousseau's framework to an issue unfolding as of November 6, 2026: how the European Union will finance Ukraine in 2027. Rousseau thought the general will could work only in a small republic. Can a union of 27 states have one?

1. Rousseau, citizen of Geneva, and his two books

Rousseau was born in 1712 in the Republic of Geneva, the son of a watchmaker. A small state in which the assembled citizens make the laws remained the model for his entire political thought.

The Discourse was an entry in a prize competition held by the Academy of Dijon, which asked: "What is the origin of inequality among men, and is it authorised by natural law?" Rousseau begins by separating two kinds of inequality. One is "natural or physical," arising from differences of age, health, bodily strength, and qualities of mind. The other is "moral or political," because it "depends on a kind of convention" and is established, or at least authorized, by human consent. It consists of privileges some enjoy at others' expense, "such as that of being more rich, more honoured, more powerful or even in a position to exact obedience" (Introduction). Rousseau's subject is the second kind. Because people made it rather than nature, people can also unmake it.

2. The state of nature and two kinds of self-love

Rousseau accuses Hobbes of projecting onto natural man the desires and fears that only society produces. Competing for honor and fearing others are passions that arise only when people compare themselves with one another. Rousseau's natural man lives alone in the forest, eating when hungry and sleeping when tired. In this creature's soul Rousseau finds "two principles prior to reason, one of them deeply interesting us in our own welfare and preservation, and the other exciting a natural repugnance at seeing any other sensible being, and particularly any of our own species, suffer pain or death" (Preface). The second principle is pity.

Self-love and egoism. Rousseau distinguishes two forms of self-regard (Part I, note). Amour de soi is the natural concern for self-preservation shared by every animal; in human beings, "guided ... by reason and modified by compassion," it "creates humanity and virtue." Amour-propre is "a purely relative and factitious feeling, which arises in the state of society, leads each individual to make more of himself than of any other, causes all the mutual damage men inflict one on another." Cole, whose translation this session follows, renders the first "self-respect" and the second "egoism"; most modern translators keep the French terms or use "self-love" and "amour-propre." In the state of nature, Rousseau insists, amour-propre did not exist, because there was no one to compare oneself with.

3. "This is mine": property and the rich man's contract

Part II of the Discourse opens with one of the most famous sentences in the history of political thought:

The first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying This is mine, and found people simple enough to believe him, was the real founder of civil society. From how many crimes, wars and murders, from how many horrors and misfortunes might not any one have saved mankind, by pulling up the stakes, or filling up the ditch, and crying to his fellows, "Beware of listening to this impostor; you are undone if you once forget that the fruits of the earth, belong to us all, and the earth itself to nobody." (Part II, opening)

Rousseau immediately adds a qualification. The idea of property depends on many prior ideas that could only have been acquired one after another, so by the time the first fence went up, things had probably gone too far to turn back. The enclosure is not the cause but one scene in a long process.

The first scene is comparison. Once people began to gather before their huts around a large tree to sing and dance, "each one began to consider the rest, and to wish to be considered in turn," and a value "came to be attached to public esteem." The best singer or dancer, the handsomest, strongest, most dexterous, or most eloquent came to be most admired, and "this was the first step towards inequality, and at the same time towards vice." From these first distinctions came vanity and contempt on one side and shame and envy on the other. This is the birth of the amour-propre described in section 2.

The last scene is the social contract itself. Property acquired by force could be taken by force, and so the rich were insecure. The rich man therefore "conceived at length the profoundest plan that ever entered the mind of man": to turn the strength of those who threatened him to his own advantage. "Let us join," he told his neighbors, "to guard the weak from oppression, to restrain the ambitious, and secure to every man the possession of what belongs to him." Rousseau's verdict: "All ran headlong to their chains, in hopes of securing their liberty." The society and laws that resulted "bound new fetters on the poor, and gave new powers to the rich," and turned clever usurpation into an unalterable right (Part II).

The passage can be read as a direct rebuttal of Locke (session 6). For Locke, government exists to protect property, and that is what makes it legitimate. For Rousseau, that is precisely the reason for suspicion: a contract to protect property favors those who already have it (this lecture's interpretation).

4. The social contract and the general will

The Social Contract sets out to correct the contract the Discourse had denounced. In book 1, chapter 6, Rousseau states the problem:

"The problem is to find a form of association which will defend and protect with the whole common force the person and goods of each associate, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before." This is the fundamental problem of which the Social Contract provides the solution. (book 1, chapter 6)

His solution is "the total alienation of each associate, together with all his rights, to the whole community." Because everyone gives himself entirely, the conditions are the same for all, and no one has an interest in making them burdensome to others. And because each gives himself to all, he gives himself to nobody. The compact reduces to a single clause:

"Each of us puts his person and all his power in common under the supreme direction of the general will, and, in our corporate capacity, we receive each member as an indivisible part of the whole." (book 1, chapter 6)

The public person created by this act is the "Sovereign." Its members are "citizens" insofar as they share in sovereign power and "subjects" insofar as they obey the laws. Under the contract, a person gives up "natural liberty," the right to whatever he can seize, and gains "civil liberty," which the general will limits, along with property secured by law. He also gains "moral liberty," for "the mere impulse of appetite is slavery, while obedience to a law which we prescribe to ourselves is liberty" (book 1, chapter 8).

The general will and the will of all. Rousseau takes care to separate the general will from the outcome of a vote: "There is often a great deal of difference between the will of all and the general will; the latter considers only the common interest, while the former takes private interest into account, and is no more than a sum of particular wills" (book 2, chapter 3). The will of all adds up what each person wants for himself; the general will is what each wants, as a citizen, for everyone. That is why the general will "must both come from all and apply to all," and why it "loses its natural rectitude" the moment it is aimed at a particular person or case (book 2, chapter 4). People judge fairly when they set rules that will apply to themselves as well.

What clouds the general will is the "partial association," or what would now be called a faction or an interest group. The will of such an association is general for its members but particular with respect to the state. When factions form, there are "no longer as many votes as there are men, but only as many as there are associations," and when one association prevails over all the rest, the general will disappears and only a single particular opinion remains (book 2, chapter 3). Rousseau's advice is to have no partial associations at all, or, if they cannot be avoided, to multiply them and keep them equal.

Equality as a precondition. Here the two books meet. The general will can operate only if citizens share a common interest, and extreme inequality turns rich and poor into groups with opposing interests. Rousseau therefore defines equality not as identical holdings but as a condition in which "no citizen shall ever be wealthy enough to buy another, and none poor enough to be forced to sell himself" (book 2, chapter 11). The prescription points in the same direction as Aristotle's argument for a broad middle class (session 3).

5. "Forced to be free": majorities, representation, and the size of states

Forced to be free. Rousseau's most controversial sentence comes in book 1, chapter 7. For the compact not to be "an empty formula," it must tacitly include the undertaking that "whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so by the whole body. This means nothing less than that he will be forced to be free." The reasoning is that obeying the general will means obeying a law one has willed as a citizen, and that law protects each citizen "against all personal dependence." Obedience to law is what prevents obedience to other people.

Is the outvoted citizen free? Rousseau confronts the question directly in book 4, chapter 2. When a law is proposed, he argues, citizens are asked not whether they like it but whether it conforms to the general will. Hence: "When therefore the opinion that is contrary to my own prevails, this proves neither more nor less than that I was mistaken, and that what I thought to be the general will was not so." The argument carries a condition, however. It holds only so long as "all the qualities of the general will still reside in the majority." Once the majority becomes the vehicle of a faction, liberty is impossible whichever side one takes.

The same chapter lays down two rules for setting the majority required. First, "the more grave and important the questions discussed, the nearer should the opinion that is to prevail approach unanimity." Second, the more a matter calls for speed, the smaller the required margin may be; "where an instant decision has to be reached, a majority of one vote should be enough." Only one law requires unanimous consent by its nature: the social compact itself. Those who oppose it at the founding do not invalidate it but are simply left out of it. "They are foreigners among citizens."

Sovereignty cannot be represented. Because sovereignty lies in the general will, and will cannot be represented, deputies are not the people's representatives but "merely its stewards," and any law the people has not ratified in person "is null and void." In a line aimed at the most admired constitution of his day, Rousseau wrote: "The people of England regards itself as free; but it is grossly mistaken; it is free only during the election of members of parliament" (book 3, chapter 15). He did, however, distinguish legislation from execution. The legislative power, which declares the general will, cannot be represented; the executive power, which merely applies the law, can and should be.

The size of states. All of this presupposes a small state. "Every extension of the social tie means its relaxation; and, generally speaking, a small State is stronger in proportion than a great one" (book 2, chapter 9). At the end of book 3, chapter 15, Rousseau promised to show how the external strength of a great people might be combined with the good order of a small state through confederation, but he never wrote that sequel (see Passage 3 in Going deeper). Section 7 begins in the space he left empty.

7. Applying it to 2026: can a union of states have a general will?

The facts. At the European Council of December 18, 2025, EU leaders agreed to lend Ukraine €90 billion over 2026 and 2027. Rather than using frozen Russian assets as collateral, the loan was to be financed by joint borrowing on the markets, backed by headroom in the EU budget. Twenty-four of the 27 member states took part through "enhanced cooperation," a treaty procedure that allows a subset of members to proceed; the Czech Republic, Hungary, and Slovakia stayed out (European Parliament Legislative Observatory, procedure summary 2026/0008(COD)). In April 2026, Hungary's election ended Viktor Orbán's 16 years in power, and on September 25, after Hungary dropped its veto, the EU approved €6.6 billion in military aid to Ukraine from the European Peace Facility that had been blocked since 2023 (Clisa Geopolitics country profile, Hungary).

At the European Council of October 15–16, President Volodymyr Zelensky said Ukraine would need about €70 billion in 2027 beyond existing commitments. Leaders did not settle how to raise it and asked the European Commission to prepare options (UNN, October 16, 2026, [secondary source]). Reuters reported on October 21 that leaders would take up the shortfall, together with the EU's 2028–2034 long-term budget, at a special summit on November 26–27.

The money itself is voted by national parliaments. On July 6, the German cabinet approved a 2027 budget draft with €11.6 billion for military support to Ukraine, and the Bundestag is scheduled to hold its final vote on November 27 (Clisa Geopolitics, basis for forecast f76 and calendar of events).

Unless the treaties provide otherwise, the European Council decides by consensus. The Council of the EU, where ministers meet, decides many matters by qualified majority, which requires the support of at least 55 percent of member states representing at least 65 percent of the EU's population, but some decisions, including the multiannual financial framework, require unanimity. Enhanced cooperation requires at least nine participating member states.

Through Rousseau's lens. The first issue is who counts as "the people." Those who decide in the European Council are not citizens but heads of state or government, each speaking for a national interest. In the language of book 2, chapter 3, each national will may be general for that nation's citizens but is particular with respect to the union. The EU, on this view, consists of 27 partial associations of very unequal size. Qualified majority voting, which weights states by population, and unanimity, which gives every state the same veto, can be seen as opposite answers to the problem of unequal associations (this lecture's interpretation).

The second issue is unanimity and the veto. By the rules of book 4, chapter 2, grave questions should approach unanimity and urgent ones should be settled by a narrow margin. Financing a country at war is both grave and urgent, so the two rules collide head-on. Under unanimity, a single government can hold up a decision for years; the European Peace Facility's military aid, blocked from 2023 until September 2026, is a case in point.

The third issue is opting out. Three states neither blocked the €90 billion loan nor joined it. That resembles the founding scene of book 4, chapter 2, in which opponents of the compact do not invalidate it but become "foreigners among citizens." Yet Rousseau also insisted that the general will must "come from all and apply to all." A decision made by 24 states and binding only on those 24 looks less like the general will of the union than like a contract among a smaller association within it (this lecture's interpretation).

The fourth issue is size and representation. Germany's contribution is decided not by German citizens but by members of the Bundestag, who on Rousseau's account are only stewards. For Rousseau, however, a decision about a particular matter is an act of government rather than a law (book 2, chapters 4 and 6), so fixing the size of an aid package falls closer to what can be left to representatives (this lecture's interpretation). The fact that a sum pledged by the German government in Brussels must still clear the Bundestag reflects the two-level game examined in session 6 of Introduction to International Relations.

The limits of the analogy. First, Rousseau's general will concerns citizens within a single body politic and cannot simply be transferred to a union of states; the exercise above borrows the structure of the concept, nothing more. Second, Rousseau left no theory of confederation. In 1761 he published a summary of the Abbé de Saint-Pierre's plan for perpetual peace in Europe, but in a commentary published after his death he doubted that princes would ever accept such a union of their own accord. Third, the forecasts can show whether a decision is made, not whether it expresses the general will or merely the will of all. Fourth, the people most affected by these decisions, the citizens of Ukraine, have no vote in them. How Rousseau's requirement that the general will "apply to all" bears on that fact is left for discussion question 3.

Check your understanding

  1. What are the "natural" and the "moral or political" inequalities that Rousseau distinguishes, and why does he focus on the second?
  2. Explain the difference between amour de soi and amour-propre, and identify the scene in Part II of the Discourse in which amour-propre emerges.
  3. How does the "rich man's contract" criticized in the Discourse differ from the compact of book 1, chapter 6 of the Social Contract?
  4. What is the difference between the general will and the will of all, and why did Rousseau think partial associations obscure the general will?
  5. What are the two rules for voting that Rousseau sets out in book 4, chapter 2? What condition does he attach to his claim that an outvoted citizen remains free?

Discussion questions

  1. Does "forced to be free" express the rule of law or the seed of totalitarianism? Whose reading do you find more persuasive, Berlin's or Rousseau's own?
  2. Rousseau said that a people under representative government is free only while it votes. Can referendums, participatory budgeting, or citizens' assemblies answer his objection?
  3. Those most affected by the EU's decisions on aid to Ukraine have no vote in them. Should the principle that everyone affected by a decision ought to take part in it apply to decisions that cross borders?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages of Rousseau in a classic English translation, works through the effect of factions on collective judgment and the arithmetic of EU voting rules, and looks at research on the EU's democratic deficit.

Reading the original

The quotations in this session, both below and in the main text, are taken from G. D. H. Cole's English translation (The Social Contract & Discourses, Everyman's Library, 1913; Project Gutenberg eBook 46333), with omissions marked by ellipses.

Passage 1. The rich man's contract (Discourse on Inequality, Part II)

All ran headlong to their chains, in hopes of securing their liberty; for they had just wit enough to perceive the advantages of political institutions, without experience enough to enable them to foresee the dangers. The most capable of fore-seeing the dangers were the very persons who expected to benefit by them ...

How to read it. Rousseau does not present the origin of civil society as historical fact. In the Introduction he warns that his inquiry offers "mere conditional and hypothetical reasonings," not "historical truths." The force of the passage lies in its question about legitimacy: a contract can win everyone's agreement and still be unjust if one party wrote its terms. The Social Contract's insistence on total and equal alienation is designed precisely to rule out that kind of rigged bargain.

Passage 2. The general will and the will of all (Social Contract, book 2, chapter 3)

There is often a great deal of difference between the will of all and the general will; the latter considers only the common interest, while the former takes private interest into account, and is no more than a sum of particular wills: but take away from these same wills the pluses and minuses that cancel one another, and the general will remains as the sum of the differences. ... But when factions arise, and partial associations are formed at the expense of the great association, the will of each of these associations becomes general in relation to its members, while it remains particular in relation to the State: it may then be said that there are no longer as many votes as there are men, but only as many as there are associations.

How to read it. The cryptic phrase "the sum of the differences" has invited many readings. One, advanced by Bernard Grofman and Scott L. Feld in the American Political Science Review in 1988, holds that the private biases mixed into each person's judgment cancel out as numbers grow, leaving the common interest visible. Read this way, Rousseau's argument comes close to Condorcet's jury theorem, discussed in session 3, and Problem 1 below puts the reading into numbers. Members of the same faction do not judge independently, so the biases that ought to cancel instead pile up in one direction. That is why factions are dangerous. In an exchange in the same journal the following year, David Estlund and Jeremy Waldron objected that the reading assumes too much independence of judgment and misses Rousseau's point that the general will is the citizens' own will before it is a correct answer.

Passage 3. Representation and confederation (Social Contract, book 3, chapter 15)

Sovereignty, for the same reason as makes it inalienable, cannot be represented; it lies essentially in the general will, and will does not admit of representation ... All things considered, I do not see that it is possible henceforth for the Sovereign to preserve among us the exercise of its rights, unless the city is very small. But if it is very small, it will be conquered? No. I will show later on how the external strength of a great people may be combined with the convenient polity and good order of a small State.

(Rousseau's note) I had intended to do this in the sequel to this work, when in dealing with external relations I came to the subject of confederations. The subject is quite new, and its principles have still to be laid down.

How to read it. Rousseau attacked representation, but he also knew that the citizens of a large modern state could never all gather in the public square. His answer was a confederation of small republics, and he admitted that its principles "have still to be laid down." Modern federalism and the EU can be seen as attempts, each in its own way, to fill the gap he left (this lecture's interpretation).

Working it out

Problem 1. Factions and the accuracy of judgment. Nine citizens judge whether a proposed law serves the common interest. Each is right with a probability of 0.6. (1) If all nine judge independently and the question is decided by majority vote, what is the probability that the outcome is right? (2) Now suppose the nine form three factions of three. Each faction settles on one judgment internally (right with a probability of 0.6), and all three members vote that way; the factions judge independently of one another. What is the probability that the outcome is right? (3) What if five citizens vote together as one faction while the other four vote independently? (4) Compare the three results with the passage from book 2, chapter 3.

Problem 2. EU voting rules. The EU has 27 member states. Under the qualified majority rule described in section 7, a blocking minority based on population must include at least four member states. (1) Under unanimity, how many states are needed to block a proposal? Under qualified majority voting, how many states must vote yes? (2) Can three states with a combined 40 percent of the EU's population block a proposal under qualified majority voting? What about 13 states with a combined 10 percent?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. The majority is right if at least five of the nine are right. The probability that exactly k are right is C(9, k) × 0.6^k × 0.4^(9−k). For k = 5 it is about 0.2508; for k = 6, about 0.2508; for k = 7, about 0.1612; for k = 8, about 0.0605; and for k = 9, about 0.0101. Together they come to about 0.733.
  2. In effect there are only three votes. The probability that at least two of the three factions are right is 0.6³ + 3 × 0.6² × 0.4 = 0.216 + 0.432 = 0.648.
  3. A faction of five holds a majority on its own, so the outcome is simply that faction's judgment, which is right with a probability of 0.6.
  4. The number of citizens stays at nine, but as the number of independent judgments falls from nine to three to, in effect, one, accuracy drops from 0.733 to 0.648 to 0.6. In Rousseau's words, "the differences become less numerous and give a less general result," and when one association prevails over the rest, the result is "no longer a sum of small differences, but a single difference." The calculation assumes, however, that each citizen is trying to judge the common interest. If each votes for his own advantage, the outcome is no longer a matter of accuracy but of whose interest wins. That is the will of all.

Problem 2.

  1. Under unanimity, a single state suffices. Under qualified majority voting, 27 × 0.55 = 14.85, so 15 states must vote yes, and they must represent at least 65 percent of the population.
  2. Three states cannot block the proposal, however populous. The supporting states represent only 60 percent of the population, short of 65 percent, but because a blocking minority must include at least four states, the qualified majority is deemed to have been reached. Thirteen states, by contrast, can block regardless of population: the remaining 14 fall short of the 15 required. Together the two thresholds keep any single bloc among these unequal members (the kind of unequal associations book 2, chapter 3, warns against) from overriding the rest.

Evidence and counterarguments

Moravcsik (2002) versus Follesdal and Hix (2006): does the EU suffer from a democratic deficit? Andrew Moravcsik argued that the EU's powers are concentrated in areas such as regulation and trade that national democracies also insulate from electoral politics, that elected national governments make decisions in the Council, and that the European Parliament provides a check, so the EU's legitimacy is not markedly weaker than that of its member states (Journal of Common Market Studies 40, no. 4). Andreas Follesdal and Simon Hix replied that the EU lacks a European-level electoral contest through which voters could change its leadership and policy direction, leaving the link between citizens' preferences and EU policy weak (Journal of Common Market Studies 44, no. 3). Seen through Rousseau, Moravcsik treats the EU as a contract among governments, while Follesdal and Hix treat it as a polity that does not yet have a people (this lecture's interpretation). The fact that Ukraine's 2027 financing depends on consensus among leaders, enhanced cooperation among some members, and budget votes in national parliaments lends support to both readings.
