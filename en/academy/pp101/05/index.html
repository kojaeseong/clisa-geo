The question for this session

Aristotle held that human beings are political animals by nature and live a complete life only within a city (session 3). Thomas Hobbes starts from the opposite end. What would life be like if there were no state at all? His answer is bleak. Without a common power to keep them all in awe, people fall into a war "of every man, against every man," and their life is "solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short." To escape it, they covenant with one another to hand their rights to one person or one assembly, and they obey the sovereign they have created.

This session follows that argument through chapters 13 to 18 and chapter 21 of Leviathan. Its pivot is a single line from chapter 17: "Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words." The session then applies that line to a question that stands open as of November 6, 2026: if Russia and Ukraine agree to a ceasefire, can it hold without a sword to enforce it?

1. What kind of book is Leviathan?

Hobbes (1588–1679) was also a translator: in 1629 he published an English version of Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War, rendered directly from the Greek. In 1640, as the conflict between king and Parliament deepened, he feared for his safety and left for Paris. He published Leviathan in London in 1651, two years after the execution of Charles I. It is a book about what it would take never to live through a civil war again.

The title comes from the sea monster of Job 41. Hobbes explains that he borrowed the image from the passage in which God calls the creature "King of the Proud," a power with nothing on earth to compare with it (chapter 28). In the introduction he describes the state as an "Artificiall Man" built by human art, with sovereignty as the "Artificiall Soul" that gives the whole body life and motion.

2. The state of nature: the war of every man against every man

Hobbes's argument begins with equality. Even if one person is stronger than another, "the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others" (chapter 13, paragraph 1). Equal ability breeds equal hope of reaching one's ends, so when two people want the same thing and cannot both have it, they become enemies. In a condition of mutual distrust, the most reasonable way to stay safe is "Anticipation": mastering as many others as possible, by force or by guile, until no power remains that could threaten oneself (13.3–4).

Hobbes reduces this to three causes of quarrel: "First, Competition; Secondly, Diffidence; Thirdly, Glory." Competition makes people invade for gain, diffidence (distrust) for safety, and glory for reputation (13.6–7).

War is like the weather (13.8). For Hobbes, war is not only the act of fighting but the whole stretch of time in which the will to fight is sufficiently known. Just as foul weather lies not in a shower or two but in a tendency toward rain over many days, war lies in the known disposition to fight for as long as there is no assurance to the contrary. When the guns fall silent, peace has not necessarily arrived. The distinction returns in section 7.

In such a condition the fruits of labor are uncertain, so there is no industry, no agriculture, no trade, no learning, and only continual fear and danger of violent death: "And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short" (13.9). Nothing in this condition is unjust, for where there is no common power there is no law, and where there is no law there is no injustice (13.13).

3. Laws of nature and covenants: whoever performs first betrays himself

The way out of the state of nature lies partly in the passions and partly in reason. Fear of death and the desire for a comfortable life incline people to peace, and reason suggests the articles of peace that Hobbes calls the laws of nature (13.14). The first and fundamental law is "To seek Peace, and follow it." The second is to lay down one's right to all things, provided others do the same. Laying it down alone, while others keep theirs, would not be peace but an offer of oneself as prey (chapter 14).

A mutual transfer of rights is a contract, and a contract in which one or both parties are to perform later is a covenant. Here lies the central problem of this session. A covenant that requires each side to trust the other is void in the state of nature upon any reasonable suspicion. Whoever performs first has no assurance that the other will follow, and words alone, without fear of some coercive power, are too weak to bridle ambition, avarice, and anger. Whoever performs first "does but betray himselfe to his enemy." Under a civil power that can compel performance, that fear is no longer reasonable, and the party bound to perform first is obliged to do so (chapter 14, under the marginal heading "Covenants Of Mutuall Trust, When Invalid").

Hobbes adds two qualifications. First, the fear that voids a covenant must arise from something new after the covenant was made; a danger already known at the time of the promise is no excuse. Second, covenants made out of fear are binding: "if a weaker Prince, make a disadvantageous peace with a stronger, for feare; he is bound to keep it; unlesse (as hath been sayd before) there ariseth some new, and just cause of feare, to renew the war" (chapter 14, under the marginal heading "Covenants Extorted By Feare Are Valide").

The third law of nature, in chapter 15, is "That Men Performe Their Covenants Made," and justice begins here. But since covenants of mutual trust are void in the state of nature, "the Validity of Covenants begins not but with the Constitution of a Civill Power, sufficient to compell men to keep them." To the Fool, who asks why breaking a covenant is unreasonable when it pays, Hobbes replies that anyone who openly declares that he may break covenants cannot be received into any society united for peace and defense, and so cannot reasonably expect safety.

4. Covenants without the sword: the birth of Leviathan

Chapter 17 draws the conclusion. The laws of nature, such as justice, equity, and mercy, run against the passions that carry people toward partiality, pride, and revenge, unless some power makes them observed. "And Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all" (17.2). A small band of allies is not enough either. The size of a group sufficient for security is set not by any fixed number but by comparison with the enemy one fears, and it is sufficient only when the enemy's advantage is not so conspicuous as to tempt him to attack.

Bees and human beings. Here Hobbes takes direct aim at Aristotle. Bees and ants live sociably without any coercive power, and Aristotle counted them among political creatures. Why can people not do the same? Because people compete endlessly for honor, take joy only in being ahead of others, can use the art of words to make good look evil and evil look good, and, above all, reach agreement only by covenant, which is artificial rather than natural. Speech, which for Aristotle was the ground of politics, is for Hobbes also a source of conflict.

The only way out, then, is for everyone to confer their power on one person or one assembly, reducing many wills to one:

"I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy Right to him, and Authorise all his Actions in like manner." ... This is the Generation of that great LEVIATHAN, or rather (to speake more reverently) of that Mortall God, to which wee owe under the Immortall God, our peace and defence. (chapter 17)

Notice that the covenant is made among the subjects themselves, not between subjects and sovereign. Chapter 18 derives the sovereign's rights from this. Because the sovereign is not a party to the covenant, the sovereign cannot breach it. If some claim that the sovereign has broken a promise and others deny it, "there is in this case, no Judge to decide the controversie: it returns therefore to the Sword again." Covenants are "but words, and breath," and the power to protect anyone comes only from the public sword (chapter 18, under the marginal heading "Soveraigne Power Cannot Be Forfeited"). The sovereign judges the means of peace and defense, holds the power of judicature, and has "the Right of making Warre, and Peace with other Nations," and these rights cannot be divided. To the objection that life under such a power is miserable, Hobbes replies that the hardships of any government are scarcely felt beside the calamities of civil war.

5. The limits of obedience and the state of nature among states

The Hobbesian sovereign is strong but not unlimited. Since people transferred their rights to preserve their lives, they cannot transfer the rights that would defeat that purpose. If the sovereign orders a man to harm himself or not to resist those who assault him, he is at liberty to disobey. The same holds for military service: in many cases a man may refuse without injustice, for instance by supplying a substitute. Those who enlist voluntarily, however, are bound, and so is everyone when the defense of the commonwealth requires all who can bear arms (chapter 21; compare the discussion of conscientious objection in session 1). And Hobbes is clear about where obedience ends:

The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them. ... The end of Obedience is Protection ... (21.21)

The posture of gladiators. What, then, of relations between states? Answering the objection that the state of nature never existed, Hobbes writes:

...yet in all times, Kings, and persons of Soveraigne authority, because of their Independency, are in continuall jealousies, and in the state and posture of Gladiators; having their weapons pointing, and their eyes fixed on one another; that is, their Forts, Garrisons, and Guns upon the Frontiers of their Kingdomes; and continuall Spyes upon their neighbours; which is a posture of War. But because they uphold thereby, the Industry of their Subjects; there does not follow from it, that misery, which accompanies the Liberty of particular men. (13.12)

In chapter 30 he adds that "the Law of Nations, and the Law of Nature, is the same thing," with "no Court of Naturall Justice, but in the Conscience onely." Such passages led twentieth-century realists in international relations to claim Hobbes as a founder, and the logic of striking first out of mutual distrust leads straight to the security dilemma discussed in session 2 of Introduction to International Relations. Yet Hobbes himself said that the state of war among sovereigns is less miserable than that among individuals, and he never called for a single Leviathan to rule the world. At home the sword was his answer; between states he did not prescribe it.

6. Classics and theory: the debate over the state of nature

Hobbes's state of nature became the starting point that later thinkers each rewrote in their own way.

Thinker Argument Relation to Hobbes
Locke, Second Treatise of Government (1689) The state of nature has its own law of nature, which everyone may enforce; the state of nature and the state of war are different things Paints a less dark state of nature and derives limited government and a right of resistance from it (session 6)
Rousseau, Discourse on the Origin of Inequality (1755) The greed and vanity Hobbes attributes to natural man are passions produced by society Rejects Hobbes's very picture of the state of nature (session 7)
Kant, Perpetual Peace (1795) States, too, must leave the lawless state of nature, but through a federation of free states rather than a world state Accepts the diagnosis for relations between states but rejects a Leviathan as the cure (session 10)
Waltz, Theory of International Politics (1979) An international system with no government above states is a self-help system Locates the logic of anarchy in the structure of the system rather than in human nature
Bull, The Anarchical Society (1977) Even without government, states form a "society" with common rules and institutions such as international law, diplomacy, and the balance of power Rejects the inference from no government to no order, partly on the ground that states are less vulnerable than individuals

7. Applying it to 2026: can a Russia-Ukraine ceasefire hold without a guarantor's sword?

The facts. In 2026, Russia and Ukraine observed three short truces, at Easter in April, on Victory Day in May, and from September 5 to 8, and each ended with a return to fighting (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Russia-Ukraine War). On October 11, President Donald Trump wrote on Truth Social that an "energy ceasefire" agreed by both sides was taking effect immediately. Moscow did not confirm any agreement, and Ukraine's presidential office confirmed that Russia struck a power plant in Kharkiv that evening, after the announcement (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, October 12, 2026, via Time and PBS). The next day President Volodymyr Zelensky accused Russia of violating the truce. On October 13, asked whether Russia had broken it, Trump said he would let reporters know later and noted that Ukraine had also hit many targets the day before (Briefings of October 13 and 14).

Moscow has attached conditions to any full ceasefire. On October 12, after Zelensky proposed a 60-day full ceasefire so that Ukraine could hold elections, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said that President Vladimir Putin was prepared to halt attacks only on election day and claimed that Ukraine wanted to use a truce to recover its strength (Briefing, October 13, via PBS and The Moscow Times). On October 30, he said Russia opposed any ceasefire that would give Ukraine a breathing spell and had no plans to stop fighting before trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi (Briefing, October 31, via TASS). On November 2, a senior U.S. official told Reuters that Washington planned to host Ukrainian and Russian delegations in Abu Dhabi on November 10 and 11 to discuss postwar security arrangements and an end to the war, among other topics (Briefing, November 3).

The sword most often proposed is a multinational force. On January 6, 2026, 35 countries meeting in Paris formalized plans for a Multinational Force Ukraine (MNF-U) led by France and Britain, on the condition that a comprehensive ceasefire come first (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Russia-Ukraine War). Russia rejects any Western troop presence. On September 5, 2025, Putin said that foreign troops deployed to Ukraine would be legitimate targets (as reported by the AP), and the only arrangement Russia has said it would accept is an unarmed civilian monitoring mission (Russia-Ukraine analysis). The precedents are sobering. The 1994 Budapest Memorandum promised respect for Ukraine's territorial integrity in return for its nuclear disarmament but had no means of enforcement. During the Minsk period of 2014–2022, unarmed monitors were on the ground, yet about 310,000 ceasefire violations were recorded in 2018 alone (precedents section of the Russia-Ukraine analysis).

Through Hobbes's lens. A ceasefire is a covenant in which each side must trust the other to follow through, precisely the covenant of mutual trust described in chapter 14. Whichever side lays down its arms first fears that the other will use the pause to rearm. The argument about a breathing spell that Moscow keeps invoking expresses that fear, and the fear Ukraine and Europe hold about Russia has the same structure. As long as there is no common power, Hobbes would regard such suspicion as reasonable (this lecture's interpretation).

The October energy truce exposed a further problem. When violations were alleged, there was no judge to decide who had broken the agreement, and the mediator declined to say. Hobbes's remark in chapter 18, made about domestic politics, fits the episode: without a judge, the matter "returns therefore to the Sword again" (this lecture's interpretation). Hobbes's question, then, is less what words a ceasefire document contains than whether a force able to uphold those words actually stands on the line. Clisa Geopolitics' Russia-Ukraine analysis reaches a similar judgment: whether a ceasefire lasts depends more on troops actually deployed than on signatures. A problem of sequence compounds the difficulty. In Hobbes's civil state, the power exists first, which is why the first performer is obliged to perform. The multinational force planned in Paris, however, would deploy only after a ceasefire takes hold. The sword cannot arrive until the covenant exists, and the covenant cannot hold until the sword arrives.

In fairness, the argument can also run the other way. Chapter 14 holds that a weaker prince who makes a disadvantageous peace out of fear is bound to keep it, so Hobbes could be read as favoring an end to the war even at the cost of territory. Yet the same chapter says that a covenant loses its force when a new and just cause of fear arises, and chapter 21, speaking of individuals, says that the right of self-defense can be surrendered by no covenant. Chapter 14, however, also holds that a danger already known when a covenant is made cannot later excuse breaking it. The full-scale invasion of 2022, which followed the annexation of Crimea in 2014, is exactly such a known danger; on Hobbes's logic it is grounds for demanding guarantees before signing rather than for breaking a ceasefire afterward (this lecture's interpretation). Hobbes's framework does not deliver a single conclusion.

The limits of the analogy. First, a multinational force is not a Leviathan. Hobbes's sovereign stands above both parties and compels them both, whereas the multinational force would be one side's troops, which Russia regards as hostile. It would deter rather than compel; in the language of chapter 17, it would resemble a band large enough that the enemy's advantage is no longer conspicuous enough to tempt an attack. Second, the premise of equality, that the weakest can kill the strongest, does not straightforwardly apply to a great power with nuclear weapons. In a diplomatic note to NATO members on September 30, Russia warned that if Kaliningrad were blockaded it was prepared to use all weapons, including nuclear weapons (Briefing, October 2, via Al Jazeera and ABC News). Because guarantors also fear escalation, confidence that their sword would actually be used is bound to be weaker than confidence in a domestic sword. Third, Hobbes himself said that the condition among states is less miserable than that among individuals, and many ceasefires have in fact held without outside guarantees (see the empirical studies in Going deeper). Fourth, the 30 days in forecast f28 below is a short span for judging what Hobbes called the weather.

Check your understanding

  1. What are Hobbes's three causes of quarrel, and for what end does each lead people to invade others?
  2. What is Hobbes trying to show by comparing war to the weather? By that definition, how does a ceasefire differ from peace?
  3. Why are covenants of mutual trust void in the state of nature, and what changes once a commonwealth is established?
  4. Why does Hobbes think human beings, unlike bees and ants, cannot live together without a coercive power? Compare his answer with Aristotle's "political animal" from session 3.
  5. Give two cases in which subjects need not obey the sovereign's commands, and state in one sentence the condition under which the duty of obedience ends.

Discussion questions

  1. Hobbes held that even a disadvantageous peace made out of fear must be kept. Is a peace bought by giving up territory a promise that must be kept? Consider the question alongside Article 73 of Ukraine's constitution, which allows changes to the country's territory only by a national referendum.
  2. The 1953 Korean Armistice Agreement has held for more than 70 years. What kept it in place: the wording of the agreement, or the "sword" of U.S. Forces Korea and the U.S.-South Korea Mutual Defense Treaty? What does the Ukrainian case share with it, and how does it differ?
  3. Is the absence of a Leviathan in international society a misfortune or a blessing? Between a world government and Kant's federation of states, which is more desirable, and which is more achievable?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages of Leviathan in the original, works through with numbers the conditions under which the sword makes covenants hold, and looks at research that has tested Hobbes's claims, along with the counterarguments.

Reading the original

The quotations from Leviathan in this session, both below and in the main text, are taken from the 1651 English text that Hobbes himself wrote (Project Gutenberg eBook 3207), with the original spelling preserved and omissions marked by ellipses. The numbers after each chapter refer to the order of paragraphs within it.

Passage 1. The war of every man against every man (Leviathan, chapter 13, paragraphs 8–9)

Hereby it is manifest, that during the time men live without a common Power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called Warre; and such a warre, as is of every man, against every man. For WARRE, consisteth not in Battell onely, or the act of fighting; but in a tract of time, wherein the Will to contend by Battell is sufficiently known ... For as the nature of Foule weather, lyeth not in a showre or two of rain; but in an inclination thereto of many dayes together: So the nature of War, consisteth not in actuall fighting; but in the known disposition thereto, during all the time there is no assurance to the contrary. All other time is PEACE. ... In such condition, there is no place for Industry; because the fruit thereof is uncertain ... and which is worst of all, continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short.

How to read it. The last sentence is the one usually quoted, but the heart of the passage is the weather analogy before it. Without assurance that fighting will not resume, a silence of the guns does not end the war (section 2). Industry and trade vanish less because of actual battles than because no one can count on tomorrow.

Passage 2. Whoever performs first (Leviathan, chapter 14, under the marginal heading "Covenants Of Mutuall Trust, When Invalid")

If a Covenant be made, wherein neither of the parties performe presently, but trust one another; in the condition of meer Nature, (which is a condition of Warre of every man against every man,) upon any reasonable suspition, it is Voyd; But if there be a common Power set over them bothe, with right and force sufficient to compell performance; it is not Voyd. For he that performeth first, has no assurance the other will performe after; because the bonds of words are too weak to bridle mens ambition, avarice, anger, and other Passions, without the feare of some coerceive Power; which in the condition of meer Nature, where all men are equall, and judges of the justnesse of their own fears cannot possibly be supposed. And therefore he which performeth first, does but betray himselfe to his enemy; contrary to the Right (he can never abandon) of defending his life, and means of living.

How to read it. Hobbes does not trace broken promises to malice. Even people of good will cannot perform first if they have no grounds for trusting the other side. The problem is compounded because each person judges for himself whether his fears are justified: what one side sees as reasonable caution, the other sees as evidence of bad faith. The passage describes the commitment problem that arises when no enforcement mechanism exists (see sessions 5 and 8 of Introduction to International Relations, on the commitment problem and third-party guarantees).

Passage 3. Covenants without the sword (Leviathan, chapter 17, paragraph 2)

For the Lawes of Nature (as Justice, Equity, Modesty, Mercy, and (in summe) Doing To Others, As Wee Would Be Done To,) if themselves, without the terrour of some Power, to cause them to be observed, are contrary to our naturall Passions, that carry us to Partiality, Pride, Revenge, and the like. And Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all. Therefore notwithstanding the Lawes of Nature, (which every one hath then kept, when he has the will to keep them, when he can do it safely,) if there be no Power erected, or not great enough for our security; every man will and may lawfully rely on his own strength and art, for caution against all other men.

How to read it. The parenthetical clause matters. Hobbes does not say that people never keep the laws of nature; he says they keep them when they are willing and can do so safely. The trouble comes when keeping them is not safe. Later in the same paragraph he extends the logic to states, observing that cities and kingdoms enlarge their dominions and weaken their neighbors on the pretext of fearing invasion.

Working it out

Problem 1. How big must the sword be? Two countries have agreed to a ceasefire. Each can keep it or break it, with the following payoffs. If both keep it, each gets 3; if both break it, each gets 1; if only one breaks it, the one that breaks it gets 5 and the one that keeps it gets 0. (1) Show that, without any enforcement, breaking the ceasefire is better whatever the other side does. (2) Suppose a guarantor imposes a loss of c on any side that breaks the ceasefire. How large must c be for keeping it to be better whatever the other side does? Compare the result with chapter 15's "terrour of some punishment, greater than the benefit they expect by the breach of their Covenant." (3) Suppose the guarantor actually intervenes with probability q, imposing a loss of 4 when it does. How large must q be?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. If the other side keeps the ceasefire, a country gets 3 by keeping it and 5 by breaking it. If the other side breaks it, a country gets 0 by keeping it and 1 by breaking it. Either way, breaking is better, and if both reason this way, each ends up with 1 instead of the 3 they would have had by both keeping it. Hobbes's state of nature, in today's terms, is a prisoner's dilemma.
  2. The payoffs for breaking become 5 − c and 1 − c. Keeping is better when the other side keeps if 3 > 5 − c, or c > 2, and better when the other side breaks if 0 > 1 − c, or c > 1. Keeping is therefore always better when c > 2. Here 2 is the extra gain from breaking when the other side keeps (5 − 3). Just as Hobbes said, the threatened punishment must exceed the benefit expected from the breach.
  3. The expected loss, q × 4, must exceed 2, so q > 0.5. However large the punishment, if few believe it will actually be imposed, the sword turns back into words. This is why the same troops deter less when a guarantor's fear of escalation lowers the probability that it will intervene.

Evidence and counterarguments

Axelrod (1984): cooperation without the sword. In The Evolution of Cooperation, Robert Axelrod ran computer tournaments showing that in a repeated prisoner's dilemma, a simple strategy of cooperating when the other side cooperates and answering a defection with one defection (tit for tat) performed well. He cited the "live and let live" system on the Western Front in World War I, where soldiers facing each other across the trenches held back from attacking, contrary to the wishes of their commanders. Cooperation can emerge without a higher power when the same parties face each other for a long time and value the future. It is one of the strongest rebuttals of Hobbes, but because cooperation collapses once repetition and the shadow of the future disappear, it does not entirely dispel his warning.

Fortna (2003): what makes ceasefires last. Virginia Page Fortna analyzed 48 ceasefires in interstate wars between 1946 and 1997. War resumed in 21 cases, while 27 were still holding when her data ended in early 1998 (International Organization 57, no. 2). The results partly support Hobbes. No ceasefire failed when an outside state had explicitly guaranteed it, and a demilitarized zone at least two kilometers wide along the full ceasefire line reduced the risk of renewed war by about 90 percent. But the findings also point elsewhere. Across all cases, unarmed monitors had a statistically clear effect while armed peacekeepers did not (counting only missions newly deployed for the war in question, both had large effects), and joint commissions of the two sides' representatives also proved effective. Design matters, not only the sword. It remains to be examined whether guarantees were given mainly to ceasefires that were easier to keep, and the sample includes almost no cases in which a nuclear-armed great power was itself a belligerent.
