The question for this session

Rawls and Nozick reached opposite conclusions in session 12, but they started from the same place: principles of justice must come from free individuals who choose their own ends in life. In the early 1980s, a handful of philosophers attacked that very starting point. People are born into families, hometowns, nations, and traditions, they argued, and those attachments already go a long way toward settling who a person is. The critique is usually called communitarianism, although the four thinkers taken up here, Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor, and Walzer, all disliked the label and stand at different distances from liberalism.

This session follows their arguments in turn and then closes the course with Bernard Crick's In Defence of Politics (1962), which treats politics as the way people with conflicting interests manage to be governed together without undue violence. Finally, it turns to the Korean Peninsula as of November 6, 2026. What does it mean to belong to a divided nation, and where do the obligations owed to separated families come from?

1. The unencumbered self: Sandel's critique of Rawls

In A Theory of Justice (1971), Rawls imagined people choosing principles of justice behind a "veil of ignorance" that hides their talents, class, and values (session 12). Underlying the device is the claim that "the self is prior to the ends which are affirmed by it" (section 84). Whatever ends a person holds, he or she can step back from them, revise them, or drop them.

Michael Sandel attacked this picture head-on in Liberalism and the Limits of Justice (1982), and two years later, in an essay, he named it the "unencumbered self." He made two points.

First, such a self cannot account for moral experience as people actually live it. Being a member of a particular family or a citizen of a particular country is not a possession one happens to have chosen; it is part of what makes a person who he or she is. Treat those attachments as a coat to be shed at will, and loyalty and solidarity become inexplicable.

Second, Rawls's theory quietly borrows what it forbids. The difference principle treats the distribution of natural talents as a "common asset." But why should my talents be an asset I share with others? The only answer, Sandel argues, is that they and I already form a community whose members share a common fate, and the unencumbered self rules out such a community from the start.

Sandel distinguishes three conceptions of community: an instrumental one, in which individuals cooperate to pursue private ends; a sentimental one, in which members feel affection for one another; and a constitutive one, in which the community forms part of its members' very identity. Rawls, on his diagnosis, gets as far as the second but never reaches the third.

2. The narrative self and rights in society: MacIntyre and Taylor

In After Virtue (1981), Alasdair MacIntyre treats a human life as a story. One can answer the question "What am I to do?" only by first answering a prior one: "Of what story or stories do I find myself a part?" (chapter 15). From the past of one's family, city, tribe, and nation, he holds, a person inherits debts and legacies, rightful expectations and obligations.

His examples are pointed. A young German born after 1945 who says that what the Nazis did to the Jews has nothing to do with him, or an American who denies any responsibility for the effects of slavery because he never owned slaves, displays for MacIntyre the characteristic stance of modern individualism, and in doing so disowns part of his own identity. By the same logic, what a community hands down is a burden as well as a source of pride.

Charles Taylor reaches the same destination by another road in "Atomism" (1979). His target is the "primacy of rights" doctrine, the view that individual rights exist prior to and independent of society. To assert a right, Taylor argues, is to affirm the worth of the capacity it protects, such as the capacity to judge for oneself and choose one's way of life. Yet such capacities develop only within a society that sustains language and culture, public debate, and institutions. Anyone who asserts rights must therefore also accept an obligation to belong to, and help sustain, the society that nurtures them. It is Aristotle's claim from session 3, that a being able to live outside the community is "a beast or a god" (Politics, 1253a), restated in the language of modern rights theory.

3. Membership as the first good: Walzer

In Spheres of Justice (1983), Michael Walzer argues that money, office, education, health care, and honor must each be distributed by its own criteria, and that "complex equality" obtains when advantage in one sphere does not dominate the others.

The book's second chapter, "Membership," deals with the good that comes before all other distributions: "membership in some human community." Only once a community knows who its members are can it decide what to distribute among them and how. Walzer compares the state with three kinds of association. A neighborhood is open to anyone who moves in; a club chooses its new members; a family owes obligations even to relatives it did not choose. States resemble clubs in controlling admission, but they also resemble families, he observes, in feeling special obligations to "national relatives," as Greece and Germany did when they took in ethnic kin expelled from neighboring countries.

Walzer treats a community's right to choose its members as central to its self-determination, but he sets two limits on it. One is the principle of mutual aid: strangers in desperate need should be helped when that can be done without great cost. The other is that people admitted to live and work in a country must be offered a path to citizenship. A state that keeps guest workers permanently outside citizenship creates a caste within its own membership.

4. Obligations of solidarity versus universal duties: Sandel's Justice

In chapter 9 of Justice (2009) and in lectures 21 and 22 of his Harvard course, Sandel divides moral responsibility into three kinds. Natural duties, such as the duty to respect persons and not to harm them, are owed to everyone and require no consent. Voluntary obligations arise from promises and contracts, and so from consent. Obligations of solidarity, owed to particular people such as family members, fellow citizens, or one's nation, arise without consent yet are not universal. Liberals such as Kant and Rawls recognize only the first two; Sandel argues that without the third, people's actual moral judgments make no sense.

His cases are memorable. In 1861 Robert E. Lee was offered command of the Union army but declined out of loyalty to his native Virginia and joined the Confederacy. William Bulger, then president of the University of Massachusetts, declined to help federal investigators find his fugitive older brother, James "Whitey" Bulger. David Kaczynski, by contrast, came to suspect that his older brother Ted was the Unabomber and, after agonizing over it, told the authorities. Sandel does not endorse Lee's choice, but he takes the fact that it posed an agonizing moral dilemma as evidence that obligations of solidarity are real.

The tension is old. At one end stands Rousseau's Emile, with its line that "every patriot hates foreigners" (see Passage 2 in Going deeper); at the other stands Kant's cosmopolitanism (session 10). In lecture 24 of the model course, Smith takes up Kant together with the novelist E. M. Forster and treats patriotism, nationalism, and cosmopolitanism as questions political life cannot avoid. Forster wrote in "What I Believe" (1938) that if he had to choose between betraying his country and betraying his friend, he hoped he would have the courage to betray his country. Liberals reply that special obligations to fellow citizens can be explained as a division of labor in carrying out universal duties (see the counterargument in Going deeper).

5. In defense of politics: Crick, and the course in retrospect

Where communitarians asked how thick a community must be, the British political scientist Bernard Crick came at the problem from the other side: how can people with different beliefs and interests live together when they will never become one? In In Defence of Politics, he defined politics as the activity by which "differing interests within a given unit of rule are conciliated," by giving each a share of power in proportion to its importance to the welfare and survival of the whole community. His shorter formulation: "Politics is a way of ruling divided societies without undue violence."

Crick traced the idea to Aristotle, who in book 2 of the Politics criticizes Plato for trying to make the city too unified. Past a certain point, Aristotle says, a city that keeps reducing itself to one either ceases to be a city or, still a city, becomes a worse one; it is "as if any one should reduce the voices of those who sing in concert to one, or a verse to a foot" (book 2, chapter 5, 1263b). A city is by nature a gathering of different people. Crick made that diversity the premise of politics and saw a threat to politics in any ideology that would end all conflict with a single truth, in the urge to turn political judgment into technical problem-solving, and in demands for the complete unity of the nation.

His definition keeps a subtle distance from communitarianism. Crick's politics does not require deep agreement about the good life. It requires only the minimal agreement to accept the adversary's right to exist and to compromise under common rules. The German jurist Carl Schmitt, whom Smith discusses alongside Crick, located the essence of the political in the distinction between friend and enemy. For Crick, politics is precisely the work of softening that distinction until enemies become rivals.

The course in retrospect. Three questions have run through these 13 sessions. The first is why anyone should obey. Socrates pointed to the benefits the city had given him and to his own promise (session 1); Hobbes pointed to security and Locke to consent (sessions 5 and 6); Rousseau held that obeying laws one has made oneself is freedom (session 7); and Kant grounded obligation in the laws reason gives itself (session 10). The second is who should rule, and how. Plato's rule of philosophers (session 2), Aristotle's rule of law and middle class (session 3), Machiavelli's virtù and fortune (session 4), and Tocqueville's majority and its checks (session 8) followed in turn. The third is what justice is. Plato answered with harmony in the soul and the city (session 2), utilitarianism put utility first (session 9), Marx class relations (session 11), Rawls and Nozick fairness and rights (session 12), and communitarianism belonging and solidarity. None of these answers has driven out the others. The politics Crick defended is not one more answer on the list; it is the way people who hold these different answers can live together in one country without violence (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Classics and theory: after the communitarian debate

The communitarian critique did less to topple liberalism than to force liberals to explain themselves anew.

Thinker Argument Relation to communitarianism and Crick
Rawls, Political Liberalism (1993) A conception of justice should rest not on a particular view of the self but on political ideas shared by citizens; different religions and philosophies can support it through an "overlapping consensus" Narrows liberalism's foundations to the political sphere, sidestepping Sandel's critique of the self
Kymlicka, Liberalism, Community, and Culture (1989) and Multicultural Citizenship (1995) People can revise their ends, but they choose within a cultural context, so protecting minority cultures is itself a liberal requirement Brings the value of community inside liberalism
Habermas, "Citizenship and National Identity" (1990) Citizens can be bound together not by descent or culture but by allegiance to democratic constitutional principles, or "constitutional patriotism" Accepts solidarity but moves its basis from the nation to the constitution
Arendt, The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951) No one protected the human rights of those who lost their nationality; the most basic right is the right to belong to a political community, the "right to have rights" Treats membership itself as a precondition of human rights, well before Walzer

7. Applying it to 2026: what does it mean to belong to a divided nation?

The facts. According to Ministry of Unification data, 32,948 of the 134,843 people who had applied for family reunions, or 24.4 percent, were still alive as of August 2026 (as reported by the Seoul Shinmun on September 23, 2026). At the end of November 2025, the figure stood at 34,368, with an average age of 82.7 (ministry data released by the office of lawmaker Lee Jae-jung and reported on January 18, 2026). No government-level reunion has taken place since August 2018, more than eight years ago (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Korean Peninsula issue).

At its Ninth Party Congress in February 2026, North Korea's ruling party formally adopted a "two-state" doctrine that defines the Republic of Korea as a "hostile state" (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Korean Peninsula issue). On October 2, Kim Yo Jong, who heads the General Affairs Department of the Workers' Party, declared that Pyongyang would allow not the slightest room for dealing with South Korea in any form. On October 6, North Korea's Foreign Ministry said Pyongyang would under no circumstances discuss questions of military sovereignty on its territory with what it called a hostile state. In Seoul, President Lee Jae-myung used his Armed Forces Day speech on October 1 to urge the North to restore dialogue, and on October 23 the Ministry of National Defense proposed military talks on verifying the Military Demarcation Line and restoring military communication lines. Pyongyang did not respond; six days later, on October 29, it fired about 10 short-range ballistic missiles (Clisa Geopolitics Briefings of October 2, 3, 7, 24, and 29, 2026).

The legal framework matters here. Article 3 of South Korea's Constitution defines the national territory as the Korean Peninsula and its adjacent islands, and the Supreme Court has consistently held that North Koreans are nationals of the Republic of Korea. The 1991 Basic Agreement described inter-Korean relations as not a relationship between states but "a special interim relationship" (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Korean Peninsula issue). South Korean attitudes are shifting as well. In the 2026 Unification Perception Survey by Seoul National University's Institute for Peace and Unification Studies (in-person interviews with 1,200 adults in July), 39.0 percent said unification was necessary, down from 41.1 percent a year earlier, and 31.5 percent preferred the status quo, the highest share since the survey began (as reported by Yonhap on September 28, 2026).

Through Walzer's lens. Two rules of membership collide on the peninsula. South Korea's Constitution and courts treat North Koreans as South Korean nationals; North Korea's ruling party defines South Korea as a foreign and hostile state. Separated families live between those two rules. They are the group in which Walzer's family-type obligation, owed to relatives one did not choose, survives in its purest form, yet honoring it requires the consent of the other state. Seoul's responsibility to them rests on two grounds: a state's duty to relieve the suffering of its own citizens, and an obligation of solidarity toward members of the same nation (this lecture's interpretation).

Solidarity versus universalism. On this issue, the two camps largely converge. Communitarians explain family reunions as an obligation a divided nation owes itself; liberal universalists explain them through the human right of the family to protection by society and the state (Article 16(3) of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights). The differences lie in priority and price. One view holds that because the deaths of elderly family members are an irreversible loss, reunions should come first even if North Korea must be given something in return. Another holds that rewarding a regime that is expanding its nuclear arsenal conflicts with the broader duty to protect the safety of 51 million citizens. On September 29, 2026, the National Assembly's National Defense Committee failed to adopt a resolution condemning the North Korean land-mine incident because the parties could not agree on wording calling for a review of planned aid to the North, a sign that this disagreement runs through South Korean politics itself (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Korean Peninsula issue). Neither side can claim a monopoly on morality.

Through Crick's lens. For Crick, politics is the conciliation of interests within a single unit of rule, and no such common rule exists between the two Koreas. The "special interim relationship" of 1991 was an attempt to build a minimal shared framework, neither state-to-state nor wholly domestic, and liaison channels and reunions were signs that it worked. By Crick's standard, Pyongyang's refusal to deal with a "hostile state" comes close to rejecting politics altogether and returning to Schmitt's friend-enemy distinction (this lecture's interpretation). Crick would put a question to Seoul as well, however. Treating complete unification as the only legitimate goal can also shrink the space for politics, which is the search for ways to live alongside an adversary one cannot absorb.

The limits of the analogy. First, Sandel, MacIntyre, and Walzer had in mind communities within stable democracies such as the United States, not a nation divided for nearly 80 years and facing a nuclear-armed adversary. Second, Walzer's theory of membership concerns whom a state admits, whereas the Korean question is whether two states recognize each other's rules of membership. Third, the forecasts below track only government-level actions and do not answer the moral question of what is owed; private confirmations arranged through third countries do not count toward f16.

Discussion questions

  1. If Germans born after 1945 bear some responsibility for Nazi crimes, what responsibility do South Koreans bear for wrongs committed in the past by their own government or military? Can such responsibility arise without individual consent?
  2. Is a government justified in caring less for foreign refugees than for its own citizens? Under Walzer's principle of mutual aid, how far does South Korea's obligation to admit them extend?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads classic passages that lie at the roots of communitarian thought, uses the number of surviving separated-family members to calculate the cost of time, and reviews empirical research on solidarity.

Reading the original

Because the modern works discussed in this session remain under copyright, the passages below come from two classics now in the public domain, together with Crick's short definition of politics. Burke is quoted from his own English (The Works of Edmund Burke, vol. 3, Project Gutenberg eBook 15679) and Rousseau from Barbara Foxley's English translation (1911; Project Gutenberg eBook 5427), with omissions marked by ellipses.

Passage 1. A partnership of the living, the dead, and the unborn (Burke, Reflections on the Revolution in France, 1790)

Society is, indeed, a contract. ... but the state ought not to be considered as nothing better than a partnership agreement in a trade of pepper and coffee, calico or tobacco, or some other such low concern ... It is a partnership in all science, a partnership in all art, a partnership in every virtue and in all perfection. As the ends of such a partnership cannot be obtained in many generations, it becomes a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born.

How to read it. Burke borrows the language of social contract theory (sessions 5 to 7) to overturn its conclusion: even if the state is a contract, it is not one the living parties may dissolve at will. MacIntyre's inherited obligations come close to rebuilding this idea without its religious premises. Applied to separated families, it suggests that family bonds formed before the division still place obligations on the state in later generations (this lecture's interpretation).

Passage 2. Man or citizen? (Rousseau, Emile, book 1, 1762)

Forced to combat either nature or society, you must make your choice between the man and the citizen, you cannot train both. ... Every patriot hates foreigners; they are only men, and nothing to him. ... Distrust those cosmopolitans who search out remote duties in their books and neglect those that lie nearest. Such philosophers will love the Tartars to avoid loving their neighbour.

How to read it. Rousseau does not hide the tension between particular solidarity and universal humanity. Patriotism strengthens a community but hardens it toward outsiders; cosmopolitanism is broad but tends to neglect the duties owed to those nearest at hand.

Passage 3. Crick's definition of politics (In Defence of Politics, 1962)

Politics is a way of ruling divided societies without undue violence.

How to read it. The definition has three elements: acknowledge that society is divided, govern it nonetheless as a single unit of rule, and reduce violence even where it cannot be eliminated. It offers no answer about the good life. Crick's claim was that people can live together without one, and that the art of doing so is politics.

Working it out

Problem 1. The cost of time. According to Ministry of Unification data, 34,368 separated-family members were alive at the end of November 2025 and 32,948 in August 2026. Treat the interval as nine months. (1) If the rate of decline over this period held for a full year, what would the annual rate of decline be? (2) If that rate continued, how many would be alive at the end of 2028, the deadline for forecast f18 (about 2.33 years after August 2026)? (3) Clisa Geopolitics projects about 20,000 survivors in 2030. Taking the span from August 2026 to the end of 2030 as about 4.33 years, what annual rate of decline does that projection assume, and why might it differ from your answer to (1)?

Problem 2. Classifying obligations. Using Sandel's three categories (natural duties, voluntary obligations, and obligations of solidarity), say where each of the following belongs and explain why: (1) the duty to save a foreign child drowning in front of you; (2) the duty to pay the rent you agreed to; (3) the South Korean government's duty to try to learn the fate of South Korean prisoners of war and abductees held in the North; (4) a duty to provide humanitarian aid to North Korea in order to secure family reunions.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. Over nine months the ratio is 32,948 ÷ 34,368 ≈ 0.9587. Extended to 12 months, 0.9587^(12/9) ≈ 0.9454, an annual decline of about 5.5 percent.
  2. 32,948 × 0.9454^2.33 ≈ 32,948 × 0.877 ≈ 28,900. At that rate, about 4,000 people would die by the end of 2028 without a chance to meet their families.
  3. (20,000 ÷ 32,948)^(1/4.33) ≈ 0.607^0.231 ≈ 0.891, an annual decline of about 11 percent, nearly double the rate in (1). In a population whose average age is past 80, mortality rises every year, so the constant rate assumed in (1) probably understates the real decline. Either way, every year reunions are postponed adds to a cost that cannot be undone. Obligations of solidarity, in other words, have a deadline (this lecture's interpretation).

Problem 2.

  1. A natural duty: it is owed to anyone, regardless of nationality or consent.
  2. A voluntary obligation: it arises from the consent expressed in a contract.
  3. It has two possible grounds. Seen as flowing from a constitutional order that promises to protect citizens, it resembles a voluntary obligation; seen as what a community owes its members, it is an obligation of solidarity.
  4. This is the most contested case. Communitarians can explain it as an obligation of solidarity, liberals as a duty flowing from the human right of families to protection. But if the aid strengthens a regime that violates others' rights, neither camp can easily call the duty unconditional. The absence of a single right answer is itself why Crick's politics of conciliation is needed.

Evidence and counterarguments

Putnam (1993): civic community and good government. In Making Democracy Work, Robert D. Putnam tracked the regional governments Italy created in 1970 over two decades and found that those in the north, where traditions of association and civic engagement were strong, performed better. The study is often cited in support of the communitarian worry that democracy weakens when community does, though critics have questioned its measures and the direction of causation.

Alesina and Glaeser (2004) and Banting and Kymlicka (2006): solidarity and the welfare state. David Miller has argued that citizens accept redistribution only when bound by a sense of national solidarity. Alberto Alesina and Edward L. Glaeser reported that racial diversity accounts for much of the gap between redistribution in the United States and in Europe (Fighting Poverty in the US and Europe). A volume edited by Keith Banting and Will Kymlicka, however, found no consistent evidence that countries with strong multicultural policies saw their welfare states erode (Multiculturalism and the Welfare State). The communitarian intuition that solidarity is a precondition for redistribution remains empirically unsettled.

Counterargument: special obligations as a division of labor. In "What Is So Special about Our Fellow Countrymen?" (1988), Robert E. Goodin argued that states looking after their own citizens first is simply an efficient way of dividing up the universal task of helping everyone. On this view, special obligations are justified not because community constitutes identity but because they are a means of carrying out universal duties well. It follows that where a state does not or cannot protect its people, the obligations of outsiders grow again.
