The question for this session

Aristotle held that what separates oligarchy from democracy is not how many rule but whether the rulers are rich or poor (session 3). Locke and Rousseau, too, placed property and inequality at the center of politics (sessions 6 and 7). Karl Marx (1818–1883) pushed the question to its limit. The state presents itself as an instrument of the common good, he argued, but in reality it maintains the rule of the class that owns the means of production. Law and politics, and even the dominant ideas of an age, grow out of economic relations.

This session works through Marx's own texts on class, the state, and ideology, along with the strongest objections to them. It then applies his framework to the U.S.-China contest over semiconductors as of November 6, 2026. In a contest where Washington and Beijing tell their own companies, and their allies' companies, what they may sell and what they must not buy, is the state a committee managing the common affairs of capital, or an independent power that commands it?

1. A history of class struggles

The Communist Manifesto was written by Marx and Friedrich Engels (1820–1895) as the program of the Communist League and published in German in London in February 1848. Its first chapter opens: "The history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggles." Freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman—"in a word, oppressor and oppressed"—have always stood against each other, and each time their fight ended either in a revolutionary reconstitution of society or in the common ruin of the contending classes.

For Marx, class is defined not by income but by one's relation to the means of production. On one side stands the bourgeoisie, the capitalist class that owns factories, land, and machinery; on the other, the proletariat, the class of wage laborers who must sell their labor power to live. This sets Marx apart from Aristotle's division into rich and poor. A highly paid doctor without a practice of her own sells her labor power; a shopkeeper with a single store owns means of production, however modest her income. The Manifesto predicted that these intermediate groups—"the small tradespeople, shopkeepers, retired tradesmen generally, the handicraftsmen and peasants"—would "sink gradually into the proletariat," crushed by competition from large capital (chapter 1).

The Manifesto finds the distinctive mark of the modern era in the simplification of class antagonism. Society is splitting more and more "into two great hostile camps, into two great classes, directly facing each other: Bourgeoisie and Proletariat" (chapter 1). Few of Marx's predictions have drawn more rebuttals. In the advanced economies of the twentieth century, a new middle class of managers, professionals, and clerical workers grew rather than shrank (see Going deeper).

2. All that is solid melts into air: the bourgeoisie and the world market

Marx was capitalism's sharpest critic and also one of the most generous admirers of its dynamism. "The bourgeoisie, historically, has played a most revolutionary part," the Manifesto declares. It put an end to feudal and patriarchal bonds and "left remaining no other nexus between man and man than naked self-interest, than callous 'cash payment.'" Its driving force is ceaseless innovation:

The bourgeoisie cannot exist without constantly revolutionising the instruments of production, and thereby the relations of production, and with them the whole relations of society. ... All fixed, fast-frozen relations, with their train of ancient and venerable prejudices and opinions, are swept away, all new-formed ones become antiquated before they can ossify. All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned ... (chapter 1)

That innovation does not stop at borders. "The need of a constantly expanding market for its products chases the bourgeoisie over the whole surface of the globe." Through the world market, the bourgeoisie gave production and consumption in every country a cosmopolitan character, replacing local and national self-sufficiency with "universal inter-dependence of nations." Cheap commodities, in the Manifesto's image, are "the heavy artillery with which it batters down all Chinese walls." Chapter 2 goes further: "National differences and antagonisms between peoples are daily more and more vanishing, owing to the development of the bourgeoisie, to freedom of commerce, to the world-market."

The passage is often cited as a portrait of the globalization of the 1990s and 2000s drawn 150 years in advance. Yet the prediction that national antagonisms would fade has failed, from two world wars to the technology contest of 2026. The world market deepened interdependence among states, but that interdependence has also become a means by which states coerce one another (Introduction to International Relations, session 10, on weaponized interdependence).

3. The state: a committee for managing the common affairs of the bourgeoisie

Having established modern industry and the world market, the Manifesto says, the bourgeoisie conquered exclusive political sway in the modern representative state. It then adds:

The executive of the modern State is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie. (chapter 1)

The words to notice are "whole" and "common affairs." The state is not the errand boy of any individual capitalist; it manages the shared interests of the capitalist class as a whole. Because capitalists compete with one another, common conditions such as the protection of property or the opening of foreign markets have to be handled by the state. It follows that the state may act against particular capitalists for the sake of the long-term interest of the class as a whole (this lecture's interpretation). Nicos Poulantzas's notion of "relative autonomy," taken up in section 6, builds on exactly this point.

At the end of chapter 2, the Manifesto defines the state more bluntly still: "Political power, properly so called, is merely the organised power of one class for oppressing another." Once class distinctions disappear, it predicts, "the public power will lose its political character." Yet the program set out in the same chapter calls for concentrating credit, transport, factories, and the instruments of production in the hands of the state. The twentieth-century regimes that claimed Marx's mantle did concentrate the means of production in the state, and far from losing its political character, the state became a party-state that controlled society as a whole. That gap is among the heaviest objections Marx's theory of the state has to answer.

Marx himself did not always portray the state as a mere instrument of class. In The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte (1852), his analysis of Louis Bonaparte's coup of 1851, he describes a French executive with "an army of office-holders, half a million strong, together with a military force of another million men," and writes: "Not before the advent of the second Bonaparte does the government seem to have made itself fully independent." He immediately adds: "And yet the French Government does not float in the air. Bonaparte represents an economic class, and that the most numerous in the commonweal of France—the Allotment Farmer" (chapter 7). Marx's method is to grant the state's apparent independence and then search for its social footing.

4. Base and superstructure: being determines consciousness

In the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859), Marx summed up in a single paragraph the conclusion that had become the guiding thread of his studies:

In the social production which men carry on they enter into definite relations that are indispensable and independent of their will; these relations of production correspond to a definite stage of development of their material powers of production. The sum total of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society—the real foundation, on which rise legal and political superstructures and to which correspond definite forms of social consciousness. The mode of production in material life determines the general character of the social, political and spiritual processes of life. It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but, on the contrary, their social existence determines their consciousness.

The forces of production are the capacity to produce, such as technology and labor; the relations of production are relations of ownership—who owns the means of production and how the product is divided. At a certain stage, the relations of production turn into "fetters" on the forces of production, an era of social revolution begins, and as the base changes, the superstructure is transformed, more or less rapidly (see Passage 3 in Going deeper).

The paragraph is easily taken as economic determinism, the view that the economy mechanically determines everything. In a letter to Joseph Bloch in 1890, after Marx's death, Engels tried to correct that reading. The economic factor is decisive only "in the last instance," he wrote; law, politics, and ideas also shape the course of historical struggles and in many cases largely determine their form. Marx himself opened the Eighteenth Brumaire with the line "Man makes his own history, but he does not make it out of the whole cloth; he does not make it out of conditions chosen by himself, but out of such as he finds close at hand" (chapter 1). The more flexible reading, however, widens the theory's reach only by making it harder to say which cases would count against it (see the counterarguments in Going deeper).

5. Ideology: the ideas of the ruling class are the ruling ideas

Written jointly by Marx and Engels in 1845–46 and not published in full until 1932, The German Ideology states the core proposition of the theory of ideology in its first part: "The ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas." The class that controls the means of material production, the argument runs, also controls the means of mental production, so the ideas of those who lack the latter are, broadly speaking, subject to it. Chapter 2 of the Manifesto says much the same: "The ruling ideas of each age have ever been the ideas of its ruling class."

In the same passage, the two authors offer a striking example. In an age when royal power, aristocracy, and bourgeoisie contend for mastery, so that rule is shared, the doctrine of the separation of powers becomes the dominant idea and is proclaimed an "eternal law." Montesquieu's separation of powers (session 3) appears here not as a universal principle but as the expression of a particular balance of forces. Each new ruling class, they go on, must present its own interest as the common interest of all members of society and give its ideas the form of universality.

Ideology in Marx's sense, then, is not simply a lie. It is a set of ideas that makes a particular interest look like everyone's interest, and its power lies in the fact that those who hold it sincerely believe it to be universal truth. The concept invites an immediate rejoinder: if all ideas express class interests, is Marx's own thought the exception? In Ideology and Utopia (1929), Karl Mannheim widened that question into an inquiry into the social basis of all thought.

6. Classics and theory: from the class state to the autonomous state

Marx's theories of the state and of ideology were extended and contested throughout the twentieth century. The table gives the commonly accepted gist of each work.

Thinker Argument Relation to Marx
Lenin, Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1917) Monopoly and finance capital compete for foreign investment outlets and territory, driving the great powers to war Reverses the Manifesto's expectation that national antagonisms would fade: competition among capitals becomes war among states
Gramsci, Prison Notebooks (written 1929–35) Rule rests not on coercion alone but on consent won in civil society—schools, churches, the press—that is, on hegemony Develops the theory of ideology and stresses the role of the superstructure
Miliband, The State in Capitalist Society (1969), and Poulantzas's critique (1969) Miliband: shared backgrounds and networks between state elites and capitalists make the state an instrument of capital. Poulantzas: whoever holds office, the state's structural function leads it to protect capital's long-term interest, and to do so it enjoys "relative autonomy" from individual capitalists Two readings of the "committee" sentence: instrumentalist and structuralist
Schumpeter, Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy (1942) "Creative destruction," the ceaseless demolition of old structures by innovation, is the essence of capitalism, which may decline because of its successes rather than its failures Inherits the image of a world in which all that is solid melts into air but rejects the conclusion of class revolution
Skocpol, States and Social Revolutions (1979) States are actors with interests of their own, shaped by military and economic competition with other states, and cannot be reduced to instruments of class The theory of state autonomy; a leading challenge to Marxist theories of the state
Piketty, Capital in the Twenty-First Century (2013) When the return on capital exceeds the growth rate (r > g), wealth becomes more concentrated; the mid-twentieth-century fall in inequality was the product of war and policy Shares the focus on accumulation and distribution but predicts no collapse and prescribes a progressive tax on capital; Piketty has said he is not a Marxist

7. Applying it to 2026: does the state manage capital or command it?

The facts. In a Federal Register notice on September 2, 2025, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) revoked the validated end-user (VEU) status of Samsung Electronics' NAND plant in Xi'an and SK hynix's DRAM plant in Wuxi and NAND plant in Dalian, effective December 31, 2025. VEU status had let the plants import U.S. equipment without case-by-case licenses. On December 30, 2025, Washington instead issued equipment licenses valid only for 2026. They cover only tools needed to maintain and run existing lines, exclude tools for expansion or upgrades, and expire on December 31, 2026. According to TrendForce (March 27, 2026), the Chinese plants accounted in 2025 for roughly 30 to 35 percent of Samsung's NAND output, 40 to 45 percent of SK hynix's NAND output, and 35 to 40 percent of its DRAM output.

The U.S. government and U.S. companies do not always pull in the same direction. On December 8, 2025, President Trump allowed Nvidia to sell its H200 chip to China on condition that the United States receive 25 percent of the revenue. In Congress, the MATCH Act, which would bar equipment sales to Chinese firms such as CXMT and YMTC and press allies to align their controls, cleared the House Foreign Affairs Committee 44–0 on April 22, 2026, and was folded into the manager's amendment to the Senate's National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA), but the Senate NDAA is stalled. The White House opposes having its discretion limited by statute, and in 2025 the GAIN AI Act was dropped from the NDAA during final negotiations after industry lobbying.

Beijing gives orders to its companies, too. In mid-January 2026, China's customs administration issued guidance that effectively barred imports of the H200, and the authorities told Chinese firms not to buy the chip unless unavoidable. State Council Decree No. 835, issued in April 2026, created a legal basis for sanctioning companies that comply with U.S. controls. The more faithfully Korean companies' plants in China observe U.S. license conditions, the greater their risk of breaking Chinese law. CXMT, a state-backed memory maker, raised its share of the global DRAM market from 4 percent to 10 percent in a year (9.5 percent by TrendForce's count). The Information reported on August 31, 2026, that CXMT had supplied HBM3E from risk production to Alibaba and Cambricon for testing, though analysts put CXMT's HBM3 yields at only 25 to 30 percent.

At their summit during Xi Jinping's visit to the United States on September 24–25, 2026, the two countries extended their tariff truce to January 10, 2027, but semiconductor export controls were not on the agenda. Two deadlines now converge: December 31, 2026, when the annual U.S. equipment licenses expire, and January 10, 2027, when the tariff truce ends. Clisa Geopolitics Briefings since October have reported no new decision on chip controls, and later developments could not be checked for this session (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the AI and Chips issue, as of late September 2026, and flashpoint status, as of November 6, 2026).

Through Marx's lens. These facts can be read in two directions. The first is the "committee" reading. U.S. export controls can be seen as a "common affair" of American capital as a whole: preserving its lead in AI and semiconductors. Individual equipment makers lose Chinese revenue, but the state makes them bear that loss for the long-term interest of the capitalist class as a whole. The H200 sales decision, the removal of the GAIN AI Act, and the licenses that let Korean plants in China keep running to avoid a memory supply shock can likewise be seen as policy bending to capital's interests (this lecture's interpretation).

The second reading points the other way. Washington stops its own equipment makers from selling, while Beijing stops its own firms from buying Nvidia chips and builds up CXMT with state support. Samsung and SK hynix command neither government; they are caught between two sets of laws. Here the security competition among states—the "superstructure"—appears to be reshaping production and trade, the "base." The Manifesto expected the world market to dissolve national antagonisms; the world market of 2026 is fracturing along national lines. For theorists of state autonomy such as Skocpol, this is evidence that the state is not an instrument of class but an actor in its own right, competing with other states.

Marxists have answers. Lenin argued that competition among capitals spills over into competition among states, and Poulantzas held that the state has the "relative autonomy" to defy individual capitalists in order to protect the system as a whole. But these answers come at a price. If the state's deference to capital proves it is an instrument and its defiance proves its relative autonomy, then no outcome can refute the theory. A fair test requires deciding in advance which outcome would weaken which reading.

The limits of the analogy. First, Marx's classes are capitalists and workers within a society, whereas the protagonists here are states and firms. Marx would have asked first how the costs of this contest are divided among the workers in the plants (this lecture's interpretation). Second, security motives surrounding military AI cannot all be reduced to corporate profit, although one can reply that business interests play a part in defining what counts as "security." Third, in the 1859 preface Marx announced a plan to treat, after capital, landed property, and wage labor, the "state, foreign trade, world market," but he never wrote those parts. His theory of competition among states remained unfinished.

Check your understanding

  1. By what criterion does Marx divide classes, and how does it differ from Aristotle's distinction between rich and poor?
  2. In the sentence "The executive of the modern State is but a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie," why do the words "whole" and "common affairs" matter?
  3. In the 1859 preface, what do the base and the superstructure refer to, and what happens when the forces and relations of production come into conflict?
  4. How did the Communist Manifesto expect the world market to change national antagonisms, and how does the semiconductor contest of 2026 differ from that expectation?

Discussion questions

  1. When a state adopts a policy that hurts particular companies, is that evidence of the state's independence from capital, or of its defense of capital's long-term interest as a whole? What evidence would tell the two apart?
  2. What are the "ruling ideas" in South Korean society, and whose interest do they present as everyone's? Can the same question be put to Marxism itself?
  3. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix are private companies, yet the operation of their plants in China depends on licenses and laws in Washington and Beijing. What may the South Korean government ask of these companies, and what should it not ask?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It covers key passages from the original texts, calculations of the rate of profit and of Piketty's formulas, and research and counterarguments that have tested claims about class and the state.

Reading the original

The passages below are taken from the 1888 English edition of the Communist Manifesto, translated by Samuel Moore and edited by Engels (Project Gutenberg eBook 61), and from N. I. Stone's 1904 translation of the 1859 preface (Project Gutenberg eBook 46423), with omissions marked by ellipses.

Passage 1. Class struggle (Communist Manifesto, opening of chapter 1)

The history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggles. Freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman, in a word, oppressor and oppressed, stood in constant opposition to one another, carried on an uninterrupted, now hidden, now open fight, a fight that each time ended, either in a revolutionary re-constitution of society at large, or in the common ruin of the contending classes.

How to read it. It is easy to overlook the alternative written into the passage: "common ruin." The Manifesto did not claim that class struggle always ends in progress. Its prediction, in the same chapter, that class antagonisms would be simplified is an empirical claim that can be tested, and it does not fit the growth of the middle class in twentieth-century advanced economies (see Wright below).

Passage 2. The world market (Communist Manifesto, chapter 1)

The need of a constantly expanding market for its products chases the bourgeoisie over the whole surface of the globe. ... The bourgeoisie has through its exploitation of the world-market given a cosmopolitan character to production and consumption in every country. ... In place of the old local and national seclusion and self-sufficiency, we have intercourse in every direction, universal inter-dependence of nations. ... The cheap prices of its commodities are the heavy artillery with which it batters down all Chinese walls ... In one word, it creates a world after its own image.

How to read it. In this picture, the force that moves the world is capital, not the state. Yet six years before the Manifesto appeared, Britain had forced China open with warships and troops in the First Opium War (1839–42). What opened markets was not only cheap goods but state force (this lecture's interpretation). In the semiconductor contest of 2026, states are closing markets rather than opening them.

Passage 3. Base and superstructure (preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, 1859)

At a certain stage of their development, the material forces of production in society come in conflict with the existing relations of production, or—what is but a legal expression for the same thing—with the property relations within which they had been at work before. From forms of development of the forces of production these relations turn into their fetters. Then comes the period of social revolution. With the change of the economic foundation the entire immense superstructure is more or less rapidly transformed. ... No social order ever disappears before all the productive forces, for which there is room in it, have been developed; and new higher relations of production never appear before the material conditions of their existence have matured in the womb of the old society.

How to read it. Taken at its word, the last sentence implies that socialist revolution should come first in the countries where the productive forces are most developed. The actual revolutions came in largely agrarian countries, Russia in 1917 and China in 1949. Critics treat this as a failed prediction.

Working it out

Problem 1. Surplus value and the rate of profit. Marx held that workers create more value than the value of their wages and that the difference, surplus value, is the source of profit. In a factory, one worker works 10 hours a day, and each hour of labor creates 10,000 won of value. The daily wage (variable capital, v) is 40,000 won, and the machinery and materials used up each day (constant capital, c) come to 60,000 won. (1) Find the daily surplus value s, the rate of surplus value s/v, and the rate of profit s/(c + v). (2) If mechanization raises constant capital to 140,000 won a day while hours and wages stay the same, what happens to the rate of profit? (3) What claim of Marx's does this calculation illustrate, and what objections has it met?

Problem 2. Piketty's formulas. Piketty argues that in the long run the ratio of capital (wealth) to national income, β, approaches the saving rate s divided by the growth rate g (β = s/g), and that capital's share of national income, α, equals the rate of return on capital r multiplied by β (α = r × β). (1) With a saving rate of 12 percent and a growth rate of 3 percent, what is β? (2) If growth slows to 1.5 percent, what does β become? (3) If the rate of return stays at 5 percent, what is α in each case?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. The worker creates 10 hours × 10,000 won = 100,000 won of value a day. With wages of 40,000 won, surplus value is 60,000 won. The rate of surplus value is 60 ÷ 40 = 150 percent, and the rate of profit is 60 ÷ (60 + 40) = 60 percent.
  2. Surplus value is still 60,000 won, so the rate of profit falls to 60 ÷ (140 + 40) ≈ 33.3 percent.
  3. In volume 3 of Capital, Marx argued that as mechanization increases, the rate of profit tends to fall (the "tendency of the rate of profit to fall"). The objections are strong. Mechanization usually raises productivity and with it the rate of surplus value, so the rate of profit need not fall. In 1961, Nobuo Okishio set out a theorem showing that, with the real wage held constant, cost-cutting technical changes chosen by capitalists cannot lower the general rate of profit. Most of modern economics also rejects the labor theory of value itself, the claim that labor alone creates value.

Problem 2.

  1. β = 12% ÷ 3% = 4; capital equals 400 percent of national income.
  2. β = 12% ÷ 1.5% = 8, or 800 percent, double the earlier figure.
  3. At 3 percent growth, α = 5% × 4 = 20 percent; at 1.5 percent growth, α = 5% × 8 = 40 percent. The core of Piketty's argument is that slower growth enlarges capital's share of income. That conclusion, however, assumes that returns do not fall much as capital accumulates, an assumption many economists dispute.

Evidence and counterarguments

Class structure: "contradictory class locations" instead of polarization. In Classes (1985), the American sociologist Erik Olin Wright conceptualized managers and professionals—people who own no means of production but control others' labor or hold scarce skills—as occupying "contradictory class locations." The revision came from within Marxism, but it also conceded that the prediction of a society split into two camps had not come true. In "Brahmin Left vs Merchant Right" (2018), Thomas Piketty analyzed electoral data from France, Britain, and the United States and found that the mid-twentieth-century pattern, in which lower-income voters leaned left, had given way to one in which the more educated vote left and those with higher incomes and wealth vote right.

Whom does the state represent? Gilens and Page (2014). Analyzing 1,779 U.S. policy issues from 1981 to 2002, Martin Gilens and Benjamin I. Page reported that the preferences of economic elites and business-oriented interest groups had substantial independent effects on policy outcomes, while those of average citizens had little or none (Perspectives on Politics 12, no. 3). The study is often cited in support of the instrumentalist reading. Critics replied that on most issues the preferences of middle-income and affluent Americans differ little, which makes their separate influence hard to isolate, and the interpretation remains in dispute.

Falsifiability: Popper's critique. In The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945) and Conjectures and Refutations (1963), Karl Popper argued that Marx's theory initially made testable predictions—revolution in the advanced countries, the polarization of classes—but that when these failed, his followers reinterpreted both theory and evidence to escape refutation. The critique applies directly to the question raised in section 7. At the same time, Marx's habit of asking what economic interests lie behind politics and ideas has become a basic tool of modern political economy.
