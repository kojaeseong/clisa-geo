The question for this session

In 1831 Alexis de Tocqueville, a 25-year-old French magistrate, crossed to the United States on an official mission to study its prisons. He stayed a little over nine months, and on his return he wrote Democracy in America (volume 1, 1835; volume 2, 1840). The great fact he found in America was a society without hereditary rank or privilege, which he called the "equality of conditions." He was convinced that the same equality was bearing down on Europe and could not be stopped. The real question, then, was not whether to accept equality but how to keep a society of equals free.

His answer carries two warnings. The first is the tyranny of the majority, in which the majority crushes minorities and any opinion that departs from its own. The second is a "soft despotism," in which a mild but all-providing power settles over a population of isolated individuals. This session examines both warnings and the remedies Tocqueville proposed: local self-government, lawyers, juries, and associations. It then takes up his claim that democracies cannot persevere in a design or wait patiently for results, and tests it against two European decisions as of November 6, 2026: Germany's budget for military aid to Ukraine and Poland's defense spending.

1. Equality as a "providential fact"

Volume 1 opens with a sentence that frames the whole book: "Amongst the novel objects that attracted my attention during my stay in the United States, nothing struck me more forcibly than the general equality of conditions" (volume 1, introduction). Equality here does not mean equal incomes. It means a social state in which everyone, in principle, holds the same rights and can climb by the same paths. Tocqueville believed that this social state shaped not only laws and government but also opinion, feeling, and the habits of daily life.

He traced the same movement through seven centuries of French history. The opening of the clergy to all classes, the rise of lawyers and merchants, and the rivalry between kings and nobles had all hastened equality. Those who worked for it and those who fought it were swept in the same direction. He concluded that "the gradual development of the equality of conditions is therefore a providential fact": "it is universal, it is durable, it constantly eludes all human interference, and all events as well as all men contribute to its progress" (volume 1, introduction; see Passage 1).

This diagnosis is the starting point of the entire book. Tocqueville set out neither to celebrate democracy nor to stop it. Equality was coming regardless; the only choice left was whether a society of equals would be free or servile.

2. The tyranny of the majority

In volume 1, part 2, chapter 7, "Unlimited Power of the Majority in the United States, and Its Consequences," Tocqueville defines the core of democracy: "The very essence of democratic government consists in the absolute sovereignty of the majority; for there is nothing in democratic States which is capable of resisting it." In that very fact he saw the danger.

The argument is simple. If one person with absolute power can wrong his adversaries, there is no reason a majority cannot do the same: "Men are not apt to change their characters by agglomeration." Tocqueville therefore refused to grant to any number of people the unlimited authority he would deny to any one of them. The rights of every people are bounded by justice, and a citizen who refuses to obey an unjust law does not deny the majority's right to command; he simply appeals "from the sovereignty of the people to the sovereignty of mankind."

Here Tocqueville parts company with Aristotle. Aristotle regarded the polity, a regime that mixes classes, as the best one most states could achieve (session 3). Tocqueville called mixed government "a mere chimera." In every society, he argued, one principle eventually prevails, and a state truly balanced between two opposing principles ends in revolution or dissolution. What he wanted was not a mixture of powers but obstacles that would slow the dominant power and force it to moderate its own vehemence. The trouble with American democracy, he wrote, lay not in its weakness, as Europeans often claimed, but in its "overpowering strength." Public opinion, the legislature, and the executive, the very places a wronged citizen might turn for redress, all belonged to the majority.

Dominion over thought. Tocqueville's sharpest warning concerned not laws but the majority's hold over what people think. The most absolute monarchs in Europe could not stop hostile ideas from circulating in secret, even at court. In America, once the majority had decided, even those who had opposed the measure fell silent. "I know no country in which there is so little true independence of mind and freedom of discussion as in America," he wrote. A writer who crossed the line the majority had drawn faced no auto-da-fé, but he suffered daily obloquy, and those who shared his views abandoned him in silence (see Passage 2).

In a footnote Tocqueville recorded that in Baltimore in 1812 a mob wrecked the presses of a newspaper that had opposed the popular war. The tyranny of the majority, the episode shows, surfaces in earnest when passions run high, as they do in wartime.

3. What tempers the tyranny of the majority

In volume 1, part 2, chapter 8, "Causes Mitigating Tyranny in the United States," Tocqueville asks how America withstands this danger.

The first answer is administrative decentralization. America had "centralized government," which decided matters common to the whole nation, but almost no "centralized administration" to carry those decisions into every village. The majority's commands had to pass through township and county officials, and local circumstances softened them along the way. Tocqueville saw self-government as a school of liberty: "Town-meetings are to liberty what primary schools are to science; they bring it within the people's reach, they teach men how to use and how to enjoy it" (volume 1, part 1, chapter 5).

The second is the legal profession, with its respect for forms and precedent. "If I were asked where I place the American aristocracy, I should reply without hesitation that it is not composed of the rich, who are united together by no common tie, but that it occupies the judicial bench and the bar."

The third is the jury. In the jury box citizens learn the law and acquire the habit of judging other people's affairs fairly, which is why Tocqueville called the jury "a gratuitous public school ever open."

The common thread is this. A society of equals has no individuals who, like aristocrats, can stand up to power alone. The strength to defend liberty must therefore come from the habits citizens form by handling common affairs together, and from the institutions that cultivate those habits. Aristotle in session 3 looked for stability in a balance among classes; Tocqueville looked for it in the mores of citizens (mœurs in French, rendered "manners" by Reeve). He held that laws contribute more than physical circumstances to sustaining the American republic, and manners more than laws (volume 1, part 2, chapter 9).

4. Individualism and soft despotism

Volume 2 asks how equality reshapes people's feelings and ideas. Its most famous term, which Tocqueville himself called "a novel expression," is "individualism" (volume 2, part 2, chapter 2). Tocqueville distinguishes it from egotism, which is "a passionate and exaggerated love of self" and a vice as old as the world. Individualism is "a mature and calm feeling" that leads each person to withdraw into a small circle of family and friends and leave the wider society to itself. Aristocracy had linked everyone from peasant to king in a single chain; "democracy breaks that chain, and severs every link of it."

Individualism is dangerous because it invites a new kind of despotism. In volume 2, part 4, chapter 6, "What Sort of Despotism Democratic Nations Have to Fear," Tocqueville argues that this despotism will look nothing like the tyrants of the past. Above a mass of equal and mutually indifferent people "stands an immense and tutelary power" that secures their pleasures and seeks to keep them "in perpetual childhood" (see Passage 3). He believed that this servitude could coexist with the outward forms of popular sovereignty: "the people shake off their state of dependence just long enough to select their master, and then relapse into it again."

His remedies are associations and "self-interest rightly understood." Where the government would take the lead in France, or a great lord in England, Americans form an association (volume 2, part 2, chapter 5). Individuals who are weak on their own and never learn to act together will end by handing everything to the state. Hence Tocqueville's law of democratic societies: "If men are to remain civilized, or to become so, the art of associating together must grow and improve in the same ratio in which the equality of conditions is increased."

Self-interest rightly understood (volume 2, part 2, chapter 8) is the moral principle with which Americans fight individualism. They do not praise virtue as beautiful; they argue that helping others serves one's own interest in the end. Tocqueville judged the principle "not a lofty one, but it is clear and sure." It produces no great acts of self-sacrifice, yet it disciplines citizens "in habits of regularity, temperance, moderation, foresight, self-command."

5. Can a democracy persevere in a design?

Tocqueville identified foreign policy as democracy's weakest point. In the section "Conduct of Foreign Affairs by the American Democracy," which closes volume 1, part 2, chapter 5, "Government of the Democracy in America," he writes:

Foreign politics demand scarcely any of those qualities which a democracy possesses; and they require, on the contrary, the perfect use of almost all those faculties in which it is deficient. ... But a democracy is unable to regulate the details of an important undertaking, to persevere in a design, and to work out its execution in the presence of serious obstacles. It cannot combine its measures with secrecy, and it will not await their consequences with patience. These are qualities which more especially belong to an individual or to an aristocracy ...

His evidence came from the wars of the French Revolution. America's interest plainly lay in staying out of Europe's war, yet popular sympathy for France ran so high that only George Washington's inflexible character and immense popularity kept the country from declaring war on Britain. Democracies, Tocqueville concluded, have a "propensity ... to obey the impulse of passion rather than the suggestions of prudence, and to abandon a mature design for the gratification of a momentary caprice." An aristocratic body, by contrast, is too numerous to be led astray by intrigue, too few to be swept away by passion, and lasts across generations. The Athenian assembly's vote for the Sicilian expedition, discussed in session 7 of Introduction to International Relations, is the classic illustration of his worry (this lecture's interpretation).

In the same section Tocqueville notes that the U.S. Constitution entrusts the permanent direction of foreign affairs to the president and the Senate, whose members then served six-year terms and were chosen by state legislatures, which "tends in some degree to detach the general foreign policy of the Union from the control of the people." He also added a caveat: because the United States of his day had almost no foreign interests, judgment on American democracy's capacity for foreign policy had to be suspended.

In volume 2 Tocqueville qualified his own verdict. In part 3, chapter 24, "Causes Which Render Democratic Armies Weaker Than Other Armies at the Outset of a Campaign, and More Formidable in Protracted Warfare," he argues that once a long war has pulled a whole society away from its peaceful pursuits, the same passions that made peace so precious turn to arms. He concludes: "An aristocratic nation, which in a contest with a democratic people does not succeed in ruining the latter at the outset of the war, always runs a great risk of being conquered by it."

Do the two verdicts contradict each other? This lecture reads them as addressing different situations. Volume 1 concerns diplomacy and alliances, in which a nation pays costs over many years for distant interests and must wait for the results. Volume 2 concerns a war that engulfs the citizens' own lives. When war shakes people's lives directly, democracies dig in; when the payoff is remote and the interest indirect, they waver (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Classics and theory: from the tyranny of the majority to public opinion and foreign policy

The majority's hold over thought became a central problem for liberal political philosophy, and democracy's weakness in foreign affairs became a central question for the study of public opinion.

7. Applying it to 2026: can democracies sustain support for a long war?

The facts. As of November 6, 2026, Russia's full-scale war against Ukraine is in its fifth year. With U.S. aid reduced, Europe has become the center of support. In December 2025 the EU agreed to lend Ukraine €90 billion over 2026 and 2027, and it finalized the loan in April 2026 (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Russia-Ukraine War).

On July 6, 2026, the German cabinet approved a 2027 budget draft with €11.6 billion for military support to Ukraine; the Bundestag's final vote is scheduled for November 27. During deliberations on the 2026 budget, the Bundestag added about €3 billion to Ukraine support. For 2027, however, Clisa Geopolitics judges that sharply higher defense spending and heavy new borrowing leave little room for an increase (the record behind forecast f76).

The 2027 draft budget that the Polish government sent to the Sejm, the lower house, on September 29 sets defense spending at 198.1 billion zlotys, or 4.51 percent of GDP: 131.7 billion from the state budget (3.0 percent of GDP) plus 66.4 billion from the Armed Forces Support Fund. On October 8, at the first reading, the Sejm rejected a motion to throw out the draft, 198–237; a final vote is expected in December. By NATO's figures, Poland's defense spending rose from 2.21 percent of GDP in 2022 to an estimated 4.25 percent in 2025 and 4.68 percent in 2026, the highest in the alliance. The president cannot veto a budget act; he can only refer it to the Constitutional Tribunal (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Russia-NATO standoff).

Public opinion. Poland's CBOS found in September 2025 that 63 percent believed "there is currently a threat to Poland's independence," the highest figure since CBOS began asking the question in February 1991. Yet in November–December 2025, 54 percent said that ending the war and restoring peace should take priority "even if Ukraine has to give up part of its territory or its independence," against 33 percent who favored continuing to fight without concessions. Because that option includes giving up independence, the figures cannot be compared directly with questions that ask only about territory.

Through Tocqueville's lens. Support for Ukraine is precisely the kind of policy in which the weakness described in volume 1 should show. The costs come out of each year's budget; the payoff, European security, is distant and uncertain; and no one knows when the war will end. Yet in the 2026 budget it was the Bundestag, the body that most directly represents voters, that raised support above the government's draft. Forecast f76 asks whether that happens again for 2027. Tocqueville's worry, though, was not timidity but inconstancy, the tendency to abandon a mature design for a momentary passion, so a single year's increase cannot settle the question of perseverance (this lecture's interpretation).

Poland illustrates the distinction drawn in section 5. In a country where 63 percent of the public sees a threat to its independence, defense spending above 4 percent of GDP looks set to continue for a third straight year, which points the same way as volume 2's claim that democracies dig in when danger touches citizens' own lives. Yet 54 percent of the same public would put ending Ukraine's war first, even at the price of concessions. One reading is that Poles judge their own defense and aid for another country's war differently (this lecture's interpretation). The two figures come from different questions asked at different times, however, and should not be treated as a single attitude.

The device Tocqueville saw in the U.S. Constitution, setting foreign policy a step back from day-to-day opinion, has modern European counterparts. Poland finances roughly a third of its defense spending through the Armed Forces Support Fund, which sits outside the state budget; the EU's loan to Ukraine fixed two years of funding at once; and in March 2025 Germany amended its Basic Law to exempt defense spending above 1 percent of GDP, among other items, from its constitutional debt brake. Such arrangements lend democracies some of the permanence Tocqueville admired in aristocracies, but they also narrow the room for correcting policy through the annual budget. Perseverance is bought with some loss of accountability (this lecture's interpretation).

The counterevidence. Since 1815, democracies have won more than three-quarters of the wars they have fought (see Reiter and Stam in Going deeper). Audience cost theory, covered in session 6 of Introduction to International Relations, holds that leaders who break public commitments pay a domestic price, which makes democratic commitments more credible, not less. Nor is war weariness a democratic monopoly: in a September 2026 Levada Center survey, 64 percent of Russians said they wanted peace negotiations (a survey conducted under wartime censorship and best treated as an indicator of direction rather than level; Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Russia-Ukraine War). The difference lies in whether public weariness has a channel through which it can change policy (this lecture's interpretation).

The limits of the analogy. First, Tocqueville was writing about the United States of the 1830s, a country with almost no foreign interests, whereas Germany and Poland decide through coalition governments and multilateral bodies inside the EU and NATO. Second, neither country is a belligerent, so volume 2's logic, in which a whole society mobilized for war grows more tenacious, does not apply directly to states that are supplying aid. Third, Tocqueville's foil was an aristocracy; the relevant comparison in this war is an authoritarian Russia.

Check your understanding

  1. What does Tocqueville mean by the "equality of conditions," and why does he call it a "providential fact"?
  2. Why does Tocqueville call mixed government a "chimera," and what does he ask for instead? Compare his view with Aristotle's polity in session 3.
  3. What does it mean to say that the tyranny of the majority acts on thought rather than through law or force?
  4. How does individualism differ from egotism, and how does individualism lead to "soft despotism"?
  5. How can volume 1's verdict on foreign policy be reconciled with the argument about protracted war in volume 2, part 3, chapter 24?

Discussion questions

  1. When someone voices an opinion on social media and is met with a wave of collective condemnation, is that what Tocqueville meant by the majority's tyranny over thought? If no legal penalty follows, can it be said that liberty is not at stake?
  2. Can South Korean policies that must look decades ahead, such as pensions, defense, and the response to a falling birth rate, survive beyond the electoral cycle? What do institutions that insulate decisions from public opinion cost in exchange for the perseverance they provide?
  3. Do South Korea's civic groups, clubs, neighborhood associations, and religious organizations serve as the "schools of liberty" Tocqueville described?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages of Democracy in America in a classic English translation, works through democratic perseverance with numbers, and surveys research on war and public opinion.

Reading the original

The quotations from Democracy in America in this session, both below and in the main text, are taken from Henry Reeve's English translation (volume 1, 1835; volume 2, 1840; Project Gutenberg eBooks 815 and 816), with omissions marked by ellipses.

Passage 1. A providential fact (volume 1, introduction)

The gradual development of the equality of conditions is therefore a providential fact, and it possesses all the characteristics of a divine decree: it is universal, it is durable, it constantly eludes all human interference, and all events as well as all men contribute to its progress. Would it, then, be wise to imagine that a social impulse which dates from so far back can be checked by the efforts of a generation? Is it credible that the democracy which has annihilated the feudal system and vanquished kings will respect the citizen and the capitalist? Will it stop now that it has grown so strong and its adversaries so weak?

How to read it. Tocqueville treats equality not as a political choice but as the direction of history. That premise changes his question from "equality or inequality?" to "freedom or servitude under equality?"

Passage 2. The body left free, the soul enslaved (volume 1, part 2, chapter 7; Reeve chapter 15)

Fetters and headsmen were the coarse instruments which tyranny formerly employed ... Under the absolute sway of an individual despot the body was attacked in order to subdue the soul, and the soul escaped the blows which were directed against it and rose superior to the attempt; but such is not the course adopted by tyranny in democratic republics; there the body is left free, and the soul is enslaved. The sovereign can no longer say, "You shall think as I do on pain of death;" but he says, "You are free to think differently from me, and to retain your life, your property, and all that you possess; but if such be your determination, you are henceforth an alien among your people. ... You will remain among men, but you will be deprived of the rights of mankind. Your fellow-creatures will shun you like an impure being, and those who are most persuaded of your innocence will abandon you too, lest they should be shunned in their turn."

How to read it. The instrument of this tyranny is not punishment but isolation. What produces the silence is not only the majority's pressure but the fear of "those who are most persuaded of your innocence."

Passage 3. Soft despotism (volume 2, part 4, chapter 6)

Above this race of men stands an immense and tutelary power, which takes upon itself alone to secure their gratifications, and to watch over their fate. That power is absolute, minute, regular, provident, and mild. It would be like the authority of a parent, if, like that authority, its object was to prepare men for manhood; but it seeks on the contrary to keep them in perpetual childhood ... The will of man is not shattered, but softened, bent, and guided: men are seldom forced by it to act, but they are constantly restrained from acting: such a power does not destroy, but it prevents existence; it does not tyrannize, but it compresses, enervates, extinguishes, and stupefies a people, till each nation is reduced to be nothing better than a flock of timid and industrious animals, of which the government is the shepherd.

How to read it. Tocqueville said the old words "despotism" and "tyranny" did not fit this power, and the power might even be elected. People want to be led and to remain free at the same time, he argued, and they try to satisfy both wishes with a tutelary government of their own choosing. The passage is often cited against the welfare state as such, but what troubled Tocqueville was not that the state takes on tasks; it was that citizens lose the habit of judging for themselves and acting together (this lecture's interpretation).

Working it out

Problem 1. Perseverance and patience (discounting). A country supports another country's war. The support costs 1 this year, 1 a year from now, and 1 two years from now, and it yields a benefit of 4 (improved security at home) in three years. The country's politics discounts the future by a factor δ (delta) per year and compares present values. (1) With δ = 0.9, is it worth starting the support this year? (2) What about δ = 0.7? (3) A country with δ = 0.7 has already started the support through some binding arrangement and paid the first year's cost. Looking ahead from one year in, is it worth continuing? What happens if, at that point, the war drags on, pushing the benefit back a year and adding a year of costs? How does this relate to Tocqueville's claim that a democracy "will not await their consequences with patience"?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. The present value of the costs is 1 + 0.9 + 0.9² = 1 + 0.9 + 0.81 = 2.71. The present value of the benefit is 4 × 0.9³ = 4 × 0.729 = 2.916. The benefit exceeds the costs by 0.206, so starting is worthwhile.
  2. The costs come to 1 + 0.7 + 0.49 = 2.19 and the benefit to 4 × 0.7³ = 4 × 0.343 = 1.372. The costs exceed the benefit by 0.818, so the country does not start.
  3. One year in, the remaining costs are 1 + 0.7 = 1.7 and the remaining benefit is 4 × 0.7² = 1.96, so continuing is worthwhile (the first year's cost is already spent and does not enter the decision). If the benefit is pushed back a year, however, the remaining costs become 1 + 0.7 + 0.49 = 2.19 and the remaining benefit 4 × 0.7³ = 1.372, the same position as in (2), and the country stops. A country with δ = 0.9 would carry on in the same situation, at 2.71 against 2.916. Every time the end recedes, the decision effectively starts over, so the less patient a polity, the harder it is to sustain support through a long war. Assigning a high δ to aristocracies and a low one to democracies is a way of modeling Tocqueville's claim, not a measured fact.

Evidence and counterarguments

Reiter and Stam (2002): democracies pick the wars they can win. In Democracies at War, Dan Reiter and Allan C. Stam report that since 1815 democracies have won about 80 percent of the wars they fought. They offer two reasons: leaders who fear the political cost of defeat choose wars they can win, and democratic soldiers fight with more initiative. The same book argues, however, that because public support erodes as casualties mount, democracies tend either to fight short, victorious wars or to accept a compromise short of outright victory. The evidence undercuts Tocqueville's claim that democracies are incompetent in foreign affairs but, if anything, supports his claim that they will not wait patiently for results (this lecture's interpretation). Michael C. Desch countered that factors other than regime type, such as relative power, explain most of the democratic advantage (2002).

Mueller (1973) and Gelpi, Feaver, and Reifler (2005): casualties or the prospect of success? In War, Presidents and Public Opinion, John E. Mueller showed that American support for the Korean and Vietnam wars fell as cumulative casualties rose. Using data from the Iraq War, Christopher Gelpi, Peter D. Feaver, and Jason Reifler found that the public's tolerance for casualties depends heavily on its belief that the war can be won (2005). Democratic patience, in other words, is not a fixed trait but varies with the prospect of success, and European opinion on aid to Ukraine should be read alongside the situation at the front (this lecture's interpretation).
