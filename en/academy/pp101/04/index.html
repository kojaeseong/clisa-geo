The question for this session

For Aristotle, the study of politics was a continuation of ethics. A good state was one that enabled its citizens to live well, and a good ruler was a virtuous one (session 3). In 1513 an out-of-work Florentine official turned that tradition on its head: a prince who wants to keep his state, he argued, must also know how to do wrong. Niccolò Machiavelli's The Prince has been the most denounced book in the history of political thought for five centuries, and for that very reason one of the best known.

This session asks whether politics must be judged by a standard different from that of morality, and if so, what that standard is and what is lost in adopting it. It follows Machiavelli's argument through chapters 15, 17, 18, and 25 of The Prince and surveys the debate over how to interpret him. It then takes up a question that is live as of November 6, 2026: can promises exchanged by leaders who do not trust each other be relied on? The test case is U.S.-North Korean summit diplomacy and North Korea's moratorium on intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) tests.

1. A little book by an unemployed secretary

Machiavelli was born in Florence in 1469. From 1498 to 1512 he served as secretary of the Second Chancery of the Florentine republic, traveling on missions to the French court, the papal curia, and the camp of Cesare Borgia, among others. When the Medici returned in 1512 and the republic fell, he was dismissed that November. Soon afterward he was accused of taking part in a plot against the Medici, imprisoned, and tortured. Once released, he withdrew to a small farm outside the city.

The Prince was written in these years. In a letter to his friend Francesco Vettori in December 1513, Machiavelli described how each evening he took off his dusty peasant clothes, put on his court dress, and entered into "conversation" with the men of antiquity, and how he had drawn on those conversations to compose a small work on principalities. The book was eventually dedicated to Lorenzo di Piero de' Medici. The quarrel over its meaning begins here: a servant of the republic offered it to the family that had destroyed the republic. It was published in 1532, five years after his death in 1527, and placed on the Church's Index of Prohibited Books in 1559.

During the same period Machiavelli was also writing his Discourses on the First Decade of Titus Livius, a study of the Roman republic. The Machiavelli of the Discourses is a republican who could write that "a people is more prudent, more stable, and of better judgment than a prince" (book 1, chapter 58). That the same man wrote both a manual of rule for princes and a celebration of republican liberty is known as the problem of the two Machiavellis.

2. Politics as it is: chapter 15

In chapter 15 Machiavelli announces that he will depart from the writers who came before him:

...many have pictured republics and principalities which in fact have never been known or seen, because how one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live, that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation... (chapter 15)

The imaginary republics are usually taken to mean Plato's Republic (session 2) along with the medieval and Renaissance "mirrors for princes," which urged rulers to cultivate justice, generosity, and good faith. Machiavelli's conclusion is that a man who acts in every respect according to his professions of virtue will be undone among so many who are not good. A prince who wishes to keep his state must therefore know how to do wrong and use that knowledge or not as necessity dictates. He should avoid the vices that would cost him his state, but he need not fear blame for vices without which the state could hardly be saved, because "something which looks like virtue, if followed, would be his ruin; whilst something else, which looks like vice, yet followed brings him security and prosperity."

What Machiavelli does not do is erase the line between good and evil. He still calls cruelty a vice and good faith praiseworthy. What changes is the standard of judgment: an action is assessed by whether it preserves or ruins the state. That is the precise sense in which politics comes to be judged apart from morality.

3. Better feared than loved: chapters 7 and 17

Cruelty well used. The figure Machiavelli treats at greatest length is Cesare Borgia, son of Pope Alexander VI. Having taken the Romagna, a region rife with robbery and feuding, Borgia gave full powers to Remirro de Orco, "a swift and cruel man," who soon restored order. Then, knowing the harshness of that rule would be laid at his own door, Borgia had Remirro cut in two and left one morning on the piazza at Cesena "with the block and a bloody knife at his side." "The barbarity of this spectacle," Machiavelli writes, "caused the people to be at once satisfied and dismayed" (chapter 7).

Fear without hatred. Chapter 17 asks whether it is better to be loved or feared. The best is to be both, but "it is much safer to be feared than loved, when, of the two, either must be dispensed with." Men will offer their blood and their lives while danger is far off and turn against the prince when it comes near. Love is held by a bond of obligation that men break whenever their interest is at stake; fear is held by a dread of punishment that never fails.

Even so, the prince must not be hated, and the way to avoid hatred is simple: keep his hands off his subjects' property and their women, because "men more quickly forget the death of their father than the loss of their patrimony." The chapter ends on a principle that runs through the whole book: since men love at their own pleasure and fear at the prince's, "a wise prince should establish himself on that which is in his own control and not in that of others." The argument about promises in chapter 18 follows directly from it.

4. The fox and the lion: chapter 18

Chapter 18 bears the title "Concerning the Way in Which Princes Should Keep Faith." There are two ways of contesting, Machiavelli says, one by law and the other by force. The first is proper to men and the second to beasts, but because the first is often not enough, a prince must know how to use both. Among the beasts he should choose the fox and the lion, since the lion cannot defend himself against snares and the fox cannot defend himself against wolves. Then comes the chapter's most notorious passage:

Therefore a wise lord cannot, nor ought he to, keep faith when such observance may be turned against him, and when the reasons that caused him to pledge it exist no longer. If men were entirely good this precept would not hold, but because they are bad, and will not keep faith with you, you too are not bound to observe it with them. Nor will there ever be wanting to a prince legitimate reasons to excuse this non-observance. (chapter 18)

The passage is an inversion of Cicero. As a note in Marriott's translation points out, the two ways of contesting come from Cicero's De Officiis (book 1, section 34). In the same work Cicero wrote that wrong is done either by force or by fraud, fraud being the mark of the fox and force of the lion, and that both are wholly unworthy of a human being, though fraud deserves the greater contempt (book 1, section 41). Machiavelli takes the same animals and reaches the opposite conclusion.

The second half of the chapter turns to appearance and reality. A prince need not actually possess the good qualities, but he must appear to possess them, because men judge by the eye rather than the hand (see Passage 2 in Going deeper).

Machiavelli returns to the theme in book 3, chapter 42, of the Discourses, holding that between princes all promises are disregarded once the causes that produced them cease to operate (see Passage 3 in Going deeper). What sustains a promise, on this view, is not the character of the person who made it but the circumstances surrounding it. That idea is the key tool for section 7.

5. Fortune and virtù: chapter 25

Virtù and fortuna. Fortuna is fortune, the shifting circumstances that human beings cannot control. Virtù is often translated as "virtue," but for Machiavelli it means not moral goodness but the ability and resolve to read circumstances, seize opportunity, and found and hold a state. In chapter 25 he writes that fortune governs half of human actions and leaves the other half, "or perhaps a little less," to human direction. He compares it to a river:

I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. (chapter 25)

Fortune shows her power where nothing has been prepared to resist her. In the same chapter Machiavelli argues that success depends on whether one's way of acting suits the times. The chapter closes by likening fortune to a woman who must be handled boldly, a sexist figure of its age that is hard to accept today.

Did Machiavelli sever politics from morality? Interpretations fall into three broad camps. Leo Strauss declared himself inclined to the old view of Machiavelli as "a teacher of evil." Isaiah Berlin argued that Machiavelli did not separate politics from morality but set two moralities against each other: the Christian ethic of humility and forgiveness and the civic ethic of ancient Rome, which put the glory of the state first. Quentin Skinner showed how Machiavelli adopted the form of the mirror-for-princes genre while overturning, one by one, the virtues it recommended (see the table in section 6).

All three readings must reckon with Machiavelli's own advice that a prince should "not... diverge from the good if he can avoid doing so, but, if compelled, then to know how to set about it" (chapter 18). He never claimed that morality was meaningless. He claimed that political action must be judged by its results and that a reputation for morality is a political resource the prince has to manage (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Classics and theory: the problem of dirty hands

Political thought since Machiavelli has long argued over the question he raised: can political necessity override moral obligation?

Thinker Argument Relation to Machiavelli
Rousseau, The Social Contract, book 3, chapter 6 (1762) Machiavelli professed to teach kings but really taught the people; The Prince is the book of republicans The republican reading, which sees the book as exposing what princely power really is (session 7)
Kant, Perpetual Peace (1795) No treaty of peace is valid if it secretly reserves material for a future war (first preliminary article); one must not be a "political moralist" who bends morality to suit statecraft A direct rejection of chapter 18's view of promises (session 10)
Weber, "Politics as a Vocation" (lecture, 1919) A politician must act not by an ethic of conviction alone but by an ethic of responsibility, answering for the foreseeable consequences of action Keeps the standard of judging by results but places the burden on the politician's own conscience

Berlin, "The Originality of Machiavelli" (1972) Christian morality and ancient civic morality are both genuine values, yet they cannot be combined Sees not a separation but a choice between two moralities
Walzer, "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands" (1973) A politician who must do wrong for the community's sake should do it, but must acknowledge that it is wrong and bear the burden Accepts Machiavelli's conclusion while insisting that the wrong remains a wrong

7. Applying it to 2026: can promises between leaders who distrust each other be relied on?

The facts. In April 2018 North Korea declared a halt to nuclear tests and ICBM test launches, and on June 12 President Donald Trump and Kim Jong Un met for the first time in Singapore. Their joint statement set out four principles but no sequence for implementing them, and the test moratorium remained a unilateral declaration outside the text. Right after the summit Trump announced, without prior consultation with Seoul, that joint U.S.-South Korean exercises would be suspended (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Korean Peninsula issue). The Hanoi summit of February 2019 ended without an agreement. At a party plenary meeting at the end of that year, Kim said there was no longer any reason to stay bound by the moratorium, citing continued joint drills and U.S. shipments of advanced weapons to the South (KCNA, January 1, 2020, as reported by NPR). North Korea resumed ICBM flight tests in March 2022 and has not flight-tested an ICBM since the Hwasong-19 on October 31, 2024. Development has continued nonetheless, through ground tests of engines and motors and the unveiling of the new Hwasong-20 (the record behind Clisa Geopolitics forecast f78).

In 2026 North Korea made its status as a nuclear weapons state official party doctrine at the Ninth Party Congress in February, and on September 24 Foreign Minister Choe Son Hui declared that status irreversible and said that dialogue was incompatible with sanctions and military pressure. On the U.S. side, Trump unilaterally scaled back the Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise in August and has sought a meeting with Kim this year (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Korean Peninsula issue). Asked on October 12 whether he might meet Kim during his trip to the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen, China, in November, Trump said he might (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, October 13, 2026, via the Korea Times). Reuters then reported that U.S. and South Korean officials saw little chance of a meeting this year because Washington had received no answer from Pyongyang (Briefing, October 23, 2026), and on October 29 North Korea fired more than 10 short-range ballistic missiles (Briefing, October 29, 2026, citing South Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff). As of November 6, 2026, Clisa Geopolitics had found no announcement of a summit date, and in late October it lowered the probability of its forecast of a Trump-Kim summit by the end of the year (f05) from 25 percent to 20 percent.

Through Machiavelli's lens. First, chapter 18 says what to look at in judging a promise: not the character of the person who made it but whether the reasons that prompted it still exist. That holds with special force between states, which have no higher authority to enforce agreements (see the commitment problem in Introduction to International Relations, session 5). The 2018 moratorium lasted as long as Pyongyang had a reason to keep talking to Washington; after Hanoi collapsed, North Korea declared that the reason was gone. It pointed to continued joint drills, even though the United States had already scaled them back. The episode fits Machiavelli's observation that a prince never lacks plausible reasons for breaking faith. The observation cuts both ways, however: the U.S. suspension of exercises was likewise an oral announcement outside the joint statement, and Pyongyang also pointed to sanctions and what it called hostile policies that remained in place (this lecture's interpretation).

Second, appearance and reality. Summits produce results that can be seen with the eye, such as photographs and joint statements, while the matters that can be known only by the hand, such as the definition of denuclearization and the sequence of steps, tend to be left to working-level talks. Singapore is the precedent. The point applies to both sides. North Korea's flight-test moratorium is visible restraint, but its ground tests and new missiles show that it does not mean a halt in development. The U.S. administration, Clisa Geopolitics judges, has kept denuclearization alive as a stated goal, announcing after the U.S.-China summit in Beijing in May that the two sides had affirmed a "common goal" of denuclearization, while in practice moving toward a deal by scaling back exercises. Verification is the work of the hand, and as chapter 17 warns, the side that relies wholly on the other's word and takes no other precautions is the one in greatest danger.

The limits of the analogy. Applying a handbook of rule from 1513 to nuclear diplomacy in 2026 has clear limits. First, Machiavelli's prince is an individual whose state and life are one and the same, whereas a U.S. president's promises are constrained by Congress and the law. Second, the cost of breaking a promise in which nuclear weapons are at stake is far greater than the loss of one prince's state, and if both sides assume that the other's word can be broken at any moment, the assumption itself can raise the danger. Third, there are parties outside the room. A deal that covers only the ICBMs aimed at the U.S. homeland would leave in place the short-range missiles aimed at South Korea. Clisa Geopolitics puts at 75 percent the probability that, if a U.S.-North Korean summit takes place without South Korea formally taking part in the preparatory negotiations, the resulting agreement will contain no provisions on inter-Korean relations or on medium- and short-range missiles (f14). Machiavelli's framework sees the two princes who make a promise but not the third parties who bear its costs.

Finally, the framework should not be used to brand one side as untrustworthy. The logic of chapter 18 applies equally to Washington, Pyongyang, and Seoul. The question is not who is honest but how long both sides will have reasons to keep their word.

  1. When Machiavelli criticizes imaginary republics in chapter 15, what does he have in mind, and by what standard does he propose to judge a prince's conduct?
  2. In chapter 17, how does being feared differ from being hated? What does Machiavelli say a prince must do to avoid hatred?
  3. In what respects does the image of the fox and the lion in chapter 18 resemble Cicero's De Officiis, and in what respects does it differ?
  4. Using the river image in chapter 25, explain how Machiavelli's virtù differs from virtue in the moral sense.

Discussion questions

  1. Walzer's argument about dirty hands holds that a politician who does wrong for the community's sake must acknowledge it as wrong. If the outcome was good, why is such acknowledgment necessary? Discuss with reference to a decision in South Korea's modern history.
  2. Chapter 18 holds that managing appearances is essential to rule. Does the claim still hold when the press and social media track a politician's every move? If so, in what form?
  3. When is it rational to make a promise with a party you know may break it? Discuss with reference to the 2018 test moratorium and the suspension of joint exercises.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages of The Prince and the Discourses in classic English translations, works through with numbers the conditions under which promises hold and the limits of judging by appearances, and looks at research on how leaders actually assess the credibility of others.

Reading the original

The quotations from The Prince in this session, both below and in the main text, are taken from W. K. Marriott's English translation (Project Gutenberg eBook 1232), and those from the Discourses from Ninian Hill Thomson's translation (Project Gutenberg eBook 10827), with omissions marked by ellipses.

Passage 1. Knowing how to do wrong (The Prince, chapter 15)

...for a man who wishes to act entirely up to his professions of virtue soon meets with what destroys him among so much that is evil. Hence it is necessary for a prince wishing to hold his own to know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity. ... for if everything is considered carefully, it will be found that something which looks like virtue, if followed, would be his ruin; whilst something else, which looks like vice, yet followed brings him security and prosperity.

How to read it. The weight of the sentence falls not on "do wrong" but on "make use of it or not according to necessity." Machiavelli does not make evil a principle; he asks for the judgment to treat both good and evil as instruments. The difficulty is who decides what necessity requires. If the prince decides for himself, necessity can justify anything. Aristotle's law, "reason without desire" (session 3), is aimed at exactly this point.

Passage 2. Judging by the eye (The Prince, chapter 18)

Therefore it is unnecessary for a prince to have all the good qualities I have enumerated, but it is very necessary to appear to have them. ... men judge generally more by the eye than by the hand, because it belongs to everybody to see you, to few to come in touch with you. Every one sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are, and those few dare not oppose themselves to the opinion of the many, who have the majesty of the state to defend them...

How to read it. In session 2, Glaucon imagined the most unjust man enjoying the greatest reputation for justice; Machiavelli turns that thought experiment into a technique of rule. But he also states the conditions under which the technique works: only a few can verify the reality, and those few dare not contradict the many. As the means of touching with the hand multiply, the politics of appearance loses its force.

Passage 3. Promises made under compulsion (Discourses, book 3, chapter 42)

...there is no disgrace in not observing promises wrung from you by force; for promises thus extorted when they affect the public welfare will always be broken so soon as the pressure under which they were made is withdrawn, and that, too, without shame on the part of him who breaks them ... Nay, as between princes, not only are such compulsory promises broken when the force which extorted them is removed, but all other promises as well, are in like manner disregarded when the causes which led to them no longer operate.

How to read it. The chapter concerns the humiliating peace the Romans accepted from the Samnites at the Caudine Forks, which the Senate later refused to honor. Whether such conduct deserves praise, Machiavelli says, he has already considered in The Prince, and he leaves it there. The passage shows that the republican Machiavelli held the same view of promises as the author of The Prince.

Working it out

Problem 1. Reasons to keep a promise. If two states both keep a promise, each gains 3 a year. If one breaks it, that state gains 5 in the year it does so, but afterward the other side stops keeping its word too, and each gains only 1 a year. Future gains are converted to present value by multiplying by δ (between 0 and 1) for each year. (1) How large must δ be for keeping the promise to be better than breaking it? (2) How does the threshold change if the gain from breaking the promise rises from 5 to 7? (3) Relate this calculation to chapter 18's condition, "when the reasons that caused him to pledge it exist no longer."

Problem 2. The eye and the hand. Suppose the prior probability that another state is negotiating in good faith is 30 percent. A state negotiating in good faith refrains from ICBM flight tests for a year with a probability of 95 percent; because a pause is not costly, a state not negotiating in good faith does so with a probability of 80 percent. (1) After observing a year without tests, what is the probability that the other side is negotiating in good faith? (2) Suppose instead that a verifiable dismantlement of facilities is observed. A state negotiating in good faith would take such a step with a probability of 60 percent, one not negotiating in good faith with a probability of 5 percent. What is the posterior probability? (3) Explain the difference between the two results using chapter 18's distinction between the eye and the hand.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. Keeping the promise yields 3 + 3δ + 3δ² + ... = 3/(1 − δ). Breaking it yields 5 + δ/(1 − δ). Multiplying both sides of 3/(1 − δ) ≥ 5 + δ/(1 − δ) by (1 − δ) gives 3 ≥ 5(1 − δ) + δ, or 3 ≥ 5 − 4δ, so δ ≥ 0.5. The promise holds only if the future counts for at least half as much as the present.
  2. Now 3 ≥ 7(1 − δ) + δ, or 3 ≥ 7 − 6δ, so δ ≥ 2/3, or about 0.67. The greater the gain from breaking a promise, the more weight the future must carry to sustain it.
  3. The disappearance of the reasons for a promise shows up in the calculation in two ways. One is a fall in the value of the future relationship (δ), as when a side concludes that negotiations are over. The other is a rise in the gain from breaking the promise, as when flight tests become essential to developing a weapon. Either way, a promise can collapse even if the other side's character has not changed. Conversely, sustaining a promise requires arrangements that raise the value of the future relationship or reduce the gain from breaking faith.

Problem 2.

  1. Apply Bayes' rule. The probability of good faith and no tests is 0.3 × 0.95 = 0.285; the probability of bad faith and no tests is 0.7 × 0.8 = 0.56. The posterior is 0.285 ÷ 0.845 ≈ 0.34, or about 34 percent, barely changed from 30 percent.
  2. With 0.3 × 0.6 = 0.18 and 0.7 × 0.05 = 0.035, the posterior is 0.18 ÷ 0.215 ≈ 0.84, or about 84 percent.
  3. Refraining from tests is a signal of the eye: a state in good faith and one in bad faith can both send it easily, so it says little about which kind of state the other side is. Verifiable dismantlement is a signal of the hand: it is costly for a state acting in bad faith, so it shifts the assessment sharply. International relations scholars call this a costly signal. Machiavelli could advise princes to manage appearances because people judged mostly by the eye; read the other way, the calculation shows what the side receiving a promise ought to ask for (this lecture's interpretation).

Evidence and counterarguments

Press (2005): leaders look at present calculations, not the past. In Calculating Credibility, Daryl G. Press examined the records of the European crises of the late 1930s, the Berlin crises of 1958–1962, and the Cuban missile crisis of 1962. He concluded that when leaders judge whether an adversary's threats are credible, they look mainly at present power and interests rather than at whether the adversary kept its word in the past. The finding fits Machiavelli's view that a promise is as credible as the reasons for keeping it that exist now.

Weisiger and Yarhi-Milo (2015): past actions matter too. Alex Weisiger and Keren Yarhi-Milo challenged that conclusion, arguing that a focus on decision-making in crises misses the effect of reputation on general deterrence. Using data on interstate disputes, they found that a state's past actions are closely linked to whether it is later challenged, and that the effect extends beyond the original adversary (International Organization 69, no. 2). Reputation is not everything, but it cannot be ignored, a result consistent with Machiavelli's warnings against being hated or despised (chapters 17 and 19; this lecture's interpretation).
