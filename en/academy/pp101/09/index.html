The question for this session

The thinkers met in earlier sessions grounded politics in virtue (Aristotle), security (Hobbes), natural rights (Locke), or the general will (Rousseau). In late eighteenth-century England, Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) proposed a far simpler standard: a law or policy is right if it increases people's pleasure and reduces their pain. His successor, John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), refined the standard, arguing that pleasures differ in quality and that justice and individual liberty ultimately rest on the same foundation.

Utilitarianism lies at the root of the cost-benefit analysis governments use today, and every such calculation raises two questions. Whose pain goes into the sum? And how can very different kinds of pain be put on the same scale? This session examines Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (printed 1780, published 1789) and Mill's Utilitarianism (serialized 1861, published as a book 1863) and On Liberty (1859). It then asks those two questions of the sanctions on Russia and the energy-price shock of 2026.

1. Two sovereign masters

Bentham's book opens with a famous sentence:

Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do. (chapter 1, paragraph 1)

The sentence makes two claims at once: a psychological claim that people in fact seek pleasure and avoid pain, and an ethical claim that what people ought to do is also settled by pleasure and pain.

From this follows the principle of utility. Any action is right if it tends to increase the happiness of those whose interests are at stake, and wrong if it tends to diminish it. Bentham made clear that the principle applies not only to the actions of private individuals but to "every measure of government" (1.2). His concern was legislation from the start.

Bentham also dissects the phrase "the interest of the community." The community, he writes, is "a fictitious body," composed of individuals, and its interest is nothing more than "the sum of the interests of the several members who compose it" (1.4). Talk of national glory that cannot be broken down into individual pleasures and pains is, on this view, empty. This starting point is the opposite of Aristotle's, for whom the polis comes before the individual (session 3).

2. Seven measures of pleasure and pain

The legislator, Bentham argues, must know how large pleasures and pains are, and chapter 4 sets out the measures. For a single person considering a single pleasure or pain, there are four: intensity, duration, certainty, and propinquity (how soon it will come) (4.2). When judging the consequences of an act, two more apply: fecundity, the chance of being followed by sensations of the same kind, and purity, the chance of not being followed by sensations of the opposite kind (4.3). When many people are involved, a seventh is added: extent, the number of persons affected (4.4).

Measure What it asks Example
Intensity How strong is it? A home too cold to live in versus slightly pricier fuel
Duration How long does it last? A month of inflation versus a recession lasting years
Certainty How sure is it? Today's fuel bill versus an eventual end to the war
Propinquity How near is it? This winter versus a decade from now
Fecundity Will more of the same follow? The pleasure of learning that leads to further pleasures
Purity Will the opposite not follow? The pain that follows the pleasure of heavy drinking
Extent How many people does it affect? A village versus a country

The procedure is concrete. Begin with the person most directly affected, add up that person's pleasures and pains, repeat the exercise for everyone affected, and strike the overall balance (4.5). Bentham conceded that the process cannot be "strictly pursued previously to every moral judgment, or to every legislative or judicial operation," but that it "may, however, be always kept in view" (4.6). The calculus works less as a form to be filled in than as a checklist to make sure nothing is left out.

Lecture 2 of Harvard's Justice exposes the calculus's uncomfortable implications through the case of the Mignonette, decided by an English court in 1884. Shipwrecked sailors killed and ate the ailing cabin boy to survive; the court convicted the captain, Tom Dudley, and the mate, Edwin Stephens, of murder, though their death sentences were later commuted to six months in prison. If it is better for three to survive and one to die than for all four to die, does utilitarianism permit the killing? Sandel uses the case to draw out two objections: that utilitarianism fails to respect individual rights, and that not every value can be converted into a single currency.

3. Mill's revision: pleasures differ in quality

Mill was raised on utilitarianism by his father, James Mill, Bentham's friend and ally. In Utilitarianism he states the creed this way: actions are "right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness," where happiness means "pleasure, and the absence of pain" (chapter 2).

Critics of the day dismissed the theory as "a doctrine worthy only of swine." Mill's reply was that pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity. If, of two pleasures, nearly everyone who has experienced both decidedly prefers one, regardless of any sense of moral obligation, that one is the more desirable. He concludes:

It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. And if the fool, or the pig, is of a different opinion, it is because they only know their own side of the question. The other party to the comparison knows both sides. (chapter 2)

When people choose lower pleasures, Mill holds, it is out of weakness of will, or because their capacity for higher pleasures has withered under the conditions of their lives. The reason a being with higher faculties never wishes to sink lower, he says, is "a sense of dignity."

The revision came at a price. Bentham's view had an egalitarian strength: a pleasure of a given size had the same value whoever enjoyed it, a view often summed up in the line attributed to him that pushpin is as good as poetry. By ranking pleasures according to the preferences of those who have experienced both, Mill made utilitarianism more dignified, but he left a new question open: who counts as such a judge?

4. Impartiality and justice

Mill insisted again and again that the standard of utilitarianism "is not the agent's own happiness, but that of all concerned." Between his own happiness and that of others, he wrote, utilitarianism requires the agent "to be as strictly impartial as a disinterested and benevolent spectator" (chapter 2). He summed up this impartiality with a maxim he attributed to Bentham: "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one" (chapter 5).

The conclusion is radical. If my family and a stranger's, citizens and foreigners, people alive today and the next generation suffer equal pain, that pain carries equal weight. Section 7 considers what this implies for sanctions and energy policy.

The oldest objection to utilitarianism is that it collides with justice. May one innocent person be sacrificed for the happiness of the many? Mill devoted all of chapter 5 of Utilitarianism to the objection. His answer is that justice is not a principle separate from utility but its most important part:

To have a right, then, is, I conceive, to have something which society ought to defend me in the possession of. If the objector goes on to ask why it ought, I can give him no other reason than general utility. (chapter 5)

Mill traces the unusual force of the sentiment of justice to security. Other goods are needed by some people and not by others, but security is "to every one's feelings the most vital of all interests." Violating a right damages not only the victim but the sense of security on which everyone depends, so it weighs far more than ordinary benefits. By this route Mill sought to ground rights and justice within utilitarianism.

5. The harm principle: On Liberty

On Liberty shows how far utilitarianism can go in protecting individual freedom (the book also figured in session 8, alongside the tyranny of the majority). In its first chapter Mill advances a single principle:

...the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant. (chapter 1)

The state and public opinion may coerce only conduct that harms others; in conduct that concerns only oneself, "the individual is sovereign" over "his own body and mind." He may be reasoned with or entreated, but not compelled.

The principle is not a theory of rights independent of utility. Mill took utility as "the ultimate appeal on all ethical questions," but insisted that it be "utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being" (chapter 1). Liberty, on this argument, fosters individuality and the diversity of ideas, and so increases the happiness of society as a whole over time.

Mill applied the principle only to "human beings in the maturity of their faculties," excluding children and "those backward states of society" he regarded as still in their "nonage" (chapter 1). That proviso is now criticized as a rationale for colonial rule.

6. Classics and theory: the debate over utilitarianism

After Bentham and Mill, utilitarianism was refined even as it became the most frequently attacked theory in political philosophy.

Thinker Argument Relation to Bentham and Mill
Sidgwick, The Methods of Ethics (1874) Morality must take "the point of view of the Universe," from which one's own happiness matters no more than anyone else's Gave Mill's impartiality its most rigorous form, while admitting he could not finally reconcile self-interest with impartial duty
Rawls, A Theory of Justice (1971) Utilitarianism "does not take seriously the distinction between persons" Rejects trading one person's pain against another's pleasure as if they were one person's pains and pleasures at different times (session 12)
Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) Suffering that can be prevented without great sacrifice should be prevented, regardless of distance or nationality Extends Bentham's "extent" and Mill's impartiality beyond national borders
Williams, Utilitarianism: For and Against (1973) Utilitarianism ignores the integrity of agents, their commitment to their own projects and convictions Argues that a calculus of summed outcomes erases the question of who did what
Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) Few people would choose to plug into an "experience machine" that supplies any experience they desire Targets Bentham's premise that pleasure is the only good

7. Applying it to 2026: whose pain counts in sanctions and energy policy?

The facts. The Iran war that began on February 28, 2026, effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, and the United States, which imposed a naval blockade on Iran on April 12, reimposed it on July 14 after a June memorandum of understanding collapsed (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Iran issue). Qatari-mediated talks continued in Doha into early November, but Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said there would be neither a final deal nor a reopening of the strait until the U.S. naval blockade ended and Iran's demands were met (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, November 3, 2026, via Reuters). The burden of energy prices spread widely. On October 13 the International Monetary Fund projected that oil would average $96 a barrel this year, a rise of nearly 41 percent (Briefing, October 14). On October 14 the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that consumer prices in September were 3.7 percent higher than a year earlier, with gasoline up 34.2 percent (Briefing, October 15). On October 9, EU gas storage stood at 73.5 percent, 16.9 percentage points below the five-year average, and disruptions to Qatari LNG supplies continued (the record behind forecast f74). South Korea responded to the shock with a $17 billion supplementary budget, a cap on fuel prices, and an accelerated restart of nuclear reactors (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Iran issue).

Under this price pressure, Washington loosened part of its sanctions on Russia. After a phone call with President Vladimir Putin on October 9 (U.S. time), President Donald Trump wrote that Russia would supply more than 300,000 tons of diesel immediately and 500,000 tons in November. The same day, the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) issued General License 135, allowing Russian diesel to be supplied to world markets; the license runs through April 7, 2027. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky objected that easing sanctions without a clear and lasting de-escalation agreement "plays into Russia's hands" (Briefing, October 10, via the Kremlin, the U.S. Treasury, and CBS). On October 16, Trump signed an executive order waiving most of the tariffs and sanctions in the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, which was due to take effect on October 18, including the 100 percent tariffs on the five largest buyers of Russian oil and gas, with no end date (Briefing, October 17, via the White House). The EU moved the other way: on October 12 it added 826 Russian defense companies and 743 of their chief executives to its sanctions list (Briefing, October 13, via the Council of the EU).

Through Bentham's lens. In the utilitarian ledger, sanctions are an instrument for inflicting pain. They aim to reduce the suffering of the people under attack by weakening the aggressor's capacity to wage war, and several groups bear the cost: ordinary people in the target country, consumers in the sanctioning countries who absorb higher prices, and households and businesses in energy-importing third countries such as South Korea. Because Bentham's principle concerns the happiness of "all those whose interest is in question" (his note of 1822 to 1.1), none of these groups can be left out of the sum (this lecture's interpretation).

Bentham's measures also explain why sanctions are politically hard to sustain. The fuel bills consumers pay are certain and near; the benefit of a shorter or less destructive war is uncertain and remote. A calculus that weighs certainty and propinquity will tend to count the first heavily and the second lightly. That much is consistent with Bentham's method, but treating an uncertain benefit as zero is not calculation; it is omission (this lecture's interpretation). Fecundity and purity are useful here too. Defenders of the diesel license can argue that cheaper fuel brings further relief through lower inflation and steadier growth, a fecund pleasure. Critics can argue that the revenue funds Russia's war and so produces greater pain later, which makes the relief an impure pleasure. Which side is right is an empirical question: how much of Russia's added revenue goes to the war, and how far diesel prices actually fall.

The demand for impartiality collides with democratic politics. Elected governments have strong incentives to weigh their own voters' pain above that of foreigners. On October 22, ahead of the November 3 midterm elections, Trump claimed that Iran was "making up fake hits, mines" to push up oil prices before the vote (Briefing, October 23), a sign of how sensitive fuel prices are at election time. Mill's "disinterested and benevolent spectator" demands that this tilt be corrected. Yet the idea that each government owes its first duty to its own citizens can itself be defended in utilitarian terms: a division of labor in which each looks after those nearest may produce more happiness overall (this lecture's interpretation).

Through Mill's harm principle. The harm principle holds that the only legitimate purpose of coercion is to prevent harm to others, and sanctions meant to halt an invasion fit that structure in part (this lecture's interpretation). But Mill's principle concerns coercion by the state against individuals within one society, and it says nothing about who bears the cost of coercion. The pain of sanctions can fall more heavily on people who had no part in the decision, and on third-country consumers, than on the decision-makers who caused the harm (see the discussion of Pape in Introduction to International Relations, session 9). On that question of distribution, chapter 5 of Utilitarianism offers a clue. One can argue that the security of people under attack outweighs consumers' convenience, but a household unable to heat its home in midwinter also faces a question of security, so the calculation is not simple.

The measurement problem. No unit converts a 34 percent jump in American gasoline prices, European heating bills, the lost income of Russian households, and a Ukrainian winter of blackouts into a common currency. Real policy converts everything into money, yet the same sum inflicts far greater pain on a poor household (see Problem 2 in Going deeper). The limits of cost-benefit analysis, the subject of lecture 3 of Justice, show up here in full.

The limits of the analogy. First, the aims of sanctions include things hard to convert into a sum of pain: upholding the norm that aggression carries a price, or the credibility of alliances. Utilitarianism tries to bring these in as long-run consequences, but those are the most uncertain and remote items in the ledger. Second, a calculus of summed outcomes erases the question of who caused the suffering. Whether it is right to put the party that started a war on the same scale as those who pay to reduce its harm echoes the question Williams raised, and Kant gives a different answer (session 10). Third, the forecasts above track government choices and market prices; they do not measure whose happiness changed, or by how much.

Check your understanding

  1. Why did Bentham call the community a "fictitious body," and how did he define its interest?
  2. List Bentham's seven measures and explain how fecundity and purity differ from the first four.
  3. How does Mill distinguish the quality of pleasures? How does the "Socrates dissatisfied" sentence support that test?
  4. How does Mill explain rights and justice in terms of utility, and why does he treat security as special?
  5. Using examples, distinguish the cases in which the harm principle permits the state to coerce an individual from those in which it does not.

Discussion questions

  1. When South Korea's government sets energy policy or decides whether to join sanctions, should it weigh foreigners' pain exactly as it weighs its own citizens'? If not, how can the difference be justified?
  2. How should the certain, near-term pain of consumers be compared with the uncertain, distant benefit of a shorter war? Is uncertainty alone a good reason to discount the latter?
  3. Should government aim to raise citizens' satisfaction, or to cultivate their capacity for what Mill called higher pleasures? What does Nozick's experience machine suggest?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages from Bentham and Mill in the original English, works through a felicific calculus of sanctions and the diminishing marginal utility of money, and looks at research on measuring happiness and on the humanitarian costs of sanctions.

Reading the original

Bentham is quoted from the English text of An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation on Wikisource; the numbers are Bentham's own chapter and paragraph numbers. Mill is quoted from the Project Gutenberg editions of Utilitarianism (eBook 11224, seventh edition, 1879) and On Liberty (eBook 34901). Omissions are marked by ellipses.

Passage 1. The two masters and the community (Bentham, Introduction, 1.1 and 1.4)

Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do. On the one hand the standard of right and wrong, on the other the chain of causes and effects, are fastened to their throne. ... The community is a fictitious body, composed of the individual persons who are considered as constituting as it were its members. The interest of the community then is, what is it?— the sum of the interests of the several members who compose it.

How to read it. Bentham put "ought" and "shall" in the same sentence, and later philosophers objected that no ought follows from an is. Paragraph 4, which treats the community as a sum of individual interests, can strip the phrase "national interest" of the private interests it sometimes hides. But a sum alone conceals who gains and who loses.

Passage 2. The quality of pleasures (Mill, Utilitarianism, chapter 2)

If I am asked, what I mean by difference of quality in pleasures, or what makes one pleasure more valuable than another, merely as a pleasure, except its being greater in amount, there is but one possible answer. Of two pleasures, if there be one to which all or almost all who have experience of both give a decided preference, irrespective of any feeling of moral obligation to prefer it, that is the more desirable pleasure. ... It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.

How to read it. Mill's test is the preference of the experienced, which anticipates today's practice of measuring well-being by asking people directly. But those who have "experience of both" tend to be the educated, and the test has long been criticized for elevating one group's tastes into a universal standard.

Passage 3. The harm principle (Mill, On Liberty, chapter 1)

The object of this Essay is to assert one very simple principle ... That principle is, that the sole end for which mankind are warranted, individually or collectively, in interfering with the liberty of action of any of their number, is self-protection. That the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. ... In the part which merely concerns himself, his independence is, of right, absolute. Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign.

How to read it. Mill applied the principle not only to legal penalties but to "the moral coercion of public opinion," the same concern as Tocqueville's warning about the pressure of the majority (session 8). What counts as harm, and whether offense counts, remains the central dispute over the principle.

Working it out

Problem 1. A felicific calculus of sanctions. Hypothetical country A imposes sanctions on country B, which has invaded country C. Assume pains and pleasures can be measured in common units. A's 100 million consumers each suffer 1 unit of pain, B's 100 million residents each suffer 2 units, and 200 million consumers in energy-importing country D each suffer 0.5 units, all with certainty. If the sanctions end the war sooner, C's 50 million residents are each spared 20 units of pain; the probability of that is p. (1) With p = 0.3, what is the expected net effect of the sanctions? (2) Find the minimum p at which the sanctions are justified. (3) If A's government leaves B's residents and D's consumers out of the calculation, how does the verdict change at p = 0.3?

Problem 2. The same sum, different pain. Assume utility is proportional to the natural log of income, so that each additional won adds less happiness the richer one is (diminishing marginal utility). A household earning 20 million won a year and one earning 200 million won each pay an extra 2 million won because of higher energy prices. (1) Calculate and compare the loss of utility for each household (ln(10/9) ≈ 0.105; ln(100/99) ≈ 0.010). (2) What does the result imply for applying the principle that everybody counts for one when costs are measured in money?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. The certain pains are 100 million units in A, 200 million in B, and 100 million in D, for a total of 400 million. The expected benefit is 50 million × 20 × 0.3 = 300 million units. The expected net effect is 300 million − 400 million = −100 million units, so the sanctions are not justified.
  2. The expected benefit, 1 billion × p, must exceed 400 million, so p > 0.4. The same sanctions can flip from unjustified to justified depending on the estimated chance of shortening the war, which is why Bentham's measure of certainty sits at the center of the calculation.
  3. The only pain left is A's 100 million units, so the result is 300 million − 100 million = +200 million units, and the sanctions are justified. Who is included matters as much as the probability. Impartiality yields the verdict in (1); a selective calculus that drops B's residents and D's consumers yields the verdict in (3).

Problem 2.

  1. The poorer household loses ln(2,000) − ln(1,800) = ln(10/9) ≈ 0.105; the richer one loses ln(20,000) − ln(19,800) = ln(100/99) ≈ 0.010. The same 2 million won inflicts roughly 10 times as much pain on a household with one-tenth the income.
  2. Counting each person as one is not the same as counting each won as one. Summing costs in money understates the pain of poorer households, so for a given total cost utilitarianism tends to favor arrangements that lighten the burden on the poor (this lecture's interpretation). The log form of utility is an assumption made for the sake of the calculation.

Evidence and counterarguments

Kahneman and colleagues (1997, 2023): back to Bentham. In "Back to Bentham?" Daniel Kahneman, Peter P. Wakker, and Rakesh Sarin argued that economists' notion of utility could be redefined, and measured, as Bentham's "experienced utility," the pleasure and pain people actually feel (Quarterly Journal of Economics 112, no. 2). In a 2023 joint study, Matthew A. Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Barbara Mellers reported that well-being generally rises with the log of income, and that the plateau above a certain income appears only among the least happy 20 percent (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 120, no. 10). That lends some support to the log assumption in Problem 2, and also warns that happiness moves differently across groups.

Gutmann and colleagues (2021): the humanitarian cost of sanctions. Using data on 98 developing and newly industrialized countries from 1977 to 2012, Jerg Gutmann, Matthias Neuenkirch, and Florian Neumeier estimated that an average episode of UN sanctions reduced life expectancy in the target country by about 1.2 to 1.4 years, and U.S. sanctions by 0.4 to 0.5 years (Journal of Development Studies 57, no. 1). The pain borne by the target's population is no small item. Because the sample covers developing and newly industrialized countries through 2012, however, the results cannot be carried over directly to the sanctions imposed since 2022 on a large energy exporter like Russia.

Counterargument: measurement is not the whole problem. However good measurement becomes, the central objections to utilitarianism remain. Rawls rejected trading pains and pleasures across persons as such, and Williams held that summing outcomes erases the agent's responsibility and integrity. Many political philosophers today conclude that utilitarianism is strong at bringing every cost and benefit of a policy into view, but needs other principles to decide how those costs and benefits should be distributed.
