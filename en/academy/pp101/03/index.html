The question for this session

Plato held that the wisest should rule (session 2). His student Aristotle asked a different question. If no such person can be found, and if even the best of people are not free of desire and anger, who should rule? He gave two answers: law should rule rather than people, and the states that last longest are those with a broad middle class between the rich and the poor.

This session reads books 1, 3, and 4 of the Politics to see where those two answers come from. It then applies Aristotle's framework to the situation just after the U.S. midterm elections of November 3, 2026, and to one question in particular: can Congress limit the president's power to make war?

1. Human beings are political animals

The Politics begins with the origins of community. Man and woman, and master and slave, come together to form a household; households come together to form a village; and villages come together to form the polis, or city-state, a self-sufficient community. Aristotle concludes: "Hence it is evident that a city is a natural production, and that man is naturally a political animal" (book 1, chapter 2, 1253a).

"Political" here does not mean taking a keen interest in parties or elections. It means that people live a fully human life, a good life, only when they live together in a polis and decide common affairs together. The basis for this is speech (logos). Other animals use their voices to signal pleasure and pain, but human beings can use speech to express "what is useful for us, and what is hurtful, and of course what is just and what is unjust" (1253a). Because they are animals that can deliberate together about justice, they form political communities. A being that "is incapable of society, or so complete in himself as not to want it," is therefore "a beast or a god" (1253a).

This starting point is the reverse of modern social contract theory. Hobbes and Locke begin with individuals who exist before the state, in the state of nature, and who create the state by contract (sessions 5 and 6). For Aristotle, the city "naturally precedes" the individual (1253a). Just as a hand severed from the body is no longer really a hand, a person outside the polis is not a complete human being.

2. Citizens and the six regimes

Aristotle defines a citizen as one who "has a right to a share in the judicial and executive part of government" in a city (book 3, chapter 1, 1275b). The definition takes as its model a state like Athens, where citizens governed directly as jurors and members of the assembly, and Aristotle himself concedes that it fits democracy best.

He then classifies regimes (politeia in Greek) by two criteria. One is who holds supreme power: one person, a few, or many. The other is whose interest that power serves: the common interest or the rulers' own (book 3, chapter 7, 1279a–b).

Who rules In the common interest (correct regimes) In the rulers' interest (deviant regimes)
One Kingship Tyranny
Few Aristocracy Oligarchy
Many Polity (mixed regime) Democracy

Notice that democracy falls among the deviant regimes. For Aristotle, democracy is a regime in which the poor majority rules in its own interest alone. For the correct form, in which the many rule for the common good, he simply used politeia, the general word for any constitution. In English it is usually rendered "polity" and sometimes "mixed regime" or "constitutional government."

The real difference between oligarchy and democracy is wealth, not numbers (book 3, chapter 8, 1279b–1280a). Aristotle unsettles his own table. If the rich are the majority in a state and that majority rules, is it a democracy? If the poor are a minority but seize power, is it an oligarchy? His conclusion is that whether the rulers are many or few is a matter of accident; it only appears to matter because in every state the poor are many and the rich are few. What separates oligarchy from democracy is whether the rulers are rich or poor. In today's terms, a regime's character depends less on the form of its institutions than on the economic interests of whoever holds power.

3. The wisdom of the many

Who, then, should rule? Aristotle gives serious consideration to one argument for rule by the many (book 3, chapter 11). Even if no single member of the many is outstanding, taken together they may be better than a few outstanding individuals. He compares this to a potluck feast, which can surpass a dinner given at one person's expense. Although each individual knows less about public affairs than those who have made a study of them, "when they come together they will know them better, or at least not worse" (1282a). He offers further examples: the person who lives in a house can judge it better than the one who built it, and the steersman who uses a tiller judges it better than the carpenter who made it.

He attaches a condition to the argument, however: "if the people are not very brutal indeed." He also separates what should be left to the many from what should not. Rather than holding the highest offices themselves, the many are better suited to electing officials and reviewing their conduct in office. The arrangement resembles the basic structure of modern representative democracy, in which citizens choose and judge their representatives while the details of policy are left to those representatives and to experts.

4. The rule of law: reason without desire

In book 3, chapter 16, Aristotle examines absolute kingship, in which the single best person rules unbound by law, and objects:

...it is more proper that law should govern than any one of the citizens ... he who would place the supreme power in mind, would place it in God and the laws; but he who entrusts man with it, gives it to a wild beast, for such his appetites sometimes make him; for passion influences those who are in power, even the very best of men: for which reason law is reason without desire. (1287a)

The passage can be read as a rebuttal of Plato's philosopher-king. Aristotle, however, presents these arguments as views that many people hold, and in the very next chapter (book 3, chapter 17, 1288a) he allows an exception: if one person surpasses everyone else in virtue, that person's kingship is just. The problem is not the ruler's wisdom but the ruler's desires. Even the wise, once in power, grow angry and covetous and judge in their own favor. Law is a judgment made in advance, in a calm state of mind, before such desires can intrude.

Aristotle also recognizes the limits of law. Law consists of general rules and cannot settle every particular case in advance. He therefore leaves what the law does not determine to the judgment of officials, but those officials must be "only guardians, and the servants of the laws" (1287a). Elsewhere he discusses equity (epieikeia), which corrects the law when its generality produces an unjust result in a particular case (Nicomachean Ethics, book 5, chapter 10).

5. A state with a broad middle class

In book 4, Aristotle asks not about the ideal regime but about the best regime that most states can actually achieve. His answer is a polity governed by the middle class (hoi mesoi) (book 4, chapter 11, 1295b–1296a).

His reasoning runs as follows. The very rich do not know how to obey and know how to rule only as masters do, while the very poor become so abject that they do not know how to rule and can only obey. A city made up of such people consists of "slaves and masters, not freemen," in which "one party must hate, and the other despise." The middle class, by contrast, "will neither covet what belongs to others, as the poor do; nor will others covet what is theirs, as the poor do what belongs to the rich." Aristotle concludes:

It is plain, then, that the most perfect political community must be amongst those who are in the middle rank, and those states are best instituted wherein these are a larger and more respectable part, if possible, than both the other; or, if that cannot be, at least than either of them separate; so that being thrown into the balance it may prevent either scale from preponderating. (1295b)

He also warns of the opposite case. When some have too much and others nothing at all, the state slides into extreme democracy or unmixed oligarchy, and tyranny arises out of either extreme (1296a). The broader the middle class, he holds, the fewer the factional conflicts and revolts, and large states are more stable than small ones precisely because they have more people in the middle.

6. Classics and theory: from the mixed regime to checks and balances

Aristotle's two prescriptions, the rule of law and the mixed regime, became one of the main currents of Western constitutional thought.

Thinker Argument Relation to Aristotle
Polybius, Histories, book 6 (second century BC) Rome is strong because its constitution mixes the consuls (monarchy), the Senate (aristocracy), and the popular assemblies (democracy) Shifts the mixed regime from a mix of classes to a mix of institutions
Montesquieu, The Spirit of the Laws (1748) Liberty is secure only when power is divided into legislative, executive, and judicial branches that check one another Gives the rule of law concrete institutional form as the separation of powers
Madison, The Federalist No. 51 (1788) "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." Since rulers' desires cannot be eliminated, they are set against one another
Lipset, "Some Social Requisites of Democracy" (1959) Economic development and a broad middle class stabilize democracy Tests the middle-class argument with statistics

7. Applying it to 2026: can the U.S. Congress stop a war?

The facts. In the U.S. midterm elections of November 3, 2026, Democrats took Republican Senate seats in North Carolina, Ohio, Texas, Kansas, Iowa, and South Carolina and won control of the Senate, the AP reported (results not yet certified). Major networks projected that Democrats would also win a majority in the House (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, November 4, 2026). Meanwhile, asked in a Time interview published October 1 whether he might step up strikes on Iran after the midterms, President Donald Trump said it was possible. On October 31, a news report said that Washington had told Gulf allies that strikes could come as early as November 4. On the night of November 2, U.S. forces carried out a limited strike on air-defense sites around Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, which Trump called a "minor attack" (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, November 4, 2026).

The U.S. Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war (Article I, Section 8), but it makes the president commander in chief of the armed forces (Article II, Section 2). The War Powers Resolution of 1973 requires the president, after sending forces into hostilities without congressional authorization, to report to Congress within 48 hours and to end their use within 60 days unless Congress authorizes it (with a 30-day extension allowed to ensure a safe withdrawal). Besides its power over appropriations, Congress can restrict military action by resolution in two ways. A joint resolution requires the president's signature, and if the president vetoes it, it must pass again with a two-thirds vote in each chamber. A concurrent resolution does not go to the president and so cannot be vetoed, but since the Supreme Court's 1983 decision in INS v. Chadha, it has been disputed whether such a resolution legally binds the president. In 2026, the House passed resolutions restricting the president's military action against Iran three times, but the Senate rejected one 49–50 on September 24 (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Iran issue).

Through Aristotle's lens. The decision to go to war is the one that most needs "reason without desire," yet it is also the one most readily left to a single person's judgment, on the grounds that military operations must be swift and secret. Following the logic of 1287a, a check is needed for precisely that reason, since passion sways even the very best of men (this lecture's interpretation). It is worth remembering, though, that Aristotle himself thought an arrangement placing military command in one person's hands for life could coexist with any constitution (book 3, chapters 14 and 16). By separating the power to declare war from the power to command, the U.S. Constitution subjects one person's judgment to the deliberation of the many. The structure also echoes the idea from section 3 that the many should be entrusted with electing officials and reviewing their conduct.

As section 2 showed, Aristotle looked past how many rule to whether the rulers are rich or poor. Seen the same way, the mere fact that Democrats are set to hold the majority in the new Senate that convenes in January 2027 does not by itself produce a real check. A joint resolution still faces the veto and the two-thirds threshold; a concurrent resolution faces the dispute over whether it is binding. Whether the check actually works can be judged only by watching both whether a resolution passes and how the administration responds afterward.

Through the middle-class argument. The share of American adults living in middle-income households fell from 61 percent in 1971 to 51 percent in 2023 (Pew Research Center, May 2024). By Aristotle's logic, as the middle class thins, politics tilts toward the extremes and fewer forces remain to act as a counterweight. Some studies find that income inequality and political polarization have grown together, but whether polarization can be explained by the income distribution alone remains in dispute (see the empirical studies in Going deeper).

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages of the Politics in a classic English translation, works through the wisdom of the many with numbers, and looks at research that has tested the link between the middle class and the stability of democracy.

Reading the original

The quotations from the Politics in this session, both below and in the main text, are taken from William Ellis's English translation (first published 1776; Dent edition, 1912; Project Gutenberg eBook 6762), with omissions marked by ellipses. The numbers are Bekker page numbers, the standard way of citing Aristotle's works.

Passage 1. The political animal (Politics, book 1, chapter 2, 1253a)

Hence it is evident that a city is a natural production, and that man is naturally a political animal, and that whosoever is naturally and not accidentally unfit for society, must be either inferior or superior to man ... it is by speech that we are enabled to express what is useful for us, and what is hurtful, and of course what is just and what is unjust: for in this particular man differs from other animals, that he alone has a perception of good and evil, of just and unjust ...

How to read it. Human beings are political not because they live in groups but because they can deliberate together, through speech, about what is just. Bees and herd animals live in groups too, but they do not argue over what is just. Aristotle's starting point, then, is that politics is a shared judgment about justice before it is a distribution of power.

Passage 2. Wealth and poverty divide regimes (Politics, book 3, chapter 8, 1279b–1280a)

It seems therefore evident to reason, that whether the supreme power is vested in the hands of many or few may be a matter of accident; but that it is clear enough, that when it is in the hands of the few, it will be a government of the rich; when in the hands of the many, it will be a government of the poor; since in all countries there are many poor and few rich: it is not therefore the cause that has been already assigned (namely, the number of people in power) that makes the difference between the two governments; but an oligarchy and democracy differ in this from each other, in the poverty of those who govern in the one, and the riches of those who govern in the other ...

How to read it. This passage is often cited as a class analysis of regimes written more than 2,000 years before Marx (session 11). Aristotle's conclusion, however, is not class struggle but a balance between the classes, struck by a broad middle class.

Passage 3. The rule of law (Politics, book 3, chapter 16, 1287a)

...it is more proper that law should govern than any one of the citizens: upon the same principle, if it is advantageous to place the supreme power in some particular persons, they should be appointed to be only guardians, and the servants of the laws ... Moreover, he who would place the supreme power in mind, would place it in God and the laws; but he who entrusts man with it, gives it to a wild beast, for such his appetites sometimes make him; for passion influences those who are in power, even the very best of men: for which reason law is reason without desire.

How to read it. In the same chapter Aristotle also takes up the objection that law cannot determine everything. His answer is that what the law leaves undetermined should be left to the discretion of officials, but that this discretion, too, should operate within limits the law sets, and that the law should be amended whenever experience shows that a better rule can be established. The rule of law does not eliminate human judgment; it places human judgment within the framework of law.

Working it out

Problem 1. The wisdom of the many (Condorcet's jury theorem). Suppose each citizen picks the right answer to a question with a probability of 0.6 and that each judges independently. The question is decided by majority vote. (1) With three citizens, what is the probability that the majority is right? (2) Why is a majority of three more accurate than one person? (3) If each individual's probability of being right is 0.4, is a majority of three more or less accurate than one person? How does this relate to Aristotle's condition, "if the people are not very brutal indeed"?

Problem 2. The counterweight. A country's electorate is made up of 20 percent wealthy voters, x percent middle-class voters, and (80 − x) percent poor voters. Assume that the wealthy and the poor always vote on opposite sides and that the middle class votes as a single bloc. (1) Find the range of x for which the side the middle class takes decides the outcome. (2) How large must x be for the middle class to be, in Aristotle's words, larger than the other two classes combined?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. The majority is right if at least two of the three are right. The probability that all three are right is 0.6³ = 0.216, and the probability that exactly two are right is 3 × 0.6² × 0.4 = 0.432. Together they come to 0.648.
  2. Even if one person is wrong, the outcome is right as long as the other two are right. If each individual's probability of being right is greater than 0.5 and their judgments are independent, the probability that the majority is right approaches 1 as the number of voters grows. Condorcet set out this theorem in 1785; a rigorous proof came later.
  3. If each individual's probability of being right is 0.4, a majority of three is right with a probability of 0.4³ + 3 × 0.4² × 0.6 = 0.064 + 0.288 = 0.352, which is lower than one person's 0.4. As the number of voters grows, the probability that the majority is wrong approaches 1. This matches Aristotle's condition: the wisdom of the many holds only when each person has at least a minimum of judgment. The condition that judgments be independent matters as well. If everyone follows the same faulty source of information, the advantage of majority rule disappears.

Problem 2.

  1. The middle class decides the outcome only if neither the wealthy (20) nor the poor (80 − x) has a majority (more than 50) on its own. At 20, the wealthy never have a majority. For the poor to fall short of one, 80 − x ≤ 50, or x ≥ 30. In addition, whichever side the middle class joins must then have a majority; joining the wealthy requires 20 + x > 50, or x > 30. The middle class therefore decides the outcome when 30 < x ≤ 80 (at x = 30, the vote ties at 50 to 50).
  2. For the middle class to outnumber the other two classes combined, x > 100 − x, or x > 50. The middle class then holds a majority on its own and can govern without allying with either extreme.

Evidence and counterarguments

Lipset (1959) and Przeworski and Limongi (1997): democracies with higher incomes do not collapse. In "Some Social Requisites of Democracy," Seymour Martin Lipset showed that the higher a country's levels of income, education, and urbanization, the more likely it is to be a stable democracy (American Political Science Review 53, no. 1). One reason he gave followed Aristotle's logic: economic development enlarges the middle class and tempers conflict between the extremes. Adam Przeworski and Fernando Limongi separated the two directions of the relationship. Rising incomes, they found, do not make dictatorships much more likely to become democracies, but once a democracy is in place, the higher its income, the far less likely it is to collapse (World Politics 49, no. 2). Their data contained no case of a democracy collapsing above a certain level of per capita income.

Easterly (2001): the middle-class share and institutions. Using cross-country data, William Easterly tested the "middle class consensus," the proposition that countries in which the middle class receives a larger share of income have higher incomes and growth and are more politically stable and democratic. He showed that the middle-class share is larger in countries that are ethnically less diverse and less dependent on natural resources (Journal of Economic Growth 6, no. 4). Here, too, it is not fully settled whether the middle class is the cause or whether other conditions produce both a large middle class and stability.

Counterargument: income alone does not explain polarization. In Polarized America (2006), Nolan McCarty, Keith T. Poole, and Howard Rosenthal, who study polarization in American politics, reported that widening income inequality and congressional polarization have moved together since the 1970s. Later studies, however, found that factors such as the media environment, redistricting, and the geographic sorting of party supporters also play a large role, and they cautioned against explaining political extremism by the shrinking of the middle class alone. Aristotle's argument is a powerful starting point, but today's data show how hard it is to pin the problem on a single cause.
