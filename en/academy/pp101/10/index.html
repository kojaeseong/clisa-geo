The question for this session

The utilitarians of session 9 judged actions by their consequences. Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) starts from the opposite end. However good the results, an act that uses a person merely as a means is wrong, and the standard of morality is not happiness but a law that reason gives to itself. Session 1 left open whether the Laws of Athens were entitled to argue from "what if everyone did this?" Kant offers a different way of putting that question.

Kant carried the same principle into relations among states. In Perpetual Peace (1795), he set out what states must refrain from even in war and what conditions a lasting peace requires. This session reads the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785) and then Perpetual Peace, and uses them to ask whether NATO and Russia, as of November 6, 2026, still preserve the minimum of trust that any future peace would need. Königsberg, where Kant was born and which he scarcely left in his lifetime, is today Russia's Kaliningrad, the city at the center of Moscow's nuclear warning in the fall of 2026.

1. The good will: motive, not outcome

The first section of the Groundwork opens with a famous sentence: "Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will" (Academy edition, vol. 4, p. 393). Talents such as intelligence and courage, and gifts of fortune such as power, wealth, and health, are good in many respects, but they become harmful when the will that directs them is not good. Coolness, Kant notes, only makes a villain more dangerous.

The worth of a good will does not lie in what it achieves. Even if misfortune left it unable to accomplish anything despite its greatest efforts, "like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself" (4:394). Success adds nothing to that worth, and failure takes nothing away.

What, then, makes a will good? Kant's answer is action done from duty. His example is a shopkeeper who does not overcharge even an inexperienced customer (4:397). The shopkeeper acts in accordance with duty, but if he is honest because honesty is good for business, his action has no moral worth. Acting in accordance with duty and acting from duty are different things.

2. The categorical imperative: the formula of universal law

Kant distinguishes two kinds of command. A hypothetical imperative, such as "exercise if you want to stay healthy," binds only someone who wants the end in question. A categorical imperative binds unconditionally, whatever one happens to want. Morality takes the categorical form, and Kant states its principle in a single sentence:

Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law. (4:421)

A maxim is the principle on which a person actually acts. Kant's best-known example is the false promise. Someone in need of money knows he cannot repay a loan but also knows that no one will lend to him unless he promises to repay. His maxim is: "When I think myself in want of money, I will borrow money and promise to repay it, although I know that I never can do so." Universalize it, so that anyone in difficulty makes promises with no intention of keeping them, and no one would believe a promise again; promising itself would become impossible (4:422). The maxim defeats itself the moment it is made universal.

Kant draws a further distinction here. Whether a false promise is prudent and whether it is right are different questions (4:402). Avoiding false promises because lost credit may cost more later is a judgment of prudence, grounded in fear of the consequences. For Kant, a false promise is wrong not because it is costly but because its maxim cannot be willed as a universal law. The Laws in session 1 argued that the city would collapse if everyone ignored its verdicts, an argument from consequences. Kant's test asks something else: not whether such a world would turn out badly, but whether it can be consistently conceived and willed at all.

3. The formula of humanity and autonomy

Kant restates the same principle in another formula:

So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only. (4:429)

The key word is "only." Paying a taxi driver to take you somewhere uses the driver as a means, but not merely as a means, since the driver can agree to the transaction. A false promise is different: the person deceived could never consent to the use being made of him, so the liar treats him merely as a means (4:429–430). Things, Kant writes, have a price and can be exchanged for an equivalent; persons have a dignity that admits of no equivalent (4:434). Clisa Geopolitics quotes this formula in its philosophical Q&A on whether a few may be sacrificed for the many.

Underlying both formulas is autonomy. "Autonomy of the will," Kant writes, "is that property of it by which it is a law to itself" (4:440). A will moved by desire, interest, or another's command is governed from outside, which Kant calls heteronomy. Rational beings are free only when they obey universal laws they give themselves. This is Rousseau's idea from session 7, that obedience to a law one prescribes to oneself is liberty, moved to the center of moral philosophy. Kant called the community of all rational beings legislating in this way a "kingdom of ends."

The strictness of the theory provoked a famous dispute. In a short essay of 1797, Kant held that one may not lie even to a murderer who asks where one's friend is hiding (8:425–430). Lecture 13 of Harvard's Justice takes up this case.

4. The preliminary articles: what even war does not permit

Perpetual Peace is written in the form of a peace treaty. Its first section lays out six preliminary articles, the conditions states must meet before a lasting peace; its second, three definitive articles on which a lasting peace would rest. The preliminary articles are as follows (8:343–347):

Article Content Kant's classification
1 No treaty of peace is valid if made with a secret reservation of material for a future war Strict; to be enforced at once
2 No independent state may be acquired by another through inheritance, exchange, purchase, or donation Enforcement may be postponed
3 Standing armies shall in time be abolished Enforcement may be postponed
4 No national debt shall be contracted in connection with external affairs Enforcement may be postponed
5 No state shall interfere by force in the constitution or government of another Strict; to be enforced at once
6 No state at war shall allow hostilities that would make mutual confidence impossible in a later peace Strict; to be enforced at once

The first article directly contradicts Machiavelli's view of promises in chapter 18 of The Prince (session 4): that a prince need not keep his word once keeping it works against him. A treaty signed by parties too exhausted to continue fighting, each waiting for the first good opportunity to resume, "would be a mere truce, a mere suspension of hostilities, not peace" (8:343). The fourth article is the subject of session 6 of Introduction to International Relations.

The article most relevant to 2026 is the sixth. The forbidden practices are "the employment of assassins (percussores) or of poisoners (venefici), breaches of capitulation, the instigating and making use of treachery (perduellio) in the hostile state." Kant's reason matters as much as his list. These methods are not only cruel: "some kind of confidence in the disposition of the enemy must exist even in the midst of war, as otherwise peace could not be concluded, and the hostilities would pass into a war of extermination" (8:346). Because no court stands above states, war is merely a "wretched expedient" for asserting a right by force, and a war of extermination would bring perpetual peace "only in the great graveyard of the human race." Kant extends the point to the use of spies. Vices that exploit the dishonesty of others, once encouraged, cannot be confined to war; they carry over into peace and destroy its purpose (8:347).

5. The definitive articles and the "race of devils"

First definitive article: "The civil constitution of each state shall be republican." By republican Kant does not mean democratic. He means a representative system that separates the executive from the legislature; democracy in the strict sense he counted as a form of despotism. A republic, he argues, inclines toward peace:

If, as must be so under this constitution, the consent of the subjects is required to determine whether there shall be war or not, nothing is more natural than that they should weigh the matter well, before undertaking such a bad business. For in decreeing war, they would of necessity be resolving to bring down the miseries of war upon their country. (8:351)

Citizens must fight, pay, and carry the debt. A ruler who owns the state loses nothing by war and can decide on it "for the most trifling reasons, as if it were a kind of pleasure party."

Second definitive article: "The law of nations shall be founded on a federation of free states." States, like individuals, live with one another in a lawless state of nature. Hobbes (session 5) described that condition, but Kant does not draw the conclusion that states, like individuals, must submit to a single world state. Fusing many nations into one would contradict the very idea of a law among nations, and states will not accept it. Kant proposes instead a "covenant of peace" (foedus pacificum), which, unlike a peace treaty that ends one war, "would seek to put an end to war for ever" (8:356). This federation without coercive power is "the negative substitute" for a world republic, and Kant expected it to spread outward from a single republic at its center.

Third definitive article: "The rights of men, as citizens of the world, shall be limited to the conditions of universal hospitality." A stranger arriving on foreign soil has a right not to be treated as an enemy, a right of visitation, but no right to be received as a guest. In the same passage Kant sharply condemns European colonial conquest.

A problem soluble even by devils. To those who say a republic would require a nation of angels, Kant replies: "The problem of the formation of the state, hard as it may sound, is not insoluble, even for a race of devils, granted that they have intelligence" (8:366). Even selfish beings, each secretly seeking an exemption from the law, can be given a constitution in which their interests check one another, so that the public effect is the same as if they had good intentions. A good constitution does not await moral progress; moral progress grows under a good constitution. Kant's political philosophy does not rely on human goodness; its logic is the same as Madison's "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition," discussed in session 3.

6. Classics and theory: from moral principle to the democratic peace

Kant's ethics became a starting point for modern theories of justice, and Perpetual Peace founded the liberal tradition in international relations.

Thinker Argument Relation to Kant
Hegel, Elements of the Philosophy of Right (1820), §§333–334 No judge stands above states, so a league of states rests on sovereign wills and remains contingent; when those wills cannot agree, only war can settle the matter Rejects the efficacy of the federation outright
John Rawls, "Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory" (1980); The Law of Peoples (1999) Principles of justice are constructed by free and equal persons under a fair procedure; internationally, a society of liberal and decent peoples Recasts autonomy and the categorical imperative as the original position (session 12) and extends the federation in The Law of Peoples
Michael Doyle, "Kant, Liberal Legacies, and Foreign Affairs" (1983) Constitutionally secure liberal states have not fought one another for almost two centuries, but they have not been peaceful toward nonliberal states Turns the definitive articles into a testable empirical claim (Introduction to International Relations, session 1)

7. Applying it to 2026: do enemies still keep the minimum of trust a future peace requires?

The facts. In 2026, a series of incidents on NATO territory was attributed to Russia. On August 4, a drone carrying explosives was found among Ukraine-bound cargo planes at Leipzig/Halle Airport in Germany, and on September 1 the German government formally named Russia as responsible. On September 23, a Russian Mi-8 helicopter from Kaliningrad entered Polish airspace to a depth of about 300 meters and stayed for 42 seconds, according to Poland's Operational Command. On September 30, Reuters reported that a Russian diplomatic note to NATO members claimed the alliance was preparing an air and sea blockade of Kaliningrad and warned that Russia was ready to use all weapons, including nuclear ones, if a blockade were carried out. Speaking at the Valdai Discussion Club on October 1, President Vladimir Putin confirmed the content of the note and described the warning not as a threat but as a response to attempts to intimidate Russia (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, October 2, 2026; Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Russia-NATO standoff).

Incidents along NATO's eastern frontier continued into the fall, though not all have been attributed to Russia. On October 18, Lithuanian President Gitanas Nausėda said an apparent drone had entered Lithuanian airspace and exploded; a spokesman for Ukraine's foreign ministry apologized, saying Russian electronic warfare might have diverted a Ukrainian drone (Briefing, October 19). On October 23, a cargo ship was struck by a drone and sank just outside Romanian territorial waters, leaving one sailor dead and three missing; Romanian President Nicușor Dan blamed Russia (Briefing, October 24). On October 26, Romania's intelligence service and its organized-crime and terrorism prosecutors announced that a September 14 fire near a subsidiary of the state arms company in Brașov had been set by a Russian-directed sabotage network; the three suspects were two Belarusians and a Ukrainian (Briefing, October 27). On November 1, Latvia's armed forces told residents near the Russian and Belarusian borders to stay indoors after an unidentified aerial object entered Latvian airspace (Briefing, November 2).

Meanwhile, the channels for keeping accidents from escalating have narrowed. NATO suspended practical cooperation with Russia in April 2014 after the annexation of Crimea, and on October 18, 2021, Russia announced it was suspending its mission to NATO and closing NATO's military liaison mission in Moscow (AP). The Pentagon said in early March 2022 that it had opened a deconfliction line with the Russian Ministry of Defense (news reports), but how that line is used in 2026 could not be confirmed from public sources. Clisa Geopolitics identifies hotlines and incident-prevention arrangements, pursued separately from NATO's forward deployments, as one way out of the standoff, while noting that Russia is likely to demand cuts in those deployments in return and has refused to modernize the Vienna Document (analysis of the Russia-NATO standoff).

Through Kant's lens. The sixth preliminary article forbids those means of fighting that leave the adversary unable to trust anything after the fighting ends. Sabotage carried out by hired proxies against an adversary's arms factories has the same structure as Kant's hired assassins and incitement to treachery (this lecture's interpretation). Repeated often enough, such methods mean that even if an agreement is eventually signed, neither side has grounds to believe what the other will do once the ink is dry. That is the danger Kant had in mind when he warned that the vices of espionage carry over into peace.

The standard applies to both sides. Russia regards Western inspections and seizures of tankers and NATO exercises around Kaliningrad as preparations for a blockade, and it has answered with nuclear warnings (analysis of the Russia-NATO standoff). Kant's article does not ask who started it; it asks which means destroy future trust. Open maritime enforcement and covert sabotage do not weigh the same by that measure, but any method that convinces an adversary that it faces a war of extermination violates the spirit of the sixth article, whoever uses it (this lecture's interpretation).

Hotlines and incident-prevention agreements can be seen as institutional forms of the "confidence in the disposition of the enemy" that the sixth article requires. They do not ask either side to trust the other's intentions. They ask only for the minimal expectation that when something goes wrong, the other side will pick up the phone and explain an accident as an accident. The logic matches the "race of devils" passage: such arrangements work even if both sides are moved by nothing nobler than self-interest in avoiding an unintended war (this lecture's interpretation). The 1972 U.S.-Soviet Incidents at Sea Agreement, concluded at the height of the Cold War, is the classic example.

Through the democratic peace. The first definitive article and Doyle's democratic peace concern peace among republics; Doyle himself stressed that liberal states have not been peaceful toward nonliberal ones. Kant would not have regarded a regime that can go to war without its citizens' consent as a republic, so the definitive articles promise little between NATO and Russia (this lecture's interpretation). The same test applies to NATO members: wherever executives use force without legislative approval, the subject of the war powers debate in session 3, they too drift from Kant's republic. What remains are the preliminary articles, the minimum rules that bind every state whatever its constitution.

The limits of the analogy. First, the sixth article addresses states at war. If certain means are forbidden even in war, they are all the more forbidden in peacetime, but Kant never dealt with a gray zone that is neither war nor peace. Second, the article assumes it is known who used the forbidden means. When attribution is uncertain, as with the Lithuanian drone, the facts are contested before any principle can be applied. Third, hotlines carry a cost. Drawing on Thomas Schelling, Clisa Geopolitics argues that because uncontrolled escalation risk is itself a source of deterrence, incident-prevention arrangements may weaken deterrence even as they curb escalation (analysis of the Russia-NATO standoff). Fourth, the sixth article has no enforcer, which is precisely Hegel's objection.

  2. How do hypothetical and categorical imperatives differ? What happens when the maxim of a false promise is tested against the formula of universal law?
  3. How does treating a person as a means differ from treating a person merely as a means? Give an example.

Discussion questions

  1. Can you accept Kant's conclusion that one may not lie even to a murderer looking for a friend? If not, which part of the categorical imperative would you revise?
  2. Should states follow the same moral rules as individuals? How might intelligence gathering and sabotage be judged differently by Kant's standard?
  3. Is opening a hotline with an adversary a prudent way of managing crises, or a signal that gray-zone attacks will be tolerated? Ask the same question about the military communication lines between North and South Korea.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages of the Groundwork and Perpetual Peace in classic English translations, works through false promises and accidental clashes with numbers, and looks at research that has tested the democratic peace and the arrangements adversaries build to manage risk.

Reading the original

Quotations from the Groundwork in this session are taken from T. K. Abbott's translation (Project Gutenberg eBook 5682), published under the title Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals; quotations from Perpetual Peace are from M. Campbell Smith's translation (first edition, 1903; third impression, 1917; Project Gutenberg eBook 50922). Omissions are marked by ellipses. The numbers refer to volume and page of the Academy edition, the standard way of citing Kant.

Passage 1. Prudence and right (Groundwork, section 1, 4:402–403)

Let the question be, for example: May I when in distress make a promise with the intention not to keep it? I readily distinguish here between the two significations which the question may have: Whether it is prudent, or whether it is right, to make a false promise? ... Now it is a wholly different thing to be truthful from duty and to be so from apprehension of injurious consequences. ... Then I presently become aware that while I can will the lie, I can by no means will that lying should be a universal law. For with such a law there would be no promises at all ...

How to read it. Refraining from false promises for fear of losing one's credit is prudence, not morality, and the merely prudent break their word whenever they are sure not to be caught. Since promises between states rarely rise above prudence, the distinction invites a closer look at what "trust" means in international politics.

Passage 2. Hostilities that destroy confidence (Perpetual Peace, first section, preliminary article 6, 8:346–347)

No state at war with another shall countenance such modes of hostility as would make mutual confidence impossible in a subsequent state of peace: such are the employment of assassins (percussores) or of poisoners (venefici), breaches of capitulation, the instigating and making use of treachery (perduellio) in the hostile state. ... These are dishonourable stratagems. For some kind of confidence in the disposition of the enemy must exist even in the midst of war, as otherwise peace could not be concluded, and the hostilities would pass into a war of extermination (bellum internecinum).

How to read it. Kant does not forbid war itself. He forbids the means that make it impossible to end a war. In counting even the use of spies among them, he set a standard far stricter than the practice of modern states.

Passage 3. A race of devils (Perpetual Peace, first supplement, 8:366)

The problem of the formation of the state, hard as it may sound, is not insoluble, even for a race of devils, granted that they have intelligence. It may be put thus:—"Given a multitude of rational beings who, in a body, require general laws for their own preservation, but each of whom, as an individual, is secretly inclined to exempt himself from this restraint: how are we to order their affairs and how establish for them a constitution such that, although their private dispositions may be really antagonistic, they may yet so act as a check upon one another, that, in their public relations, the effect is the same as if they had no such evil sentiments."

How to read it. Kant's moral philosophy demands pure motives; his political philosophy assumes impure ones. The two do not conflict. Morality is what each person is asked to do, and institutions are designed to work even when that demand goes unmet.

Working it out

Problem 1. False promises and credit. In a certain town, a share s of borrowers promise to repay with no intention of doing so, while the rest repay the principal plus 25 percent interest. Lenders lend only if the expected repayment is at least equal to the principal. (1) What is the largest value of s at which lending continues? (2) What happens if the maxim of the false promise becomes universal, so that s = 1? (3) Is this calculation the same as Kant's argument?

Problem 2. Accidental clashes and hotlines. Suppose that in a given year there are 20 dangerous close encounters between NATO and Russian forces, and that each one, independently, has a 2 percent chance of escalating into an armed clash. (1) What is the probability that at least one encounter escalates that year? (2) If a hotline cut the escalation probability per encounter to 0.5 percent, what would that probability become? (3) What if, reassured by the hotline, one side doubled the number of encounters to 40?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. With the principal set at 1, expected repayment is (1 − s) × 1.25. For this to be at least 1, 1 − s ≥ 0.8, or s ≤ 0.2. Lending continues as long as no more than one borrower in five makes a false promise.
  2. At s = 1, expected repayment is zero and no one lends. With no one believing promises, the practice of promising disappears.
  3. No. The calculation shows that false promises, once widespread, end lending; that is a consequence, and judging by it is what Kant calls prudence. Kant's argument points to a contradiction in conception: in a world where s = 1, the act of making a false promise cannot even occur, because deception depends on others believing promises. The liar wants an exception for himself and cannot will his maxim as universal. And by Kant's test, a false promise is wrong even when s ≤ 0.2 and lending carries on.

Problem 2.

  1. The probability that no encounter escalates is 0.98^20 ≈ 0.668, so the probability that at least one does is about 0.332, or 33 percent.
  2. With 0.995^20 ≈ 0.905, the probability falls to about 0.095, just under 10 percent.
  3. With 0.995^40 ≈ 0.818, it rises again to about 0.182, or 18 percent. Part of the hotline's benefit is offset by the additional encounters, a numerical version of the cost of hotlines discussed in section 7. All the figures in this problem are assumptions for illustration.

Evidence and counterarguments

Doyle (1983) and Maoz and Russett (1993): democracies do not fight one another. Building on Kant's definitive articles, Michael W. Doyle identified a "liberal peace": constitutionally secure liberal states had almost never gone to war with one another since the late eighteenth century (Philosophy & Public Affairs 12, nos. 3 and 4). Using data from 1946 to 1986, Zeev Maoz and Bruce Russett confirmed that pairs of democracies were less likely than other pairs to become involved in militarized disputes, and they examined democratic norms and institutions as joint explanations (American Political Science Review 87, no. 3). These studies do not find that democracies fight nondemocracies any less often than other states do.

Counterargument: Rosato (2003). Sebastian Rosato examined each causal mechanism proposed by democratic peace theory and argued that democracies whose interests clash do not trust one another and do issue threats, and that democracies have intervened covertly against one another (American Political Science Review 97, no. 4). Even where peace among democracies is observed, he contended, it is unclear whether democracy is the cause or whether some other condition, such as American hegemony during the Cold War, is responsible.

Do arrangements between adversaries work? Lynn-Jones (1985) and Fortna (2004). Sean M. Lynn-Jones reported that dangerous incidents between U.S. and Soviet naval forces declined after the 1972 Incidents at Sea Agreement, which he called "a quiet success for arms control" (International Security 9, no. 4). Analyzing interstate cease-fires since 1946, Virginia Page Fortna showed that stronger agreements, with mechanisms such as demilitarized zones, third-party monitoring, and joint commissions, produce more durable peace (Peace Time, 2004). Both findings support the Kantian hope that trust between enemies can be built by institutions rather than goodwill. Fortna herself, however, had to address a selection problem: parties that want peace more may also be more willing to sign strong agreements.
