The question for this session

Most rich countries today are democracies, and many poor countries are dictatorships. The conclusion that leaps out is that countries become democratic as they grow rich. In the 1950s and 1960s this idea took hold in American social science under the name "modernization theory." South Korea and Taiwan, which democratized in the late 1980s after decades of rapid growth, are often held up as textbook cases.

The counterexamples are just as striking. China's income per person is now far higher than Taiwan's was in 1987, yet China holds no competitive national elections. Saudi Arabia and Qatar have incomes close to or above South Korea's and remain monarchies. Is the link between income and democracy one of cause and effect, or did something else produce both?

The Evidence and counterarguments section of Introduction to Political Philosophy, session 3, briefly introduced Lipset (1959) and Przeworski and Limongi (1997). This session goes into the debate itself: half a century of research on whether income creates democracy or merely preserves democracies that already exist. It then applies that debate to Taiwan and China.

1. Lipset's question: why are rich countries democratic?

Seymour Martin Lipset posed the question systematically in "Some Social Requisites of Democracy: Economic Development and Political Legitimacy," published in the American Political Science Review in 1959. He divided European and English-speaking countries, and separately Latin American countries, into more democratic and less democratic groups, then compared them on a range of indicators of wealth, industrialization, education, and urbanization. On every indicator, the democratic group scored higher on average.

Lipset offered three explanations for the correlation. First, education broadens people's outlook and makes tolerance and compromise possible. Second, economic development reshapes society from a pyramid, with a few rich people perched atop a poor majority, into a diamond with a broad middle class. The middle class supports moderate parties and dampens conflict between the extremes, a modern sociological version of Aristotle's "counterweight" argument (Introduction to Political Philosophy, session 3, section 5). Third, in a rich society the side that loses an election does not lose everything, so defeat is easier to accept.

In the second half of the article, Lipset argued that stable democracy needs more than economic development. It also needs "effectiveness," a government's actual ability to solve problems, and "legitimacy," the belief that the system is the most appropriate one for the society. Lipset himself, in other words, did not treat income as the only cause. And what he showed was a correlation across countries at one point in time, not that any particular country became democratic as it grew richer. All the research that followed starts from that gap.

2. Exogenous and endogenous democratization: are democracies born, or do they survive?

In "Modernization: Theories and Facts," published in World Politics in 1997, and in Democracy and Development (2000), written with Michael E. Alvarez and José Antonio Cheibub, Adam Przeworski and Fernando Limongi pointed out that Lipset's correlation could arise from two quite different processes.

The first is endogenous democratization: as a dictatorship grows richer, it becomes more likely to turn into a democracy. This is the process modernization theory originally had in mind. The second is exogenous democratization: dictatorships fall for reasons unrelated to income, such as a leader's death, defeat in war, or economic crisis, but once a democracy is in place, it survives longer in a rich country. Under this process, too, rich countries end up with more democracies over time, because democracies in poor countries keep collapsing while those in rich countries endure.

A village full of sturdy houses may be a village where people built sturdy houses, or one where the flimsy houses fell down and only the sturdy ones are left. To tell the two apart, you have to look separately at how often houses are built and how often they collapse.

Using country-year data for 1950–1990, Przeworski and his colleagues estimated those two rates separately and came down on the exogenous side. The probability that a dictatorship would fall rose with income only up to about $6,000 per person; richer dictatorships were, if anything, more stable, and the authors concluded that democracy is not a by-product of economic development. The probability that a democracy would collapse, by contrast, fell steadily as income rose. In their data, no democracy with an income per person above $6,055 (in 1985 purchasing-power dollars, Penn World Table) ever fell. That threshold is Argentina's income in 1975; Argentina's democracy fell to a military coup the following year. Economic performance mattered along with the level of income: democracies whose economies were shrinking broke down far more easily than those that were growing.

If wealth does not make countries democratic but only keeps rich democracies from collapsing, then China and the Gulf monarchies are not exceptions at all.

3. The rebuttal: Boix, Stokes, and a longer history

Carles Boix and Susan C. Stokes challenged this conclusion in "Endogenous Democratization," published in World Politics in 2003. The data used by Przeworski and his colleagues begin in 1950, but many countries in Europe and the Americas democratized before then. Boix and Stokes reported that once the data are extended back to the mid-nineteenth century and problems of sample selection and model specification are addressed, economic development does raise the probability that a dictatorship will democratize, and that from the mid-nineteenth century to World War II this effect was even larger than its effect on democratic stability. For recent decades, too, their model predicted that some countries that had developed but remained dictatorships would democratize within as few as three years of reaching an income of $12,000 per person.

The mechanism they emphasized is inequality. As income spreads more evenly, the rich stop fearing that a poor majority will use democracy to strip them of their property. Session 4 returns to this logic.

In "Democracy, Development, and the International System," published in the American Political Science Review in 2011, Boix used data running from the early nineteenth century to the end of the twentieth. He reported that income helps countries democratize and consolidate democracy even after controlling for country and period effects and instrumenting for income. The effect fades as income rises, and the international system matters as well. The share of democracies was high when democratic hegemons led the international order, as after the Cold War, and low when authoritarian great powers dominated it, as in the era of the Holy Alliance.

4. Income, or something else? The fixed-effects debate and value change

Acemoglu and colleagues (2008): within countries, the relationship disappears. In "Income and Democracy," published in the American Economic Review in 2008, Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, James A. Robinson, and Pierre Yared asked whether the fact that the United States is richer and more democratic than Colombia means that Colombia will become more democratic as it grows richer.

Comparisons across countries cannot filter out factors such as history, institutions, and geography that may have shaped both income and democracy. So the authors added "country fixed effects," which strip out each country's unchanging characteristics and ask only whether income and democracy scores move together within a single country. Whether democracy was measured by Freedom House or Polity scores, adding fixed effects made the statistical relationship disappear. Countries whose incomes grew more between 1970 and 1995 did not see their democracy scores rise more.

Where, then, does the cross-country correlation come from? In a sample of former European colonies, once the authors controlled for historical factors that shaped both economic and political development, the correlation vanished even without fixed effects. Wealth and democracy may both grow from the same historical root (session 11).

Fixed effects, however, can also wipe out effects of income that unfold slowly over decades. Boix's 2011 finding of an income effect with fixed effects, using two centuries of data, is one version of this objection, and there is still no consensus on what drives the difference between the two conclusions (see Evidence and counterarguments in Going deeper).

Inglehart and Welzel: what changes is values. Using World Values Survey data, Ronald Inglehart and Christian Welzel proposed a different pathway. When economic development makes survival secure, people shift toward "self-expression values" that prize autonomy and choice, and in such societies elites find it hard to resist citizens' demands for democracy for long (Modernization, Cultural Change, and Democracy, 2005). Sirianne Dahlum and Carl Henrik Knutsen, however, reported in the British Journal of Political Science in 2017 that once sample selection bias, fixed effects, and reverse causation are taken into account, there is no evidence for this pathway, and there are some signs that experience with democracy is what fosters those values.

5. The exceptions: oil states and China

Does oil block democracy? In "Democracy's Third Wave" (1991), Samuel P. Huntington noted that in 1990 Singapore was the only "high-income" country (by the World Bank's definition) that was neither an oil exporter nor a democracy.

The standard explanation is the theory of the "rentier state," set out in The Rentier State (1987), edited by Hazem Beblawi and Giacomo Luciani. When public revenue comes from oil sales rather than taxes, the state has no need to tax its citizens, and citizens have less ground to demand representation. This is "no taxation without representation" turned on its head.

In "Does Oil Hinder Democracy?," published in World Politics in 2001, Michael L. Ross tested the claim with data on 113 countries from 1971 to 1997. Oil exports were strongly associated with authoritarian rule, and the effect was not confined to the Middle East. He found at least partial support for three mechanisms: a rentier effect (low taxes and patronage dampen pressure for democracy), a repression effect (resource revenue pays for stronger security forces), and a modernization effect (growth based on resources fails to bring the social and cultural changes that produce democracy). The third mechanism actually confirms modernization theory, since it implies that income without modernization does not bring democracy.

On the other side, Stephen Haber and Victor Menaldo reported in the American Political Science Review in 2011 that when long historical data are used to track changes within countries, greater reliance on resources is not associated with authoritarianism, and many of their results point instead to a "resource blessing." The fixed-effects debate repeats itself here.

The Gulf in 2026 offers an unusual test of rentier-state theory. Amid the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and Iranian attacks, the IMF projects that Saudi Arabia's economy will shrink by 3.3 percent in 2026 and Qatar's by 15.6 percent (IMF World Economic Outlook, October 2026, via IMF DataMapper, accessed November 13, 2026). If a rentier state's bargain rests on its ability to hand out oil revenue, shrinking revenue puts that bargain under strain, though a crisis can also rally people around security (this lecture's interpretation).

China: authoritarian resilience. China is a harder exception. It grew through manufacturing and urbanization rather than oil, and an educated urban middle class has indeed expanded, yet one-party rule persists. In "China's Changing of the Guard: Authoritarian Resilience," published in the Journal of Democracy in 2003, Andrew J. Nathan traced the reason to institutionalization: succession politics had become more bound by norms, merit counted for more than faction in elite promotion, institutions within the regime had become functionally specialized, and channels for participation and appeal had strengthened the party's legitimacy. In the same issue, Bruce Gilley replied in "The Limits of Authoritarian Resilience" that the evidence of such institutionalization was faint and that, as in earlier cycles, the logic of concentrated power would reassert itself.

The regime's survival for more than twenty years since then vindicates Nathan, but the 2018 constitutional amendment that abolished the term limit on the presidency, which broke the norm-bound succession he had stressed, bears out Gilley's warning (this lecture's interpretation). Since 2023, more than half of China's full generals have been purged, and on September 21, 2026, Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli were expelled from the party and the military (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Taiwan Strait issue). Session 6 covers how authoritarian regimes survive.

Moore: the path, not the level. In Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy (1966), Barrington Moore Jr. argued that whether a country ended up democratic, fascist, or communist was decided not by its level of income but by the class coalitions among landlords, peasants, and the urban bourgeoisie. His best-known thesis, that without a bourgeoisie there is no democracy, is taken up in session 4.

7. Applying it to 2026: what do Taiwan and China, which share roots but ended up with different regimes, tell us about modernization theory?

The facts. The IMF estimates 2026 GDP per person (nominal U.S. dollars) at about $44,000 for Taiwan and about $15,400 for China. In 1987, the year Taiwan lifted martial law, its figure was about $5,300 (IMF DataMapper, accessed November 13, 2026). In nominal terms, China's income today is roughly three times that. Dollars from the two years are not directly comparable because of inflation, and the $6,055 threshold of Przeworski and his colleagues and the $12,000 figure of Boix and Stokes are measured on different bases from the IMF's nominal figures. Even so, China can reasonably be said to have entered Huntington's "political transition zone" (Going deeper, Reading the original, passage 2) (this lecture's interpretation).

Taiwan lifted martial law in 1987, held its first direct presidential election in 1996, and has seen power change hands peacefully three times, in the elections of 2000, 2008, and 2016. On November 28, 2026, it holds local elections for 22 mayoral and county magistrate posts; the opposition Kuomintang (KMT) currently holds 13 or 14 of them (the basis for Clisa Geopolitics forecast f72).

China keeps up the pressure. The People's Liberation Army held the Justice Mission-2025 exercise on December 29–31, 2025 (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Taiwan Strait issue), and on November 10, 2026, Taiwan's defense minister, Wellington Koo, confirmed in the Legislative Yuan that China had conducted "area denial" drills in the Western Pacific with the Liaoning carrier group and other forces. China did not formally announce the drills (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, November 12, 2026).

Through the lens of modernization theory. Taiwan followed the path modernization theory describes almost step by step. As early as 1991, Huntington observed that rapid growth had produced a substantial entrepreneurial class, composed largely of native Taiwanese, that became a source of power and wealth independent of the mainlander-dominated state. Yet Huntington also credited leadership: Chiang Ching-kuo and Lee Teng-hui responded to the pressures of social change by gradually opening up politics. From Boix's 2011 perspective, Taiwan democratized within an order led by the United States, and its democracy's survival still depends heavily on that order (this lecture's interpretation).

China is the strongest apparent counterexample. Under the exogenous view, China is no exception at all; under Nathan's resilience argument, institutionalization helped the regime withstand the pressure. Under the endogenous view, China defies the prediction and needs explaining. Candidate explanations include the state's grip on the commanding heights of the economy, which kept entrepreneurs from becoming a force independent of the state, and the fact that China is itself a great power and so faces little outside pressure to democratize (this lecture's interpretation).

The premise that Taiwan and China are "the same Chinese society" also needs care. In a survey by the Election Study Center of National Chengchi University for January–June 2026, 2.5 percent of respondents in Taiwan identified as only Chinese, while 63.1 percent identified as only Taiwanese (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Taiwan Strait issue). If the experience of democracy reshaped identity, causation ran from politics to culture.

What can be tested. In Democracy and the Market (1991), Przeworski defined democracy this way: "Democracy is a system in which parties lose elections." By that definition, the test of Taiwan's democracy is not whether the ruling Democratic Progressive Party wins, but whether it can lose and, if it does, hand over power. By the survival analysis of Przeworski and his colleagues, a democracy with Taiwan's income is very unlikely to collapse from within. The threat comes from outside, from China's pressure, and modernization theory, centered on income, has nothing to say about it (this lecture's interpretation).

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages in the original, works through numerical examples of democratic survival and the logic of fixed effects, and sums up the debate over causation.

Reading the original

Passage 1. Lipset's modernization thesis ("Some Social Requisites of Democracy," 1959, p. 75)

...only in a wealthy society in which relatively few citizens lived in real poverty could a situation exist in which the mass of the population could intelligently participate in politics and could develop the self-restraint necessary to avoid succumbing to the appeals of irresponsible demagogues.

How to read it. Lipset introduces this sentence as a view that many have held since Aristotle. Wealth here is not an end in itself but a condition that produces the civic qualities of capable participation and self-restraint. What later research asks is whether that condition is a cause or a companion grown from the same root.

Passage 2. The political transition zone (Huntington, "Democracy's Third Wave," 1991, pp. 30–31)

Few relationships between social, economic, and political phenomena are stronger than that between the level of economic development and the existence of democratic politics. ... The correlation between wealth and democracy implies that transitions to democracy should occur primarily in countries at the mid-level of economic development. In poor countries democratization is unlikely; in rich countries it usually has already occurred. In between there is a "political transition zone" ...

How to read it. Huntington found that third-wave transitions were concentrated in this zone, especially at its upper end. In the same essay he suggested that democratization could become a dominant feature of Middle Eastern and North African politics in the 1990s. That did not happen. He noted that heavy dependence on oil exports enhances the control of the state bureaucracy, though he added that this did not make democratization impossible. It was that caveat that proved the better guide (this lecture's interpretation).

Passage 3. China, Taiwan, and leadership (Huntington, same essay, pp. 25 and 33)

In China, economics reinforced culture in holding back democracy. In Singapore, Taiwan, and Korea, on the other hand, spectacular growth created the economic basis for democracy by the late 1980s. (p. 25)

Economic development makes democracy possible; political leadership makes it real. (p. 33)

How to read it. In 1991 China was poor and lacked the economic conditions for democracy. By 2026 it has met many of those conditions without democratizing. That shifts more weight onto the variable that turns possibility into reality: leadership (this lecture's interpretation).

Working it out

The tables and numbers below were invented for practice; they are not real data.

Problem 1. How long democracies survive (illustrative data). "Democracy-years" is the number of democracies observed multiplied by the number of years each was observed (10 democracies observed for 10 years give 100).

Income group (illustrative) Democracy-years Breakdowns observed
Low 400 32
Middle 600 18
High 500 0

(1) For each income group, find the probability that a democracy breaks down within a year. (2) If that probability stays the same each year, how many years can a democracy in each group expect to survive (expected lifetime = 1 ÷ annual breakdown probability)? (3) What is the probability that a democracy in the low and middle groups survives 10 years without breaking down? (4) Does zero breakdowns in the high group mean the breakdown probability is zero?

Problem 2. The logic of fixed effects (illustrative data). Here are income per person (in thousands of dollars) and democracy scores (0 to 10) for two countries, A and B.

Country (illustrative) Income, time 1 Score, time 1 Income, time 2 Score, time 2
A 10 8 20 8
B 2 2 4 2

(1) Comparing the two countries at time 1, how many points higher is the democracy score per $1,000 of income? (2) Within each country, how much did the score change as income rose? (3) How does the difference between these two calculations relate to the argument of Acemoglu and his colleagues? (4) What might the fixed-effects approach miss in this example?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. Low: 32 ÷ 400 = 0.08 (8 percent). Middle: 18 ÷ 600 = 0.03 (3 percent). High: 0 ÷ 500 = 0 (as observed).
  2. Low: 1 ÷ 0.08 = 12.5 years. Middle: 1 ÷ 0.03 ≈ 33.3 years. With no breakdowns observed in the high group, this method cannot pin down an expected lifetime. Przeworski and his colleagues faced the same situation when they found that no democracy above $6,055 ever fell.
  3. Low: 0.92¹⁰ ≈ 0.434. Middle: 0.97¹⁰ ≈ 0.737. A 5-percentage-point gap in the annual probability becomes a gap of about 30 percentage points over 10 years.
  4. No. It means only that no breakdown occurred during the period observed. By the statistical "rule of three," when an event has not occurred in n observations, the upper bound of a 95 percent confidence interval for its probability is roughly 3 ÷ n. Here that is 3 ÷ 500 = 0.006, so an annual probability of up to 0.6 percent cannot be ruled out. Whether rich democracies can break down too is the question of session 5.

Problem 2.

  1. The scores differ by 8 − 2 = 6 points and incomes by 10 − 2 = 8 thousand dollars, so the score is 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75 points higher per $1,000. Across countries, income and democracy move closely together.
  2. Both countries doubled their incomes, but neither score changed. Within countries, the effect of income is zero.
  3. The cross-country correlation (0.75) arises because A and B were different from the start, not because rising income made them democratic. Acemoglu and his colleagues argued that this is what happens in the real data, and they identified historical factors as what made countries like A and B different to begin with.
  4. If income affects democracy only after decades, a short interval between two observations will not show it. And if B's long history before time 1 kept its score at 2 because of low income, fixed effects wipe out that effect too, treating it as an "unchanging national characteristic." This is why Boix (2011) used two centuries of data.

Evidence and counterarguments

Przeworski et al. versus Boix and Stokes: what separates the samples? Much of the difference lies in the period observed. The years 1950–1990 were the Cold War, when American and Soviet intervention arguably weighed heavily on both democratization and breakdown (this lecture's interpretation). On the other hand, nineteenth-century data carry large measurement errors, so a longer sample is not necessarily a more accurate one.

Acemoglu et al. versus Boix (2011): does the method decide the result? Boix (2011) reported that over a longer period the income effect survives fixed effects and is strongest at low incomes. Models that assume a straight-line relationship can dilute a nonlinear effect of this kind. From the standpoint of Acemoglu and his colleagues, however, an effect found in a long sample may instead reflect omitted variables, such as an international environment that changes from era to era. The debate is a leading example of how much the choice of method can drive conclusions in comparative politics.

What the debate leaves us with. The observation that rich democracies rarely collapse has been repeated across many studies, though from the perspective of Acemoglu and his colleagues even that may reflect historical factors rather than income. The sharpest dispute concerns income's power to turn dictatorships into democracies. A cautious conclusion is that this power, if it exists, is weak, or varies greatly with the era, the international environment, and the source of the income (this lecture's interpretation).
