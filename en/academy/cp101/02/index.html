The question for this session

To compare countries, we first have to decide what unit we are comparing (session 1). The most basic unit in comparative politics is the state. But what exactly is a state, and how does it differ from a government? Why are some states good at collecting taxes and enforcing laws, while others cannot extend their authority much beyond the capital?

This session takes up three questions in turn: how to define the state (Max Weber), where the modern state came from (Charles Tilly's claim that war made the state), and whether that account holds outside Europe (counterarguments from Africa and Latin America, and the concept of state capacity). It then applies these ideas to the way the wars of 2026 are reshaping public finances in Poland, Germany, Russia, and Iran.

1. What is a state? Weber's definition

The German sociologist Max Weber gave his lecture "Politics as a Vocation" to a student association in Munich on January 28, 1919. In it he defined the state this way:

Today, however, we have to say that a state is a human community that (successfully) claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory.

Weber defined the state by its means, not its ends. Churches, merchant guilds, and feudal lords have all run schools, cared for the poor, and built roads, so there is no task that only states have ever performed. What makes a state a state is not what it does but the means it uses: physical force. Weber quotes Trotsky ("Every state is founded on force") and adds, "That is indeed right." He is careful to say, though, that force is neither the normal nor the only means a state uses.

Read word by word, the definition yields four criteria for comparison.

Element Meaning Question to ask when comparing
Monopoly The state is the sole source of the "right" to use force; others may use it only as far as the state permits Do warlords, militias, or organized crime use force on their own authority?
Legitimate The people ruled regard the state's force as rightful Do citizens accept the decisions of the police and the courts?
(Successfully) claims The monopoly is a claim, and a state exists when the claim actually holds Does the government's claim hold across the whole territory?
Territory The state is rule over a bounded piece of land Are the borders settled, and does the state really control what lies inside them?

The definition also separates the state from the government. A government is the set of people who run the state's institutions at a given moment, and it changes through elections or coups; the army, the courts, and the tax authorities remain. A regime is the set of rules that determines who governs the state and how; it may be democratic or authoritarian. "The state is weak," "the government is unpopular," and "democracy is in trouble" are three different claims, and mixing them up muddies any comparison.

2. Three types of legitimacy

Force alone cannot sustain a state. Weber calls the state "a relation of men dominating men" and asks when and why people obey. His answer is that, in principle, there are three inner justifications for domination.

Type Basis of obedience Weber's examples
Traditional The authority of the "eternal yesterday," that is, custom recognized since time immemorial The patriarch, the patrimonial prince of old
Charismatic Devotion to and trust in a leader's extraordinary personal qualities, the "gift of grace" The prophet, the elected war lord, the plebiscitarian ruler, the great demagogue, the party leader
Legal Belief in the validity of legal statute and in official "competence" based on rationally created rules The modern "servant of the state," the bureaucrat

Weber stresses that these are pure types, which in reality appear mixed, and that actual obedience also rests on fear, hope, and interests of every kind. Even so, the type of legitimacy goes a long way toward explaining how domination is structured. Charismatic rule depends on one person, so it is most fragile at the moment of succession. In 2026, after Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed during the war, his son Mojtaba succeeded him (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Iran issue). That can be read as a regime born of revolutionary charisma reaching for the traditional claim of hereditary succession (this lecture's interpretation). On the other hand, Mojtaba was chosen by the Assembly of Experts, a constitutional body (Fortune, March 9, 2026), so the succession can also be read as following the procedures of legal rule.

3. Tilly: war made the state

Where Weber defined what a state is, the American historical sociologist Charles Tilly asked how such states came to be. Medieval Europe was a patchwork of city-states, bishoprics, principalities, and empires. Why did large, centralized national states win out? Tilly's most famous answer is a single sentence from "Reflections on the History of European State-Making," his chapter in The Formation of National States in Western Europe (1975), a volume he edited:

War made the state, and the state made war.

According to Tilly's later book, Coercion, Capital, and European States (1990), Europeans began to make serious use of gunpowder in war around the middle of the fourteenth century, and over the following 150 years the invention and spread of firearms gave the military advantage to monarchs who could afford to cast cannon and build fortresses that cannon could not easily breach. To fight, a ruler had to extract money and men from his subjects. To extract them, he needed a bureaucracy that could assess taxes, keep accounts, and enforce collection. That apparatus outlasted the wars and became the skeleton of the state, while the units that lost were absorbed by those that won.

The state as a protection racket (1985). A decade later, in "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime," Tilly offered a more provocative analogy:

If protection rackets represent organized crime at its smoothest, then war making and state making, quintessential protection rackets with the advantage of legitimacy, qualify as our largest examples of organized crime.

"Someone who produces both the danger and, at a price, the shield against it is a racketeer," Tilly writes. In his account, European rulers did four things: they eliminated or neutralized rivals outside their territory (war making), eliminated or neutralized rivals inside it (state making), eliminated or neutralized the enemies of their clients (protection), and acquired the means to do all three (extraction). This picture, Tilly argues, fits European history far better than the idea of a social contract does. If Hobbes's Leviathan was born of a covenant (Introduction to Political Philosophy, session 5), Tilly's was born of war and tax collection.

Extraction breeds bargaining (1990). In Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1990 (1990), Tilly emphasizes that rulers had to bargain for taxes and soldiers with estates, cities, merchants, and eventually ordinary subjects. The rulers of Brandenburg, for example, had to come to terms with noble-dominated Estates, which won the power to grant or deny revenue for war. Such bargains, Tilly argues, led to representation and rights. He distinguishes three paths according to whether rulers commanded mainly coercion (land and armed force) or capital (cities and commerce).

Path How rulers operated Tilly's examples
Coercion-intensive Squeezed the means of war from their own and conquered populations, building massive structures of extraction Brandenburg, Russia
Capital-intensive Relied on compacts with capitalists to rent or buy military force, waging war without vast permanent state structures Genoa, Dubrovnik, the Dutch Republic
Capitalized coercion Did some of each, but worked harder to incorporate capitalists and sources of capital directly into the state France, England

From the seventeenth century on, Tilly argues, capitalized coercion proved the most effective in war, and states that had started down other paths eventually followed it. His summary: "War wove the European network of national states, and preparation for war created the internal structures of the states within it."

4. Where war did not build states: Africa and Latin America

Tilly himself was wary of exporting this account beyond Europe. In the 1985 essay he observes that European rulers, through long struggles and bargains with their subjects, came to accept constraints from courts and assemblies, whereas states created by decolonization acquired their military organization from outside, without the same internal forging of mutual constraints between rulers and ruled. When outside powers supply the weapons and guarantee the borders as well, the military becomes the most powerful and least constrained organization in the country. Two lines of research built on this concern.

Africa: no struggle for survival. In his 1990 article "War and the State in Africa," Jeffrey Herbst argued that African states had seldom fought interstate wars since independence and therefore faced little pressure to reform their tax systems or forge national identities. In States and Power in Africa (2000) he traced the problem further back. Europe was densely populated and short of land, but most of Africa was historically sparsely populated, so projecting power into the distant countryside was costly, and farmers could simply move away rather than fight. After independence, the international system guaranteed the colonial borders, allowing states to survive without actually controlling their territory.

Latin America: war left blood and debt. The title of Miguel Angel Centeno's Blood and Debt (2002) plays on Bismarck's "blood and iron." Latin America's wars, he argues, were mostly short and limited rather than total wars that mobilized whole societies, and they were paid for with foreign loans and customs duties instead of broader domestic taxation. When the fighting ended, what remained was debt, not a stronger tax administration.

These counterarguments do not so much refute Tilly as specify the conditions under which he is right. War builds states when it is large and long enough to threaten survival, and when rulers have no choice but to extract its costs from their own people. If they can borrow abroad, draw on foreign aid, or live off resource exports, war may leave behind debt and an overbearing military instead of a state. Session 11 returns to this point.

5. State capacity: despotic and infrastructural power

Calling a state "strong" can mean two different things. In a 1984 article, the British sociologist Michael Mann separated them. Despotic power is "the range of actions which the elite is empowered to undertake without routine, institutionalised negotiation with civil society groups." Infrastructural power is "the capacity of the state to actually penetrate civil society, and to implement logistically political decisions throughout the realm." Treating the two as independent dimensions, Mann proposed four ideal types.

Low infrastructural power High infrastructural power
Low despotic power Feudal Bureaucratic (today's capitalist democracies)
High despotic power Imperial Authoritarian (Mann's examples: Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union)

A modern democracy has little despotic power, since its president can do few things at will, but it has far more infrastructural power than any ancient empire: it knows everyone's income and taxes it, knows everyone's address, and delivers their mail.

In State-Building (2004), Francis Fukuyama drew a related distinction between the scope of what a state takes on and the strength with which it carries those tasks out, and he judged the combination of wide scope and weak capacity the worst for economic performance. What are often called weak or failed states are, in Weber's terms, states that fail the "(successfully)" test: their claim to a monopoly of force does not hold over part of their territory.

Topic 1 of MIT 17.50 goes on to ask about the harms states cause, and whether a world without states would be better than life under a bad state. Tilly's analogy sharpens that question. Yet by Mann's distinction, what protects ordinary modern life is precisely the state's infrastructural power: courts, tax offices, and clinics that work all the way to the edges of the territory.

7. Applying it to 2026: are this year's wars building states or building debt?

The facts. By NATO's measure, Poland's defense spending rose from 2.21 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) in 2022 to an estimated 4.68 percent in 2026 (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Poland issue). The draft 2027 budget the Polish government adopted on August 28, 2026, puts defense spending at 198.1 billion zloty, or 4.5 percent of GDP. In the same draft, state budget revenue is 695 billion zloty and spending 977.6 billion zloty, a state budget deficit of 282.6 billion zloty, while the general government deficit on the EU measure is projected at 7.1 percent of GDP. In 2026 Poland's public debt passed the EU's limit of 60 percent of GDP for the first time on record (Notes from Poland, August 28, 2026; Polish Radio, August 28, 2026). About 131.7 billion zloty of the defense total, roughly 3 percent of GDP, comes from the state budget (Defence24, September 18, 2026); the rest is spent outside it, mainly through the Armed Forces Support Fund, which is run by the state development bank BGK and relies on bonds and loans (Eska, August 28, 2026; Supreme Audit Office of Poland, January 5, 2026). Meanwhile, on December 18, 2025, President Karol Nawrocki vetoed government bills raising the excise on alcohol and the levy on sugary drinks, accusing the government of taking the easy option of "reaching into Poles' pockets" (Notes from Poland, December 19, 2025).

On March 18, 2025, the German Bundestag amended the Basic Law to exempt defense spending above 1 percent of GDP from the constitutional debt brake, by a vote of 512 to 206 (German Bundestag). The government's draft 2027 budget allocates €11.6 billion to military aid for Ukraine (the basis of Clisa Geopolitics forecast f76). The 2027 budget approved by the Bundestag's budget committee in the early hours of November 13, 2026, provides for about €560.9 billion in total spending and about €123 billion in net new borrowing in the core budget; with the special funds included, new borrowing is expected to exceed €200 billion. The final floor vote is scheduled for the sitting week of November 24 to 27 (ZDF, November 13, 2026). This lecture could not confirm whether the committee changed the Ukraine aid figure.

Russia raised its standard value-added tax (VAT) rate from 20 to 22 percent on January 1, 2026. The Finance Ministry said the extra revenue would go mainly to defense and security spending (Meduza, September 24, 2025), and President Putin signed the law on November 28, 2025 (Anadolu Agency, November 28, 2025).

In Iran, after the war began with U.S. and Israeli strikes on February 28, 2026, GDP in the first quarter of the Iranian year (March 21 to June 20) fell 10.1 percent from a year earlier, crude loadings dropped from about 2 million barrels a day in March to 220,000 to 250,000 in August, and twelve-month average inflation was reported at 69.9 percent. The United States reimposed its naval blockade on July 14. Iran, for its part, opens the Strait of Hormuz selectively, letting ships from "friendly" countries through after coordination or payment of a toll (one reported case: $2 million per ship) (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Iran issue). On November 7, U.S. Central Command launched the Iron Gate task force to stop advanced weapons, dual-use technology, sanctioned goods, and illicit imports from crossing Iran's land borders and the Caspian Sea (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, November 9, 2026).

Through Tilly's lens. The four countries show four ways of paying for war and for preparing for it. Russia raised its broadest tax, the VAT, increasing domestic extraction. Although the State Duma passed the law, the extraction came without real bargaining with a legislature, so it is closer, in Mann's terms, to extraction resting on despotic power (this lecture's interpretation). Poland and Germany are covering much of their rising defense costs with debt rather than taxes. Poland has raised some taxes, including a new levy on banks that took effect in 2026 (Notes from Poland, August 28, 2026), but the 2027 draft still projects a general government deficit above 7 percent of GDP. By drawing on bond markets and the EU as sources of capital to prepare for war, they resemble Tilly's capitalized coercion path (this lecture's interpretation).

The bargaining Tilly emphasized is visible too. The Polish president's veto shows where political resistance comes from when the state tries to take more, and Germany's constitutional amendment required a bargain that won two-thirds of the Bundestag. Debt does not eliminate bargaining; it postpones it. Interest and principal must eventually be paid out of future taxes, and Poland's debt-servicing costs are projected to reach 107 billion zloty in 2027, 17 billion more than the year before (Notes from Poland, August 28, 2026).

In this lecture's reading, Iran fits Tilly's analogy almost literally. Threatening ships in the strait while charging tolls and guaranteeing passage to "friendly" nations is a business that sells both the danger and the shield (this lecture's interpretation). Tilly applied the analogy to state making across early modern Europe, so it does not single Iran out as a criminal enterprise, and Tehran presents its control of the strait as a wartime measure against the countries attacking it and those that side with them (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Iran issue). Yet the blockade has sharply cut the Iranian state's main base of extraction, its oil exports. For a state with little room to broaden taxes, the easy way to pay for war is to print money, and inflation near 70 percent can be read as a sign that the cost is being passed to the public as a hidden tax (this lecture's interpretation). Import shortages caused by the blockade and the fall of the rial (from about 1 million to more than 2.2 million per dollar) also push prices up, however, so the share due to money printing is hard to isolate. Beyond the naval blockade, the Iron Gate task force extends the pressure to the land and Caspian supply routes.

Limits. Tilly's thesis concerns processes that unfolded over centuries, and one or two years of budgets cannot test it. Today's European states also have what early modern monarchs lacked, namely deep bond markets, joint EU borrowing, and the NATO alliance, so the pressure to build capacity on their own is correspondingly weaker.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads key passages from Weber in a classic English translation, works through the arithmetic of war finance, and looks at research that has tested the link between war and state capacity.

Reading the original

The quotations from Weber in this session, both below and in the main text, are taken from the English translation by H. H. Gerth and C. Wright Mills in From Max Weber: Essays in Sociology (Oxford University Press, 1946), with omissions marked by ellipses. The numbers in parentheses are that book's page numbers.

Passage 1. Defining the state ("Politics as a Vocation," p. 78)

If no social institutions existed which knew the use of violence, then the concept of 'state' would be eliminated, and a condition would emerge that could be designated as 'anarchy,' in the specific sense of this word. ... Today, however, we have to say that a state is a human community that (successfully) claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory. Note that 'territory' is one of the characteristics of the state. ... The state is considered the sole source of the 'right' to use violence. (78)

How to read it. What Weber emphasizes is not the amount of force but monopoly and legitimacy. The more secure and legitimate the monopoly, the less often a state actually has to use force. Think of a country where people obey court rulings without the police ever drawing a gun.

Passage 2. Three legitimations (pp. 78–79)

Like the political institutions historically preceding it, the state is a relation of men dominating men, a relation supported by means of legitimate (i.e. considered to be legitimate) violence. ... When and why do men obey? ... First, the authority of the 'eternal yesterday,' ... This is 'traditional' domination exercised by the patriarch and the patrimonial prince of yore. There is the authority of the extraordinary and personal gift of grace (charisma), ... This is 'charismatic' domination, as exercised by the prophet or ... by the elected war lord, the plebiscitarian ruler, the great demagogue, or the political party leader. Finally, there is domination by virtue of 'legality,' by virtue of the belief in the validity of legal statute and functional 'competence' based on rationally created rules. (78–79)

How to read it. Notice that Weber puts the "elected war lord" and the "political party leader" on the charismatic side. Leaders in a modern democracy take on a charismatic character to the extent that they rely on personal appeal.

Working it out

Problem 1. Taxes or debt? A country whose peacetime tax revenue is 200 a year fights a four-year war that costs 100 a year. Compare (a) paying for the war entirely with taxes and (b) paying 40 a year with taxes and 60 a year with bonds at 5 percent interest. (To keep things simple, ignore interest during the war, and assume the bonds are never repaid, with interest paid indefinitely.) (1) How much extra tax do citizens pay over the four years in each case? (2) In case (b), how much debt is left after the war, how much interest is owed each year, and what percentage of peacetime revenue is that interest? (3) Through Tilly's logic of bargaining, how do the two approaches differ?

Problem 2. Poland's 2027 defense finances. Use the figures from section 7: defense spending of 198.1 billion zloty, of which about 131.7 billion comes from the state budget; state budget revenue of 695 billion zloty; and spending of 977.6 billion zloty. (1) What share of defense spending comes from outside the state budget? (2) What share of state budget spending is the deficit? (3) Assume that defense spending inside the budget relies on deficit financing in the same proportion as the budget as a whole, and that everything outside the budget is borrowed. Roughly what share of defense spending is then financed by debt? Why might this estimate be too high?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. In (a), 100 × 4 = 400. In (b), 40 × 4 = 160.
  2. The debt in (b) is 60 × 4 = 240. Interest is 240 × 0.05 = 12 a year, or 6 percent of peacetime revenue of 200, and it must be paid out of taxes every year after the war.
  3. In (a), the ruler has to raise an extra 100 every year during the war and so must win the consent of parliament and taxpayers again and again. By Tilly's logic, that bargaining produces representation and rights. In (b), the main bargaining partners during the war become creditors, that is, capitalists. Bargaining with taxpayers does not disappear; it is postponed until after the war and stretched out as 12 in interest each year. As Centeno found in Latin America, a war fought on borrowed money can leave debt behind without building a tax administration.

Problem 2.

  1. The off-budget share is 198.1 − 131.7 = 66.4 billion zloty, and 66.4 ÷ 198.1 ≈ 0.335, or about 33.5 percent.
  2. The deficit is 977.6 − 695 = 282.6 billion zloty, and 282.6 ÷ 977.6 ≈ 0.289, or about 28.9 percent.
  3. The deficit-financed part of on-budget defense spending is 131.7 × 0.289 ≈ 38.1 billion zloty. Adding the 66.4 billion off budget gives about 104.5 billion, and 104.5 ÷ 198.1 ≈ 0.527, or about 53 percent. The estimate may be too high, because the Armed Forces Support Fund may have income other than borrowing, and one could argue that defense is funded from revenue before other spending is. The exact share would have to be checked against final accounts. Still, the calculation shows that much of Poland's defense buildup rests on future taxes, in other words on debt.

Evidence and counterarguments

Besley and Persson (2009): war and inclusive institutions build state capacity. The economists Timothy Besley and Torsten Persson modeled a state's fiscal and legal capacity as the accumulated result of past investment. They concluded that common-interest public goods such as fighting external wars, along with political stability and inclusive political institutions, are conducive to building state capacity. They reported that some cross-country correlations were consistent with the theory, which falls short of a causal test (American Economic Review 99, no. 4).

Thies (2005): rivalry instead of war. Cameron G. Thies argued that the conclusion that war did not build Latin American states came from looking only at wars themselves. He replaced interstate war with the broader concept of interstate rivalry and added domestic rivals to the analysis. His cross-sectional time-series analyses found that external and internal rivals shaped Latin American states in the way bellicist theory, the theory that war makes states, would predict (American Journal of Political Science 49, no. 3). It is a useful lens for a country like Poland in 2026, which is raising defense spending without fighting a war.

Schenoni (2021): winning or losing sets a state's course. Using Latin American data from 1865 to 1913, Luis L. Schenoni focused on how wars ended. Losers fell behind winners in railroad mileage and revenue per capita, and the gap widened over time. Paraguay (the 1864–1870 war) and Peru (the War of the Pacific, 1879–1883) are his cases of defeat, while victorious Chile introduced an income tax and greatly expanded its state railways (American Journal of Political Science 65, no. 2).

Counterargument: doubts about Herbst's population-density explanation. In a review essay on Herbst's book, the political economist James A. Robinson questioned the population-density explanation, noting that areas with higher precolonial population density had, on average, fared worse after colonial rule (Journal of Economic Literature 40, no. 2). Whether weak states are best explained by geography and population or by the institutions colonialism left behind and the choices of elites remains in dispute, and session 11 returns to the question.
