The question for this session

The Iran war that began in late February 2026, and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz that came with it, hit Asia's oil importers with the same shock. Yet the alternatives they turned to were different. In South Korea, the Middle East's share of crude imports fell while imports from the United States, Canada, and Australia rose. In India, Russian crude made up more than half of all crude imports in June. Why did two countries facing the same shock take such different paths?

To answer that, we have to set the two countries side by side, sort out what they share and where they differ, and then find a way to judge which of those differences actually changed the outcome. That is the basic work of comparative politics. In his 1891 poem "The English Flag," Rudyard Kipling asked, "And what should they know of England who only England know?" One way to read the line: if you study only one country, you cannot even tell what about it is unusual and what is commonplace.

This session covers how to find causes by comparing countries, and the limits of doing so. It then introduces the five approaches this course will use and applies the method of difference to the question above.

1. What comparative politics studies

Where international relations deals with what happens between countries, such as war, alliances, trade, and international organizations (Introduction to International Relations), comparative politics deals with politics within countries. It centers on four areas: why some countries are democracies and others are authoritarian (regimes); why some states can enforce the law in every corner of their territory while others have little power outside the capital (the state); how presidential and parliamentary systems, electoral rules, and parties shape the behavior of politicians and voters (institutions); and why economic development, civil war, corruption, and revolution vary so much from country to country (outcomes).

Comparative politics is defined by its method as well as its subject. Even a scholar who studies only South Korea has to look at countries that abandoned presidential government, and at countries that chose parliamentary government from the start, to answer the question "Why does South Korea keep its presidential system?" Nor do the units of comparison have to be countries. One can compare regions within a country, different periods in the same country, or parties across several countries.

The two fields do meet. Session 6 of Introduction to International Relations traced how domestic politics shapes foreign policy, and session 3 of that course treated the Strait of Hormuz as an interaction between the choices of the United States and Iran. Section 7 of this session looks instead at how one external shock was filtered through each importing country's institutions and interests and produced different choices.

2. Why compare? Experiments for a field that cannot experiment

Natural scientists find causes through experiments, for example by randomly dividing subjects into two groups and giving a drug to only one of them. Political scientists usually cannot do that. Nobody can randomly assign a presidential system to one country and a parliamentary system to another.

In "Comparative Politics and the Comparative Method" (1971), Arend Lijphart divided scientific methods into the experimental, the statistical, and the comparative, and treated the case study as closely related to the comparative method. All three seek general relationships between variables while holding other conditions constant. The experimental method controls conditions directly; the statistical method uses many cases to filter out the influence of other variables mathematically; and the comparative method, used when there are too few cases for statistical control, selects cases carefully and compares them.

Comparison does three jobs. First, it provides a baseline. The fact that the Middle East supplied 65 percent of South Korea's crude imports in April 2025 means little until it is set against other importers. Second, it tests explanations. If it is true that "allies of the United States do not buy oil from sanctioned countries," then allies and non-allies should actually behave differently. Third, it lets the real experience of other countries stand in for counterfactuals. We cannot rerun history to see what South Korea would have done if it had not been a U.S. ally, but we can observe what a non-allied country in a similar position did. How similar that country really is determines how much the substitution is worth.

3. Mill's methods of agreement and difference

The first systematic statement of the logic of finding causes by comparison came from the English philosopher John Stuart Mill. In book 3, chapter 8 of A System of Logic (1843), "Of the Four Methods of Experimental Inquiry," he identified two "simplest and most obvious" ways of singling out causes. The method of agreement compares different instances in which a phenomenon occurs. The method of difference compares an instance in which the phenomenon occurs with an instance, similar in other respects, in which it does not. Mill stated their rules, or canons, as follows:

If two or more instances of the phenomenon under investigation have only one circumstance in common, the circumstance in which alone all the instances agree, is the cause (or effect) of the given phenomenon. (book 3, chapter 8, section 1, first canon)

If an instance in which the phenomenon under investigation occurs, and an instance in which it does not occur, have every circumstance in common save one, that one occurring only in the former; the circumstance in which alone the two instances differ, is the effect, or the cause, or an indispensable part of the cause, of the phenomenon. (book 3, chapter 8, section 2, second canon)

A simple table with conditions A through G and an outcome y makes the contrast clear.

Method Case 1 Case 2 Case 3 Conclusion
Agreement A, B, C → y A, D, E → y A, F, G → y Only A is common to all, so A is the candidate cause
Difference A, B, C → y B, C → no y The cases differ only in A, so A is the candidate cause

Mill called the method of difference "a still more potent instrument of the investigation of nature." The method of agreement eliminates conditions that cannot be the cause, but there is no way to confirm that only one condition is truly shared. The method of difference requires two cases that differ in just one condition, and the surest way to produce such cases is an experiment. Mill therefore treated the method of difference as the method of artificial experiment and the method of agreement as the one to fall back on when experiment is impossible.

Comparative politics borrows Mill's methods for comparing cases. Mill himself, however, was deeply skeptical about applying them to society. In book 6, chapter 7, he wrote that the supposition that one could find two nations alike in everything except their trade policy is "manifestly absurd." We will see why in Going deeper, where we read the original.

4. Most similar and most different: research designs and natural experiments

In The Logic of Comparative Social Inquiry (1970), Adam Przeworski and Henry Teune distinguished two designs for comparative research.

The most similar systems design. The researcher selects countries that resemble one another in many respects, such as history, culture, and level of economic development. Shared conditions cannot explain differences in outcomes, so they drop out, and the few remaining differences become the candidate causes. This is the method of difference put into practice. Its weakness is that even similar countries differ in several ways, which makes it hard to tell which difference produced the outcome.

The most different systems design. This is the design Przeworski and Teune preferred. The researcher selects countries that are very different from one another and then examines relationships between variables at the level of individuals or groups within each country. If a relationship holds in the same way across countries with entirely different cultures and institutions, then country characteristics are not needed to explain it; if the relationship varies from country to country, factors at the country level are at work. The design builds on the logic of agreement while multiplying observations within cases to get around the shortage of cases.

Natural experiments. Sometimes the accidents of history create a situation that resembles an experiment. Daniel N. Posner's 2004 study is a classic example. The border between Zambia and Malawi was drawn by the British South Africa Company in 1891 to separate administrative territories, with no attention to how ethnic groups were distributed on the ground. As a result, the Chewa and Tumbuka peoples were split by the border. Through surveys in villages on both sides, Posner showed that the objective cultural differences between the two groups were the same on each side, yet the two groups were political adversaries in Malawi and allies in Zambia.

His explanation is group size. In Malawi, the Chewa make up 28 percent of the population and the Tumbuka 12 percent, large enough to serve as building blocks for national political coalitions; in Zambia, they make up only 7 percent and 4 percent. Whether a cultural difference becomes a political cleavage depends on whether the groups it defines are large enough to be useful in political competition (session 10). Before reaching that conclusion, Posner examined competing explanations one by one, including gaps in colonial-era mission schooling, the timing of his surveys relative to elections, and the ethnic policies of each country's first president, and concluded that none of them accounted for the difference. The same missionary societies, for instance, had set up schools on both sides of the border, and the educational gap between the two groups was nearly identical on each side. Even a natural experiment yields a conclusion only after rival explanations have been ruled out.

The Korean case. For Korean readers, the most familiar natural experiment is the division of the peninsula. In chapter 3 of Why Nations Fail (2012), Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson argue that the two halves of Korea, which shared a language, a history, and a culture until 1945, adopted different institutions after division and ended up with sharply different economic fortunes, and they conclude that institutions, not culture or geography, made the difference. It is a powerful case, but three points deserve attention. First, Japanese colonial rule had placed heavy industry, such as steel and chemicals, and hydroelectric power mainly in the north (Library of Congress, South Korea: A Country Study). Since this favored the north, it does not weaken the institutional explanation, but it does mean the two halves did not start out identical. Second, the institutions were brought in by two occupying powers, the United States and the Soviet Union, and with them came different alliances, aid, and security environments. Third, there is only one pair, which makes it hard to say which institutions mattered and how much (session 11).

5. Many variables, few cases: case studies, large-N statistics, and selection bias

The problem of too few cases. Lijphart summed up the central problem of the comparative method as the combination of many variables and few cases. Few countries have gone through revolutions or democratic transitions, while the conditions that might affect such outcomes are countless, so almost any explanation can be made to fit the data and none can be properly tested. He proposed four remedies: increase the number of cases as far as possible; reduce the number of variables by combining similar ones; focus on comparable cases, those that are alike in many respects; and focus on the variables that matter most in theory. He also distinguished six types of case study, and he valued deviant cases in particular, since a close look at a case that departs from a theory's prediction can reveal variables the theory has missed.

Case studies and large-N statistics. Statistical analysis of data from many countries allows conclusions to be generalized and other variables to be controlled, but it shows little about the process that produces a relationship. In-depth study of a few cases makes it possible to trace how a cause leads to an effect, but it is hard to know whether the conclusions hold elsewhere. The work of Lipset (1959) and of Przeworski and Limongi (1997), discussed in session 3 of Introduction to Political Philosophy, is an example of the first strategy (session 3 of this course takes it up in more depth), and Posner's study is an example of the second. Much research combines the two.

Selection bias. In "How the Cases You Choose Affect the Answers You Get" (1990), Barbara Geddes pointed to one of the most common mistakes in comparative politics: studying only cases in which the outcome to be explained actually occurred. Her example is labor repression and economic growth. Scholars who studied fast-growing economies such as those of East Asia, Brazil, and Mexico found that these countries had all repressed organized labor and argued that labor repression was a condition for rapid growth. Without looking at countries that repressed labor and still failed to grow, however, the claim has no support. Geddes reexamined three well-known studies in comparative politics and showed that when the same arguments were tested on cases selected without regard to the outcome, the results differed from the original conclusions. The method of agreement, which compares only cases in which the outcome occurred, is especially vulnerable to this trap. The remedy is to choose cases so that the outcome varies.

6. Classics and theory: a map of this course in five approaches

Throughout the course, we will bring several approaches to bear on the same phenomenon and use the comparative methods of this session to judge which explanation fits the evidence best.

7. Applying it to 2026: why did South Korea and India buy different oil after the same Hormuz shock?

The facts. The war began on February 28, 2026, with U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran. Iran restricted traffic through the Strait of Hormuz with mines and attacks on merchant ships, and on July 14 the United States reimposed a naval blockade of Iran (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Iran issue). Brent crude settled at $116.72 a barrel on October 23 (Clisa Geopolitics Briefing, October 24, 2026). Here is how the two countries' situations and choices compare.

Point of comparison South Korea India

Through the method of difference. The two countries were exposed to the same condition, the Hormuz shock, but their outcomes differ, so the comparison has the shape the method of difference calls for. Some conditions can be eliminated right away. When oil prices surged in March, Washington itself temporarily allowed trade in Russian oil (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the Russia-Ukraine war). The first waiver, General License 133 of March 5, covered only cargoes bound for India, but General License 134 of March 12 authorized the sale and delivery of Russian crude and petroleum products already loaded on vessels, with no restriction on destination (U.S. Treasury, Office of Foreign Assets Control). From then on, U.S. legal permission was the same for both countries, so it cannot by itself explain the difference. The fact that India, having agreed in February to stop importing Russian oil, was getting more than half its crude from Russia by June points the same way.

At least three differences remain: the alliance with the United States, the way Iran treats each country's ships, and the fact that India already had channels, open since 2022, for importing Russian crude (this lecture could not confirm the situation on the Korean side). With several conditions differing at once, the method of difference narrows the list of candidate causes but cannot settle on one.

Looking inside the case. In a situation like this, one can trace how the decision was made within a single case. On March 24, 2026, the Asia Business Daily reported that although the United States had temporarily lifted sanctions on Russian and Iranian crude already loaded and at sea, South Korean refiners saw no real benefit. They gave three reasons: most domestic refineries are designed for Middle Eastern crude; refiners earn most of their profits from exporting refined products, and buyers may shy away from products made from sanctioned crude, which could also face restrictions in Europe; and Russia is cut off from the SWIFT payment network, which makes payment difficult. This testimony supports the interpretation that what constrained South Korea was less whether a purchase was legally permitted than which markets its exports and payments are tied to (this lecture's interpretation).

The limits. Mill's warning applies to this interpretation too. The alliance, the export markets, and the payment channels are not independent conditions. All three grew out of South Korea's decades of integration into a U.S.-centered economic order, so in Mill's words they are "effects of pre-existing causes," and their effects cannot be separated one by one (this lecture's interpretation). There is also a measurement problem: the Korean figure comes from April data published by the Korea International Trade Association and the Indian figure from June ship-tracking data, so the timing and sources differ. Above all, there are only two cases. Adding Japan and Taiwan, which have close security ties with the United States and import nearly all their crude, and China, whose reliance on Russian oil has deepened (Clisa Geopolitics analysis of the world order), would help separate the alliance explanation from the existing-channels explanation. Comparing the same country across time is another option, and the forecasts below provide that opportunity.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It reads Mill in the original, applies the methods of agreement and difference to a small truth table, and looks at criticisms of using Mill's methods for small-N comparison, along with the replies.

Reading the original

The quotations from A System of Logic in this session are taken from the eighth edition (Harper & Brothers, 1882; Project Gutenberg eBook 27942), with omissions marked by ellipses. The first edition appeared in 1843, and the wording may vary slightly between editions.

Passage 1. The gunshot (book 3, chapter 8, section 2)

When a man is shot through the heart, it is by this method we know that it was the gunshot which killed him: for he was in the fullness of life immediately before, all circumstances being the same, except the wound.

How to read it. Mill treats the search for causes as a process of elimination. Comparing the same person immediately before and after is the cleanest form of the method of difference, which is why section 7 recommends comparing the same country over time. Unlike a person, though, a country changes in many respects between "before" and "after."

Passage 2. The method of difference is impossible in social science (book 6, chapter 7, section 3)

In order to apply to the case the most perfect of the methods of experimental inquiry, the Method of Difference, we require to find two instances which tally in every particular except the one which is the subject of inquiry. If two nations can be found which are alike in all natural advantages and disadvantages; whose people resemble each other in every quality, physical and moral, spontaneous and acquired; whose habits, usages, opinions, laws, and institutions are the same in all respects, except that one of them has a more protective tariff ... But the supposition that two such instances can be met with is manifestly absurd. ... Two nations which agreed in every thing except their commercial policy would agree also in that. Differences of legislation are not inherent and ultimate diversities; are not properties of Kinds. They are effects of pre-existing causes.

How to read it. This is the most important passage of the session. Differences in policy or institutions do not appear out of nowhere; they arise from other differences between the two countries, so two countries that differ in that one respect alone cannot exist. In section 4 of the same chapter, Mill is skeptical of the method of agreement as well. Social phenomena admit a "plurality of causes," so the fact that an outcome occurred somewhere without a given condition does not show that the condition was not a cause elsewhere. Instead, Mill recommended deducing social phenomena from the laws of individual human behavior.

Working it out

Problem 1. Finding causes with a truth table. Five hypothetical countries, labeled 1 through 5, are hit by the same energy shock. The outcome Y is "the share of crude from a particular sanctioned country rose sharply within six months of the shock." There are four conditions: A (heavy dependence on oil imports), B (an existing channel for importing the sanctioned country's crude), C (an alliance with the country leading the sanctions), and D (a state-owned stake in the major refiners). A 1 means the condition is present and a 0 means it is absent.

Country A B C D Y
1 1 1 0 1 1
2 1 1 0 0 1
3 0 1 0 1 1
4 1 0 1 1 0
5 1 1 1 0 0

(1) Applying the method of agreement to the countries where the outcome occurred (1, 2, and 3), which conditions remain as candidate causes? (2) Find two countries that differ in only one condition and have different outcomes, and apply the method of difference. Why does a comparison of countries 1 and 4 allow no conclusion? (3) What is the simplest rule that accounts for the whole table? Of the possible combinations of four conditions, how many does the table observe, and what limit does this place on the conclusion?

Problem 2. The arithmetic of selection bias. Twelve hypothetical countries are hit by the same oil shock. In eight of them, gasoline prices rose by more than 30 percent; in the other four, they rose less. The ruling party lost the next election in four of the eight countries with large increases and in two of the four with smaller ones. (1) If you look only at countries where the ruling party lost, what share of them saw gasoline prices rise by more than 30 percent? (2) Calculate the rate at which ruling parties lost for each group. (3) What is wrong with concluding that "most of the countries where the ruling party lost saw gasoline prices soar, so soaring prices cause electoral defeat"?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. The conditions shared by countries 1, 2, and 3 are the presence of B and the absence of C. A is absent in country 3 and D is absent in country 2, so both are eliminated. The method of agreement leaves two candidates and cannot tell which is the cause.
  2. Countries 2 (1, 1, 0, 0) and 5 (1, 1, 1, 0) differ only in C and have different outcomes, so by the method of difference C (the alliance) is a condition that blocks the outcome, or part of one. Countries 1 (1, 1, 0, 1) and 4 (1, 0, 1, 1) differ in both B and C, so there is no way to say which one accounts for the difference in outcomes. The comparison of South Korea and India in section 7 has this shape.
  3. The whole table is accounted for by the rule "Y occurs when B is present and C is absent." Country 5 shows that B alone is not sufficient; what matters is a combination of conditions. There are 2⁴ = 16 possible combinations, but the table observes only five. We cannot tell whether the outcome would occur in a country with neither B nor C, and we cannot rule out that some condition missing from the table is the real cause. A fundamental limit of small-N comparison is that the real world does not supply every combination.

Problem 2.

  1. Six countries saw the ruling party lose (4 + 2), and in four of them gasoline prices rose by more than 30 percent, so the share is about 67 percent.
  2. Among countries with increases above 30 percent, the ruling party lost in 4 ÷ 8 = 50 percent; among countries with smaller increases, in 2 ÷ 4 = 50 percent. The rates are the same.
  3. Prices soared in 67 percent of the countries where the ruling party lost simply because they soared in 67 percent of all twelve countries (eight of them). Looking only at cases where the outcome occurred makes a common condition stand out as if it were a cause; once all countries are compared, the loss rates do not differ. This is the structure of the selection bias Geddes identified.

Evidence and counterarguments

Lieberson (1991): small N's and big conclusions. The sociologist Stanley Lieberson argued in "Small N's and Big Conclusions" that studies applying Mill's methods to a handful of cases rest on strong but unstated assumptions: that causes operate deterministically rather than probabilistically, that measurement is free of error, that there is only one cause, and that causes do not interact. He held that unless these assumptions are rigorously justified, Mill's methods should not be used on small numbers of cases.

King, Keohane, and Verba (1994): one logic of inference. In Designing Social Inquiry, Gary King, Robert O. Keohane, and Sidney Verba argued that qualitative research should follow the same logic of inference as quantitative research. They advised against selecting only cases in which the outcome occurred and recommended that researchers with few cases increase the number of observations by looking within each case for several observable implications of their theory. The use of South Korean refiners' testimony in section 7 follows the same idea.

Counterargument: case studies are not an inferior substitute for statistics. In The Comparative Method (1987), Charles C. Ragin held that social causes often work through combinations of conditions and that several different combinations can lead to the same outcome. He proposed comparing combinations of conditions across cases with Boolean algebra, an approach later known as qualitative comparative analysis (QCA), to handle the combinatorial causation and plurality of causes that Mill's methods could not. The answer to problem 1, "when B is present and C is absent," is an example of such combinatorial causation. Rethinking Social Inquiry (2004), edited by Henry E. Brady and David Collier, criticized King, Keohane, and Verba, arguing that observations showing how a cause leads to an effect within a case differ in kind from statistical observations and can support strong inferences even when cases are few. Theda Skocpol's States and Social Revolutions (1979), which explicitly drew on Mill's methods, is the study most often cited in this debate (session 12). Today, comparative politics tends to combine natural experiments, large-N statistics, and process tracing within cases so that each method offsets the weaknesses of the others.
