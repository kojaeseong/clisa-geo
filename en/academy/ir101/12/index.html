The question for this session

On September 16, 2026, Belarus freed 25 prisoners and sent them across the border into Lithuania; the next day, the U.S. Treasury Department removed two Belarusian companies from its sanctions list. On September 24, President Alexander Lukashenko pardoned 15 more people but did not release their names. Around the same time, the Zaporizhzhia nuclear power plant in Ukraine, occupied by Russian forces since 2022, lost off-site power on August 20 and ran on emergency diesel generators until September 7 [secondary source]. Neither a government's jailing of its own citizens nor the risk posed by a nuclear plant on a front line stays within one country's borders. This session takes up four questions: Why is cooperation to protect the global environment so hard? Why do some environmental treaties work while others fail? Why do states join human rights treaties they do not intend to keep? And when does pressure from outside actually improve human rights?

1. Key concepts: externalities, public goods, and reciprocity in human rights

When a factory dumps wastewater into a river, the cost falls not on the factory but on the people downstream. A cost or benefit that lands on someone who is not party to a transaction is called an externality. A public good, such as a stable climate, is one from which no one can be excluded and whose use by one person leaves no less for others. A common-pool resource, such as a fishery or a pasture, is one from which it is hard to exclude anyone but which is depleted as people use it. Either way, each country has an incentive to free-ride on the emissions cuts of others. This is the collective action problem that Mancur Olson described, which we met in session 3.

Environmental problems are also problems of distribution. The costs of cutting emissions fall on the present generation and on particular industries, such as coal and oil, while the benefits are spread across future generations and the whole world. In a 2007 article in The Atlantic, Gregg Easterbrook argued that warming could hand Alaska, Canada, Greenland, Russia, and Scandinavia windfalls from rising land values while hurting low-latitude countries such as Indonesia, Mexico, and Nigeria. A problem that creates winners is harder to solve by agreement than one in which everyone loses (this lecture's interpretation).

Human rights are international norms governing how states treat their own people. On December 10, 1948, the UN General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in Resolution 217 A (III), by a vote of 48 to 0, with 8 abstentions. The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), both adopted on December 16, 1966, entered into force in 1976. Unlike a trade or arms control agreement, a human rights treaty can be broken by one country without directly harming any other. Reciprocity—answering a violation with one's own—therefore has no purchase, and the central question in the politics of human rights becomes who will make states comply (this lecture's interpretation).

2. A classic: Hardin's "The Tragedy of the Commons" and Ostrom's reply

In "The Tragedy of the Commons," published in Science in 1968, Garrett Hardin pictured a pasture open to all. A herdsman who adds one more animal keeps the entire gain for himself, while the cost of the depleted grass is shared among all the herdsmen. So each rational herdsman keeps adding animals without limit, and all of them rush toward ruin (p. 1244). Pollution, Hardin wrote, is the same tragedy in reverse—putting something into the commons rather than taking something out—because a polluter's share of the cost of his waste is smaller than the cost of treating it (p. 1245). Hardin's remedy was "mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon by the majority of the people affected" (p. 1247). His real subject, though, was population. He argued that the population problem has no technical solution and that the "freedom to breed" would have to be relinquished (pp. 1243, 1248).

In Governing the Commons (1990), Elinor Ostrom challenged this pessimism. Neither the state nor the market, she observed, has consistently succeeded in managing natural resources, and communities have sustained resources over long periods with institutions that resemble neither (p. 1). In the Swiss village of Törbel, a rule written in 1517 barred anyone from sending more cows to the summer alpine pastures than he could feed through the winter (pp. 61–62). From long-enduring common-pool resource institutions, Ostrom drew a set of design principles, among them clearly defined boundaries, participation by users in setting the rules, monitors who are accountable to the users, and graduated sanctions that grow heavier with the seriousness of the offense. She herself called the list "still quite speculative" (p. 90). The work earned her the 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, which she shared with Oliver E. Williamson. Because there is no government above states to do the coercing, mutual coercion in international environmental politics takes the form of arrangements states agree to by treaty, such as the trade restrictions of the Montreal Protocol. Because the users—here, states—write their own rules and sanctions, it also resembles Ostrom's self-governance (this lecture's interpretation).

3. Montreal, Kyoto, and Paris: how treaty design matters

The Montreal Protocol, which regulates chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and other substances that deplete the ozone layer, was signed on September 16, 1987, and entered into force on January 1, 1989. Its Article 4 banned imports of controlled substances from nonparties. The 1990 London Amendment created a financial mechanism to help developing countries meet the costs of compliance, and an interim Multilateral Fund began operating in January 1991. On September 16, 2009, the protocol and its parent treaty, the Vienna Convention, became the first treaties in the UN system to be ratified by every country. The 2022 scientific assessment by the World Meteorological Organization and the UN Environment Programme projected that total ozone over Antarctica would return to 1980 levels around 2066.

The Kyoto Protocol (adopted December 11, 1997; in force February 16, 2005) imposed binding, country-by-country targets only on the states listed in its Annex I—the developed countries and the former communist "economies in transition"—requiring them to cut their combined emissions over 2008–2012 to at least 5 percent below 1990 levels (Article 3.1). The United States signed in 1998 but never ratified, and Canada gave notice of withdrawal on December 15, 2011. The Paris Agreement (adopted December 12, 2015; in force November 4, 2016) took a different approach: every party submits a nationally determined contribution every five years, and each new pledge must go further than the last (Article 4). The United States gave notice of its second withdrawal on January 27, 2025, and the withdrawal took effect on January 27, 2026.

In Environment and Statecraft (2003), Scott Barrett argued that treaties change behavior only by changing incentives, and that a successful treaty must be self-enforcing, making it in each country's interest both to join and to comply. Montreal's trade restrictions are his example; Kyoto, he predicted, would fail because it did not restructure incentives [secondary source]. In 2015, William Nordhaus found, in a modeling study, that a "climate club" imposing small trade penalties on nonmembers could induce a large, stable coalition to make deep emissions cuts. Another difference matters too: a handful of companies made CFCs, and substitutes existed, whereas carbon dioxide comes from virtually every kind of economic activity (this lecture's interpretation).

4. Are human rights treaties kept? Hathaway and the paradox of empty promises

In a 2002 article, Oona A. Hathaway analyzed treaties on genocide, torture, fair trials, civil liberties, and the political representation of women, using data on 166 countries from 1960 to 1999 (pp. 1939, 1967). Countries that had ratified generally had better ratings. But once other factors were taken into account, ratification was not infrequently associated with worse ratings than would otherwise be expected, and not a single treaty was reliably associated with better practices. She did find evidence that ratification by full democracies was associated with better practices. Hathaway cautioned that this does not necessarily mean treaties make practices worse: countries with poor records may be more inclined to ratify, or violations by ratifying countries may simply be better documented (p. 1940). Her explanation was that treaties also have an expressive function—they declare a commitment. Because monitoring and enforcement are minimal, the relatively costless step of ratification can relieve pressure for costly changes in policy (p. 1941).

Using data on 153 countries from 1976 to 1999, Emilie M. Hafner-Burton and Kiyoteru Tsutsui reported in 2005 what they called the "paradox of empty promises." Ratification of the six core UN human rights treaties was associated with more repression, not less; yet the more of a country's citizens belonged to international nongovernmental organizations, the better its government protected their rights (pp. 1373, 1389, 1398). In Mobilizing for Human Rights (2009), Beth A. Simmons argued that treaties change politics by reshaping national legislative agendas, supporting litigation, and spurring social mobilization (p. 8). In an article published the same year, she argued that treaties have their greatest effect not in stable autocracies, where demands are crushed, nor in stable democracies, where rights are already largely secured, but in transitional countries, where citizens have the most to gain from mobilizing.

5. When does outside pressure work? The boomerang pattern and the spiral model

In Activists beyond Borders (1998), Margaret E. Keck and Kathryn Sikkink described a "boomerang pattern": when the channels between a state and its domestic groups are blocked, those groups bypass their government, seek out international allies, who then bring pressure on the state from outside [secondary source]. In 1999, Thomas Risse, Stephen C. Ropp, and Sikkink put forward a five-phase "spiral model": repression, denial, tactical concessions, prescriptive status, and rule-consistent behavior. In the tactical-concessions phase, a government makes concessions to get the international human rights community "off their backs," as Risse and Ropp put it: releasing a few political prisoners, tolerating more public protest, or signing international treaties (Risse and Ropp 2013, pp. 6–7).

Other research suggests that material incentives work better than persuasion. Examining 177 countries from 1972 to 2002, Hafner-Burton reported in 2005 that preferential trade agreements with "hard" human rights standards—ones that tie the material benefits of integration to compliance—often produced better practices, while human rights treaties and agreements with only "soft" standards did not systematically produce improvement (p. 593). Read alongside Hathaway, this points to the same conclusion as Barrett's account of environmental treaties: a promise that costs nothing to break does not change behavior (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Historical cases

The Helsinki Final Act (1975). Signed on August 1, 1975, the Helsinki Final Act committed its signatories, in Principle VII, to respect human rights and fundamental freedoms, confirmed "the right of the individual to know and act upon his rights and duties in this field," and included a section on "Co-operation in Humanitarian and Other Fields," the so-called third basket. In The Helsinki Effect (2001), Daniel C. Thomas argued that the document gave opposition movements in Eastern Europe a common platform around which to mobilize and helped undermine one-party Communist rule [secondary source]. The case fits the boomerang pattern: a nonbinding pledge became a lever that activists at home and abroad used to apply pressure (this lecture's interpretation).

North Korea's attempt to withdraw from the ICCPR (1997). North Korea, which had acceded to the ICCPR on September 14, 1981, sent the UN secretary-general a notice of withdrawal dated August 23, 1997. The covenant contains no withdrawal clause, and the secretary-general took the view that withdrawal was not possible unless all the other parties agreed. In General Comment No. 26, adopted on October 29, 1997, the UN Human Rights Committee went further, declaring that international law does not permit a state to withdraw from the covenant (para. 5). That Pyongyang tried to withdraw at all suggests that it did not regard its commitment as merely a costless gesture (this lecture's interpretation).

The destruction of the Kakhovka dam (2023). After the Kakhovka dam in southern Ukraine was destroyed on June 6, 2023, the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), citing Ukrainian authorities, reported that at least 37 towns and villages had been flooded and that the government was supporting the evacuation of some 16,000 people. Water levels in the reservoir, a source of drinking water for about 700,000 people, reportedly fell by 70 percent. Article 56 of the 1977 Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions prohibits attacks on works and installations containing dangerous forces—dams, dikes, and nuclear power stations—even when they are military objectives, if the attack may release those forces and cause severe losses among civilians; paragraph 2 sets out narrow exceptions. The case shows how environmental damage in wartime becomes a human rights problem as well (this lecture's interpretation).

7. Applying it to 2026: Belarus's prisoner deals and the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant

The facts. Belarus began releasing political prisoners a few at a time through pardons in July 2024, and from June 2025 it freed them in larger groups timed to visits by U.S. envoys. Washington eased some sanctions in response to most of these releases. Clisa Geopolitics judges that Lukashenko's government, even as it deepens its integration with Russia, is pursuing transactional diplomacy—trading political prisoners for U.S. sanctions relief—and lists "whether the U.S.-Belarus trade of sanctions relief for prisoner releases expands" among the developments it is watching. The number of political prisoners counted by the Belarusian human rights group Viasna fell from at least 1,177 in June 2025 to 887 on September 16, 2026. But on September 15 a Viasna lawyer said the regime runs a "revolving door," constantly jailing new political prisoners, and the group called the September 16 release, in which prisoners were stripped of their passports and expelled, a "forced deportation" [secondary source]. Since June 23, 2025, when the figure of 1,177 was reported, the releases in the table below alone total 450, yet Viasna's count fell by only about 290. Not everyone freed was on Viasna's list—14 of the 52 people released on September 11, 2025, were foreign nationals—so the gap cannot be attributed entirely to new arrests. Prisoners released at the end of their sentences, on the other hand, would have pushed the count down further and so cannot explain the gap; new arrests appear to have offset much of the effect of the releases (this lecture's interpretation).

Russian forces seized the Zaporizhzhia plant on March 4, 2022, and all six of its reactors are in cold shutdown. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) has kept staff on site since September 1, 2022, and on May 30, 2023, its director general, Rafael Grossi, set out five principles before the UN Security Council, including that the plant must not be attacked and that its off-site power must not be put at risk. The IAEA's September report stated that the plant lost off-site power 10 times between June 2 and August 29, 2026, bringing the total since the war began to 26. Clisa Geopolitics' People's Lives tab treats an attack on, the occupation of, or a loss of power at a nuclear facility—or the destruction of a large dam—as a trigger for its environmental alert. As long as the Zaporizhzhia plant remains occupied, the alert for Ukrainians stays on in every future the site projects. On March 17, 2023, the International Criminal Court issued arrest warrants for Russian President Vladimir Putin and Maria Lvova-Belova, the Russian presidential commissioner for children's rights, over the unlawful deportation and transfer of Ukrainian children from occupied areas. A Ukrainian government website puts the number of children reported as unlawfully deported at 20,651 and the number returned—from deportation, forcible transfer, or occupied territory—at 2,689 (accessed October 15, 2026).

Through the lens of theory. In the language of the spiral model, releasing some political prisoners is a tactical concession. Because a material reward—sanctions relief—secured the releases, the arrangement resembles Hafner-Burton's "hard standards." The difference is that her trade agreements made continued respect for rights the condition, whereas in a deal-by-deal exchange the government is paid per prisoner released, which leaves it an incentive to arrest more. The conditions Simmons associated with transitional countries—situations in which citizens have much to gain from mobilizing—disappeared in Belarus as the 1996 constitutional referendum concentrated power in the presidency and the mass protests that followed the 2020 presidential election were crushed, and the country is now closer to a stable autocracy in which domestic mobilization is crushed, so improvement through treaties is unlikely. The risk at Zaporizhzhia is a cross-border externality. In keeping with the managerial model discussed in session 9, the IAEA's presence and reporting increase transparency, but the agency has no means of enforcing its principles. Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows the arguments of Hardin and Hathaway in the original, works through free riding and the measurement of treaty effects with numbers, and looks at research that has put the theories to the test.

Reading the original

Passage 1. Rational individuals and common ruin

"Ruin is the destination toward which all men rush, each pursuing his own best interest in a society that believes in the freedom of the commons. Freedom in a commons brings ruin to all." (Hardin 1968, p. 1244)

The premise is that the herdsman who adds an animal captures the whole gain (in Hardin's terms, nearly +1) while the loss is shared by all (only a fraction of −1) (p. 1244). The conclusion is that each person's rational choice leads to ruin for all. Hardin thought the tragedy of the pasture could be averted by private property or something like it, but that the air and water, which cannot be fenced, would have to be protected from pollution by coercive laws or taxes (p. 1245). Ostrom's objection is that the model takes an assumption made for the sake of analysis—that users cannot change the rules—and treats it as a fact about the real world (pp. 6–7). Among states, the coercion Hardin called for can come only from what they agree to among themselves (this lecture's interpretation).

Passage 2. A costless promise

"Countries that take the relatively costless step of treaty ratification may thereby offset pressure for costly changes in policies." (Hathaway 2002, p. 1941)

The premise is that the monitoring and enforcement of human rights treaties are usually minimal (also p. 1941). Ratification therefore becomes less a means of changing behavior than a way of expressing commitment, and the conclusion follows that ratification and poor practices can go together. A rebuttal comes from measurement: Fariss (2014), discussed below, argued that the increasingly stringent standards applied by monitoring organizations may have masked real improvement.

Working it out

Problem 1. Free riding and the climate club. There are five countries. A country that cuts emissions pays a cost of 10, and each country's cut gives all five countries a benefit of 4. (a) If no other country cuts, what is the net payoff to a country that cuts alone? (b) If all five cut, what is each country's net payoff? (c) Suppose a club imposes penalty tariffs on the exports of any country that does not cut, so that a holdout loses t. If the other four countries all cut, what is the smallest value of t at which staying in the club leaves a country no worse off?

Problem 2. Did ratification reduce torture? One hundred countries are divided into 50 democracies and 50 authoritarian states. Among the democracies, torture is reported in 4 of the 40 that have ratified the Convention against Torture and in 1 of the 10 that have not. Among the authoritarian states, torture is reported in 14 of the 20 that have ratified and in 18 of the 30 that have not. (a) Overall, in what share of ratifiers, and of nonratifiers, is torture reported? (b) What happens when the countries are separated by regime type? (c) Why do the two results differ?

Problem 3. Placing cases in theoretical categories. Assign each of the following to one of these categories and justify your answer: (i) the tragedy of the commons; (ii) Ostrom's self-governing institutions; (iii) the boomerang pattern; (iv) a tactical concession in the spiral model; (v) a verifiable equipment standard. (a) Törbel's 1517 rule barring villagers from sending more cows to the summer pastures than they could feed through the winter; (b) factories that each dump wastewater into a river because the damage they themselves suffer from the dirtier river is smaller than the cost of treatment; (c) the 1978 international rules that, instead of capping discharges, required oil tankers to be fitted with segregated ballast tanks or crude-oil washing systems; (d) Eastern European dissident groups that, invoking the Helsinki Final Act, alerted Western groups and governments to rights abuses at home; (e) an authoritarian government under sanctions that frees some political prisoners while continuing to make new arrests.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. (a) A benefit of 4 minus a cost of 10 gives −6. Acting alone, no country has an incentive to cut.
  2. (b) Five cuts yield 5 × 4 = 20, minus the cost of 10, for a net payoff of 10. Everyone is better off if all cut, yet each does better by dropping out.
  3. (c) Staying in yields 10. A country that drops out alone receives the benefit of the other four cuts, 16, pays no abatement cost, and loses t, for 16 − t. Staying in requires 10 ≥ 16 − t, so t must be at least 6. At exactly 6 the two choices pay the same; above 6, staying in is better.

Problem 2.

  1. (a) Among ratifiers, 18 of 60 (4 + 14), or 30 percent; among nonratifiers, 19 of 40 (1 + 18), or 47.5 percent. Ratification appears to reduce torture.
  2. (b) Among democracies, the rate is 10 percent for ratifiers and nonratifiers alike; among authoritarian states, it is 70 percent for ratifiers and 60 percent for nonratifiers, so the ratifiers actually do worse.
  3. (c) The overall association is spurious: it arises because democracies, which torture less, also ratify more. Factors such as regime type that affect both ratification and practice must be taken into account; the same issue explains why Hathaway's results changed once she controlled for other factors. (The numbers are hypothetical.)

Problem 3.

  1. (a) (ii). Rules the users set for themselves capped use of the resource.
  2. (b) (i). This is the pollution version of the tragedy of the commons, in which the cost each polluter bears is smaller than the cost of treatment.
  3. (c) (v). Whether a ship had the equipment was easy to verify, which produced the high compliance Mitchell (1994) found, as discussed below.
  4. (d) (iii). Groups whose channels at home were blocked pressed their governments through international allies.
  5. (e) (iv). This is a concession made to ease pressure, not a sign that the norm has been accepted (this lecture's interpretation).

Evidence and counterarguments

Mitchell (1994): what produces compliance? Studying the international regime on intentional oil discharges from tankers, Ronald B. Mitchell found that a rule requiring tanker owners to install specified equipment produced far higher compliance than a rule requiring tanker operators to limit their discharges. Because both rules created strong economic incentives to cheat and applied to the same countries over the same period, the difference could be attributed to the design of the rules. The equipment rule increased transparency, made credible sanctions possible, lowered governments' costs of implementation by building on existing infrastructure, and prevented violations rather than merely deterring them. The limitation is that for a problem like greenhouse gases, whose sources run through every sector of the economy, such equipment standards are hard to find (this lecture's interpretation).

Hathaway (2002) and Fariss (2014): have human rights improved? Christopher J. Fariss argued that the apparent lack of improvement in existing indicators of human rights practices over the past 35 years reflects a rising standard of accountability: monitors such as Amnesty International and the U.S. State Department look harder for abuse, look in more places, and classify more acts as abuse. With a measurement model that accounts for this, he found that respect for human rights has improved over time and that ratification of the Convention against Torture is positively associated with respect for rights, contradicting earlier findings. The limitation is that changes in monitors' standards cannot be observed directly, so the results rest on the model's assumptions (this lecture's interpretation).

Barrett (2003) and Nordhaus (2015): broad and shallow treaties, or narrow and deep clubs? Barrett argued that what actually limits the depth of cooperation under a self-enforcing treaty is the difficulty of enforcing participation, and that when countries must choose between a "broad but shallow" treaty and a "narrow but deep" one, negotiations tend to favor the broad and shallow [secondary source]. Nordhaus calculated that without sanctions against nonparticipants, the only stable coalitions are those that cut emissions minimally, whereas a club that imposes small trade penalties can sustain a large coalition with deep cuts. Both see linking climate to trade as the answer, but penalty tariffs may clash with World Trade Organization rules and invite retaliation, and that problem remains unresolved (this lecture's interpretation).
