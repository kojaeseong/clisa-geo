The question for this session

War is costly for both sides. People die, property is destroyed, and no one can know the outcome in advance. Would two states not always do better to anticipate how a war would end and simply agree, before fighting, to divide whatever they are contesting in line with that outcome? This session starts from that question and asks why even states that calculate rationally fail to reach such a deal. One of those reasons—the commitment problem, in which rapid shifts in power make the other side's promises impossible to trust—offers a particularly useful framework for understanding U.S.-China relations in 2026.

1. Key concept: the bargaining range

Suppose two states are disputing territory worth 100. If they go to war, A wins with a probability of 60 percent, and each side pays a cost of 10. A's expected share from war is therefore 60 minus its cost of 10, or 50; B's is 40 minus 10, or 30.

Any division that gives A between 50 and 70, with B taking the rest, leaves both states at least as well off as war, and anything strictly inside that interval leaves both better off. Without fighting, A gets at least 50 and B at least 30. This interval is called the bargaining range. So long as neither state relishes risk the way a gambler does, a bargaining range always exists whenever war is costly. If war breaks out, then, it is not because no bargaining range existed but because the two states failed to reach an agreement within it.

2. Three reasons states fail to agree

The American political scientist James D. Fearon took on this problem directly in his 1995 article "Rationalist Explanations for War." He identified three reasons why rational states go to war even when a bargaining range exists.

The first is private information. Each state knows its own military strength and its resolve to fight better than its rival does, and each has an incentive to exaggerate both in order to get more at the table. The rival knows this, so it does not take words at face value. Simply disclosing the information might seem to solve the problem, but as long as there is an incentive to bluff, even disclosed information is hard to believe. The two sides end up misjudging each other's strength and using war to find out who is actually stronger.

The second is the commitment problem. International politics has no higher authority to enforce agreements. A deal struck today can be overturned tomorrow if power shifts, and the problem is most acute when one state's power is growing quickly. The rising state has no credible way to promise that it will honor today's agreement once it has grown stronger. The state whose relative power is declining may calculate that fighting while it still has the strength is better than accepting worse terms later. This is the logic of preventive war. Preemptive war, in which each side races to strike first because the first mover gains a large advantage, has the same structure.

The third is issue indivisibility. When the stakes are hard to divide, such as a holy site or a capital city, it is difficult to construct a division that falls within the bargaining range. Fearon nonetheless regarded this as a rarer cause than the first two, since side payments in some other form can often make up the difference.

In his 2006 article "War as a Commitment Problem," the American political scientist Robert Powell pushed the second reason further. He argued that preventive war, preemptive war, and conflicts over goods that alter future bargaining power all share the same structure, and that in each case a large, rapid shift in the distribution of power can lead to war. Indivisible issues, he argued, should themselves be understood as commitment problems. One distinction matters a great deal. Information problems can diminish as the two sides test each other, but the commitment problem persists even under complete information. Powell defined the commitment problem as the case in which two states fight even though each knows everything about the other.

3. Classics and theory: a 2,400-year-old diagnosis

The theory is new, but the diagnosis is old. Thucydides distinguished between the openly stated reasons for the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) and its truest cause, which he located in the growth of Athenian power and the fear it aroused in Sparta (History of the Peloponnesian War 1.23.6). Recast in Fearon's terms, a rising Athens had no credible way to promise that it would continue to respect Sparta's interests. That reading is this lecture's interpretation, not Fearon's.

In 2012, the American political scientist Graham Allison applied this diagnosis to U.S.-China relations and called it the Thucydides Trap. In a 2015 article in The Atlantic, he made public the case study from Harvard's Belfer Center on which it rested. The study counted 16 cases since the late fifteenth century in which a rising power confronted an established one; 12 ended in war. Allison warned that, on the current trajectory, a U.S.-China war in the coming decades is far more likely than is currently recognized and that, judging by the historical record, war is more likely than not. The claim has its critics. In 2021, Richard Hanania calculated that if the 14 cases the researchers had been considering for inclusion are added, only 19 of 30 ended in war, and that if the sample is limited to cases after 1950, only one of seven did. Even within the original 16, the two cases that arose after the advent of nuclear weapons (the United States and the Soviet Union; the United Kingdom and France versus reunified Germany) both passed without war.

Scholars and statesmen divide over what the diagnosis calls for.

  • John Mearsheimer expects a stronger China to seek dominance in Asia, much as the United States dominates the Western Hemisphere, and expects the United States and China's neighbors to join together to stop it. His argument rests on two conditions: an international system with no higher authority and the impossibility of being certain about another state's future intentions. Translated into the bargaining model, his view implies that because those conditions are themselves the problem, diplomacy can do little to solve the commitment problem (this lecture's interpretation).
  • Henry Kissinger held that a balance of power alone cannot stabilize an order unless the major powers agree on which diplomatic goals and methods are permissible. In the epilogue to On China (2011), he explored a "Pacific Community" that the United States and China would build together. Seen through the bargaining model, mutually accepted rules raise the price a rising power would pay for breaking an agreement once it has grown stronger, in lost reputation and severed institutional and economic ties, and so preserve its incentive to keep the deal even after its power has grown. That connection is this lecture's interpretation, not Kissinger's own formulation.
  • Lee Kuan Yew considered U.S.-China competition inevitable but conflict avoidable, and argued that smaller countries retain room to maneuver only if the United States remains the balancer in Asia.

4. Historical cases

The Peloponnesian War. Thucydides' diagnosis has its critics, too. In The Outbreak of the Peloponnesian War (1969), the American historian Donald Kagan argued that the war could have been avoided at several junctures and resulted from choices made by leaders in Corinth, Sparta, and Athens. In his view, Athenian power was not even growing on the eve of the war. Shifts in power and the fear they produce create the conditions for war, he held, but whether war begins depends on leaders' choices.

Germany, 1914. On December 8, 1912, the German emperor convened a meeting of his military leadership; the chancellor was not present. Admiral Georg von Müller, chief of the Naval Cabinet, recorded in his diary that the chief of the General Staff, Helmuth von Moltke the Younger, said, "I believe war is unavoidable, and the sooner the better." The remark is often cited as evidence that Germany's leaders were weighing the logic of preventive war, that it was better to fight before French and Russian military power grew further. Historians disagree, however, over whether the meeting was where war was decided. Fritz Fischer and others see it as a decisive step toward war, but Müller himself wrote in his diary that the meeting's result was "practically nil."

Cases that passed without war. The four cases in the Belfer Center's 16 that did not end in war include Britain and the United States in the early twentieth century. The transfer of primacy from a declining Britain to a rising United States took place without war, which shows that a shift in power does not automatically lead to conflict.

5. Applying it to 2026: U.S.-China relations

The facts. During President Donald Trump's state visit to China on May 13–15, 2026, the two governments agreed on the purchase of 200 Boeing aircraft and on permitting exports of Nvidia's H200 chips to China, and they described their relationship as one of constructive strategic stability. During President Xi Jinping's visit to the United States in September, the two sides extended by two months, to January 10, 2027, the one-year tariff truce they had concluded in Busan in October 2025, and they set up a new channel for dialogue on AI. Semiconductor export controls, however, were left off the agenda. The Chinese chipmaker CXMT's share of the DRAM market rose from 4 percent to 10 percent in a single year. A $14 billion arms sale to Taiwan, on hold since May, has no set date for approval, and Washington has floated arms sales to Taiwan as a bargaining chip. In late September, the Chinese navy and coast guard blocked a Philippine resupply mission bound for Second Thomas Shoal in the South China Sea.

Through the lens of the bargaining model. Reciprocal summits and new dialogue channels can reduce misunderstanding, but as the discussion of private information in section 2 showed, words alone do little to shrink the information problem as long as the incentive to bluff remains. What actually shrinks it is costly action: whether the Boeing purchase commitment and the H200 export licenses are in fact carried out. The commitment problem remains untouched. Semiconductors are the domain in which power is shifting fastest between the two countries, as CXMT's rising market share shows, and that is precisely the domain left out of the deal. The fact that a one-year truce was extended this time by only two months can be read as a sign of the same problem. If neither side can credibly commit to how it will behave once power has shifted, the remaining option is to keep renewing short agreements. The extension could also be explained by a desire to preserve bargaining leverage or by U.S. domestic politics, so this reading is this lecture's interpretation.

By Kissinger's standard, the spectacle of leaders performing coexistence resembles the outward form of a Pacific Community, but the agreement on permissible goals and methods is missing. By Lee Kuan Yew's standard, Washington's willingness to put Taiwan arms sales on the bargaining table and to impose tariffs on its own allies can be read as a sign of the inconstant America he warned against.

Assessment. In the near term, managed competition is likely to continue as the tariff truce is extended again and again. That, however, defers the commitment problem rather than solving it, and the shifts in power under way in the semiconductor race and in the military balance across the Taiwan Strait are not slowing. Coexistence is possible, but it will not come about on its own. It requires an agreement on permissible goals and methods and a United States that remains the balancer in Asia. This assessment should be revisited if the tariff truce is not extended in January 2027 and semiconductor controls are expanded, if the China Coast Guard actually begins inspecting vessels bound for Taiwan, or if the United States freezes arms sales to Taiwan.

What it means for South Korea. Samsung Electronics' plant in Xi'an and SK hynix's plants in Wuxi and Dalian are likely to keep receiving U.S. authorization to bring in equipment in 2027 (an 80 percent probability that this is confirmed by the end of January 2027). If relations break down, South Korea could be among the first to feel the pressure, along two paths. In one, China takes formal economic measures against South Korea over the Taiwan issue (15 percent by the end of 2027). In the other, South Korea is drawn into the deployment of U.S. Forces Korea (USFK) beyond the peninsula in a Taiwan contingency; the key question there is whether Seoul and Washington put a consultation procedure in writing (30 percent by the end of June 2027).

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows Fearon's argument in the original, works through the bargaining range and preventive war with numbers, and looks at research that has put the bargaining model and power transition theory to the test.

Reading the original

Passage 1. Why a bargaining range exists

"...there always exists a set of negotiated settlements that both sides prefer to fighting." (Fearon 1995, p. 387)

Fearon represents the issue in dispute as the interval from 0 to 1 and sets A's expected value for war at p − cA and B's at 1 − p − cB, where p is the probability that A wins and cA and cB are each side's costs of war. Any division that gives A between p − cA and p + cB is then at least as good as war for both states; the two endpoints are the reservation levels (p. 387). Because fighting shrinks the pie by the amount of those costs (war is inefficient ex post), dividing it in advance benefits both sides. The result rests on three assumptions: that both states know there is a true probability p of victory, that they are risk-averse or risk-neutral, and that any division between 0 and 1 is possible (pp. 388–389). Examining the 2003 Iraq War, David A. Lake criticized the model for leaving out the costs of governing after the war and the role of domestic interest groups (Lake 2010/11).

Passage 2. Incentives to misrepresent

"...incentives to misrepresent can explain why even rational leaders may be unable to avoid the miscalculations of relative will and power that can cause war." (Fearon 1995, p. 396)

Fearon located the cause of war not in a lack of information as such but in whatever keeps states from sharing it (pp. 390–391). A state that does not know its rival's resolve runs a greater risk of war the more it demands, but it gains more if the rival backs down, so even its best demand carries some chance of war (p. 394). Asking the other side whether it will fight reveals nothing as long as answering costs nothing (pp. 395–396). Only costly signals convey information, and even they do not remove the risk of war entirely (pp. 396–397). Erik Gartzke pushed this logic further, arguing that the outbreak of any particular war cannot be predicted in advance: "War is literally in the 'error term'" (Gartzke 1999, p. 567). Lake, by contrast, found that the information failures of both governments in the Iraq War arose not from the other side's bluffing but from self-delusion, and he called for a behavioral theory.

Working it out

Problem 1. Calculating the bargaining range. A and B are disputing a maritime area worth 200. If they go to war, A wins with a probability of 70 percent, and the winner takes everything. War costs A 30 and B 10. Find the bargaining range, and determine whether the current division, 100 apiece, falls within it.

Problem 2. When A does not know B's type. A makes a one-time demand for a share x of territory worth 100, and B either accepts or fights (B accepts if it is indifferent). A believes there is a 0.3 probability that B is the strong type. A wins a war against a strong B with a probability of 50 percent and against a weak B with a probability of 70 percent, and war costs each side 10. (a) Should A demand 60 or 80, and what is the probability of war? (b) If a weak B claims to be strong, should A believe it?

Problem 3. The declining state's calculation. An established power A and a rising power B split 100 today and another 100 tomorrow (the future is not discounted). If they fight today, A wins with a probability of 70 percent, and the winner takes both periods' shares, 200 in all. Tomorrow A's probability of winning falls to 30 percent, and a war fought tomorrow decides only tomorrow's 100. War costs each side 10 whenever it is fought, and tomorrow B will push A down to its reservation level. A chooses peace if it is indifferent. (a) Does A fight today even if B concedes all 100 today? (b) Suppose B concedes all 100 today and promises to give A y tomorrow. How large must y be for A to choose peace? Will tomorrow's B have any incentive to keep that promise?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. A's expected value for war is 0.7 × 200 − 30 = 110 and B's is 0.3 × 200 − 10 = 50, so the bargaining range consists of the divisions that give A between 110 and 150. Its width, 40, is the sum of the two sides' costs of war.
  2. The current division gives A 100, which lies outside the range, so A has an incentive to demand a new division.

Problem 2.

  1. (a) A strong B expects 0.5 × 100 − 10 = 40 from war, so it accepts any demand up to 60; a weak B expects 0.3 × 100 − 10 = 20, so it accepts up to 80. A demand of 60 gets A 60. A demand of 80 means fighting a strong B (A's value for that war is 40), for an expected 0.7 × 80 + 0.3 × 40 = 68. A should demand 80, and the probability of war is 30 percent.
  2. (b) If A believed the claim and demanded 60, a weak B would get 40 instead of 20. A weak B therefore has an incentive to claim it is strong as well, and such claims convey no information.

Problem 3.

  1. (a) Fighting today gives A an expected 0.7 × 200 − 10 = 130. Tomorrow A's reservation level is 0.3 × 100 − 10 = 20, so even receiving all 100 today brings it only 120. A therefore launches a preventive war today. In general, A fights if 200p₁ − 10 > 100 + (100p₂ − 10), that is, if p₁ > (1 + p₂)/2, where p₁ and p₂ are A's probabilities of winning today and tomorrow.
  2. (b) 100 + y ≥ 130 gives y ≥ 30. With y = 30, B also comes out ahead: it gets 0 today and 70 tomorrow, more than the 50 it would expect from fighting today (0.3 × 200 − 10). But tomorrow B will need to give A only 20, so it will have no incentive to give 30. A deal exists that would leave both states better off, yet because the promise cannot be trusted, they go to war. That is the commitment problem.

Evidence and counterarguments

Power transition theory is concerned with war at the moment one state overtakes another, whereas the commitment problem concerns the incentives of a declining state before it is overtaken, so the findings below cannot be carried over directly to the logic of Fearon and Powell (this lecture's interpretation).

Organski and Kugler (1980): overtaking leads to war. In The War Ledger, A. F. K. Organski and Jacek Kugler asked whether major wars follow when a challenger overtakes the dominant nation (the established power of the main text). Measuring national power by gross national product (GNP), they found that overtaking was significantly associated with war among the contenders, the states competing for dominance, but not among the great powers as a whole [secondary source]. In 1988, Henk Houweling and Jan G. Siccama reanalyzed the question with different data and tests and reported that the hypothesis could not be rejected for the great powers as a whole either. In 1997, Indra de Soysa and colleagues found support for the hypothesis but noted that the strength of the evidence depended on the measure of national power and the choice of cases.

Lebow and Valentino (2009): power transitions have been rare and mostly peaceful. Richard Ned Lebow and Benjamin Valentino reexamined the theory's propositions against the historical record (their data and classification criteria could not be confirmed). They concluded that a single state has rarely dominated the system, that power transitions have been rare and have mostly taken place without war, and that wars have often brought about power transitions rather than the other way around. This conclusion, too, may depend on what one counts as a power transition (this lecture's interpretation).

Reed (2003): information problems grow as power evens out. William Reed tested balance of power theory and power transition theory with an ultimatum game model (his data could not be confirmed). He found that the closer two states are in power, the greater the uncertainty about the minimum terms the other side would accept rather than fight (its reservation level), and the greater the likelihood of military conflict. Because the study uses the distribution of power as a stand-in for uncertainty, however, it is hard to tell whether that risk stems from the commitment problem (this lecture's interpretation).
