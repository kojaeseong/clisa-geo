The question for this session

This lecture treats 1648, 1815, 1919, and 1945 as the great turning points of the modern international system. Each of the new orders established in those years emerged from a major war that drew in several great powers; only the order of 1991 arrived without one. In 2026, with the distribution of power shifting once again, which path is the world on? This session organizes the history of the international system by polarity, that is, by the number of great powers. It then examines the debate over polarity and stability and the ways several major thinkers have understood order, and closes by comparing five world scenarios for the period through 2030 with the historical record.

1. Key concept: the international system and polarity

An international system consists of states that affect one another and the distribution of power among them. In the structural realism of Kenneth N. Waltz, introduced in session 1, every system shares the same basic condition, the absence of a higher authority, so the differences between systems come mainly from how power is distributed.

Polarity refers to the number of great powers in a system. A system with three or more great powers is multipolar, one with two is bipolar, and one dominated by a single power is unipolar. Because scholars count great powers by different measures, they can classify the same period differently; the period since 1991 is the clearest example.

To polarity this lecture adds a second axis, legitimacy, in the sense Henry Kissinger gave the term (see section 3): a minimal agreement among the major powers on the permissible aims and methods of foreign policy. The history that follows is read along these two axes, polarity and legitimacy (this lecture's interpretation).

2. Polarity and stability: a divided debate

Bipolarity is stable. In "The Stability of a Bipolar World" (1964), Waltz argued that the bipolar system of the Cold War was more stable than a multipolar one. In 1990, John J. Mearsheimer extended the same logic. In a multipolar system, he argued, there are more pairs of states that might come into conflict; imbalances of power are common, which makes deterrence harder; several states can more easily gang up on one; and because it is hard to gauge an adversary's resolve or the size of the coalitions that might form, miscalculation is frequent. He therefore warned that if NATO and the Warsaw Pact dissolved and U.S. and Soviet forces withdrew from Europe, a multipolar Europe would become unstable.

Multipolarity is stable. In 1964, the same year as Waltz's article, Karl W. Deutsch and J. David Singer argued the opposite: as a system moves from bipolarity toward multipolarity, the frequency and intensity of war decline.

Is unipolarity stable? In 1990, Charles Krauthammer called the post–Cold War world "the unipolar moment." In 1999, William C. Wohlforth challenged the conventional view that unipolarity is unstable, arguing that it is durable and peaceful and that its main threat is American disengagement. In 2011, Nuno Monteiro countered that the United States had been at war for 13 of the 22 years since the end of the Cold War.

The alliance dilemma. In 1984, Glenn H. Snyder argued that the dilemma allies face between the risk of being abandoned by a partner (abandonment) and the risk of being dragged into conflicts they do not want (entrapment) is more acute in a multipolar system, and he used Europe from 1904 to 1914 as his illustration.

The debate remains unsettled because the cases are few and because scholars do not even agree on how to count great powers. The Cold War is the only clear instance of bipolarity in the modern era, and it is hard to separate whether the peace of that period owed more to bipolarity or to nuclear weapons (this lecture's interpretation).

3. Classics and thinkers: where on the map does power lie?

Polarity theory counts great powers. The British geographer Halford Mackinder and the American political scientist Nicholas Spykman asked a different question: where on the map that power is located.

Mackinder. In "The Geographical Pivot of History," a paper delivered to the Royal Geographical Society in 1904, Mackinder argued that the "Columbian epoch," the 400 years in which Western Europe encircled Eurasia from the sea, was coming to an end. "From the present time forth, in the post-Columbian age, we shall again have to deal with a closed political system," he wrote (p. 422). The pivot area was the interior of Eurasia, inaccessible to ships, and transcontinental railways were giving the land power located there a new mobility; Russia, he wrote, "occupies the central strategical position held by Germany in Europe." He warned that if the pivot state upset the balance of power, expanded over the marginal lands of Eurasia, and used the continent's resources to build fleets, "the empire of the world would then be in sight" (p. 436), and he named an alliance between Germany and Russia as the development that might bring this about. In 1919 he renamed the pivot area the Heartland and argued that whoever rules Eastern Europe commands the Heartland, and whoever rules the Heartland commands the World-Island (Eurasia and Africa). The 1919 proposition seemed to be borne out when the Soviet Union, having taken control of Eastern Europe, became a superpower. Yet the Soviet Union never dominated the World-Island, and it dissolved in 1991.

Spykman. Spykman accepted Mackinder's map but reversed his conclusion. What mattered most, he argued, was not the Heartland but the rimland that surrounds it, a belt running from the coastlands of Europe through Arabia and the Middle East to the monsoon lands of India and East Asia. In The Geography of the Peace (1944), he wrote: "Who controls the rimland rules Eurasia; who rules Eurasia controls the destinies of the world" (p. 43). The United States, in his view, therefore had to engage in the balance of power to keep the rimlands at both ends of Eurasia from falling to a single hostile state or coalition. This reasoning is often described as the geographical blueprint for the Cold War policy of containment.

Kissinger. In A World Restored (1957), Kissinger analyzed the settlement that followed the Napoleonic Wars and built a framework in which order arises from the combination of a balance of power and legitimacy. "'Legitimacy' as here used should not be confused with justice," he wrote; it meant nothing more than an international agreement about the nature of workable arrangements and about the permissible aims and methods of foreign policy [secondary source]. His conclusion was that once a great power appears that rejects this agreement and seeks to overturn the order, the balance of power alone cannot preserve the peace. In World Order (2014), he argued that in a world where regions hold different conceptions of legitimacy, regional orders must be built first and then linked to one another [secondary source].

4. Historical cases

1648: Westphalia and the "Westphalian myth." The Peace of Westphalia, which ended the Thirty Years' War in 1648, is commonly treated as the starting point of the system of sovereign states. In a 2001 article, Andreas Osiander called this conventional account the "Westphalian myth." His argument is that much of the past against which international relations scholarship measures the present is an invention produced by a nineteenth- and twentieth-century fixation on the concept of sovereignty. The treaties contain no provision establishing sovereignty as a principle governing relations between states; the Holy Roman Empire continued to exist afterward; and France and Sweden, as guarantors of the empire's constitutional order, gained a right to intervene in it [secondary source].

1815: The Vienna settlement and the Concert of Europe. After the Congress of Vienna (1814–1815), Britain, Austria, Prussia, and Russia formed the Quadruple Alliance (1815). When France joined in 1818, the Concert of Europe took shape, a system in which five great powers managed crises by meeting in conference. It was a multipolar order whose great powers shared the goal of preserving the status quo—in Kissinger's terms, a balance of power reinforced by legitimacy. For roughly 40 years afterward there was no war among the great powers, but the Crimean War (1853–1856), in which Britain and France fought Russia, and the wars of Italian and German unification (1859–1871) left the Concert badly weakened [secondary source].

1871: German unification and the hardening of alliance blocs. In 1871, after three wars, Prussia founded the German Empire. The Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy (1882) was followed by the Franco-Russian Alliance (1894), the Entente Cordiale between Britain and France (1904), and the Anglo-Russian Convention (1907), and Europe hardened into two camps [secondary source]. The system remained multipolar, but the system now had a structure in which a crisis involving one state could spread to an entire camp (this lecture's interpretation).

Nineteenth-century East Asia: from the tributary order to the treaty system. For centuries, East Asian relations had been organized as a tributary order centered on China, a hierarchy in which neighboring rulers sent tribute missions to the Chinese emperor in return for recognition. That order collapsed in the late nineteenth century. Article 1 of the Treaty of Ganghwa (the Japan–Korea Treaty of 1876), defined Joseon (Korea's last dynasty) as "an independent state" (jaju jibang, 自主之邦, in the classical Chinese text) enjoying the same sovereign rights as Japan. In Article 1 of the Treaty of Shimonoseki, which ended the First Sino-Japanese War in 1895, Qing China recognized Korea's full independence, and with it Joseon's tributary rites came to an end [secondary source]. Deals among the great powers, such as the Taft–Katsura agreement of 1905, shifted their costs onto the Korean Empire (the name Joseon had adopted in 1897), which had no seat at the table. The system of sovereign states arrived in East Asia from outside, imposed alongside unequal treaties (this lecture's interpretation).

1914–1945: The collapse of the system. After World War I, the United States did not join the League of Nations. Through the Locarno Treaties of 1925, Germany accepted its western borders and joined the League the following year, but its eastern borders were left without a guarantee [secondary source]. In defining legitimacy, Kissinger cited Germany after the Treaty of Versailles as an example of a great power so dissatisfied that it expressed its grievances in a revolutionary foreign policy [secondary source].

1945–1991: Bipolarity and the "Long Peace." After World War II, two superpowers remained, the United States and the Soviet Union. In 1986, the historian John Lewis Gaddis called this period, in which the two never fought each other directly, the "Long Peace" [secondary source]. Yet major wars were fought in the rimland, among them the Korean War and the Vietnam War. The stability of bipolarity was stability between the superpowers (this lecture's interpretation).

After 1991: Unipolarity and what followed. With the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991, the system shifted from bipolarity to unipolarity without a major war. In 2023, Stephen G. Brooks and Wohlforth argued that although the United States had become less dominant over the previous 20 years, it remained at the top of the global hierarchy of power, and they called multipolarity a myth [secondary source]. Others counter that the world is already bipolar, divided between the United States and China, or that it is becoming multipolar [secondary source].

Through the lens of theory. One of the structural trends the site identifies, U.S. offshore balancing combined with allied self-reliance, points in the same direction as American disengagement, which Wohlforth saw as the main threat to unipolarity (this lecture's interpretation). None of the five scenarios, however, includes all-out war among the great powers, the event that produced most of the transitions in history. The implicit view is that if the system changes before 2030, the change will come either through an internal fracture in one pole, as in 1991, or through a diffusion of power as allies strengthen themselves (this lecture's interpretation).

What it means for South Korea. The site assigns weights to the four factors that drive South Korea's grand strategy: 40 for geography, 25 for national power, 15 for regime and domestic politics, and 20 for historical memory. It explains that geography—a peninsula where continental and maritime powers meet—has always forced the country to choose among great powers. South Korea sits on what Spykman called the rimland. Managed competition is the most favorable scenario for South Korea economically, but direct dealings between Washington and Pyongyang could push Seoul to the sidelines of talks on the Korean Peninsula. Armed multipolarity would give South Korea an opportunity to widen its security autonomy but would also carry costs. By Snyder's logic, the more multipolar the system becomes, the sharper the dilemma of abandonment and entrapment South Korea faces within its alliance (this lecture's interpretation).

Assessment. The two most likely paths through 2030 are managed competition and armed multipolarity, and what separates them is whether the United States sustains its commitment to its allies. This assessment should be revisited if signals of armed multipolarity become reality, such as an announced reduction of U.S. Forces Korea (USFK) or a formal revision of Japan's three non-nuclear principles, or if the U.S.-China tariff truce is neither extended again nor replaced by a comprehensive agreement before it expires on January 10, 2027 (this lecture's interpretation). The site puts the probability of such an extension or replacement at 75 percent (resolution date January 31, 2027).

  2. Give three reasons Mearsheimer considered a multipolar system more dangerous than a bipolar one. How did Deutsch and Singer argue the opposite?
  3. What is the "Westphalian myth," and why did Osiander call it a myth?
  4. How does legitimacy, in Kissinger's sense, differ from justice? Use that standard to compare the Vienna settlement with the order after 1919.

Discussion questions

  1. If the world moves toward armed multipolarity, will it become as dangerous as Europe in 1871–1914, or will it stabilize, as Deutsch and Singer would predict? How do nuclear weapons, and hedging—middle powers lining up issue by issue rather than joining one camp—change the answer?
  2. Was the unipolarity that followed 1991 peaceful? Whose argument, Wohlforth's or Monteiro's, better fits the record since then?
  3. For South Korea, a peninsula on the rimland, which is more favorable: managed competition, which comes close to bipolarity, or armed multipolarity?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows Wohlforth's case for a stable unipolar world in the original, uses capability shares to work out polarity and concentration, and looks at research that has tested the link between polarity and war.

Reading the original

Passage 1. A system without hegemonic rivalry

"an important source of conflict in previous systems is absent: hegemonic rivalry over leadership of the international system." (Wohlforth 1999, p. 7)

The argument rests on two premises. The first is that hegemonic rivalry was a major cause of great-power wars in the past. The second is that American superiority extends across every component of power, so the system is "unambiguously unipolar" (p. 7). With the United States set at 100, China's 1997 GDP at purchasing power parity stood at 53, and Russia's 1996 military spending at 26 (p. 12). No great power, therefore, will challenge the United States, and security competition among the other great powers will also subside; unipolarity, Wohlforth concludes, is peaceful (p. 7). The argument draws on an assumption of hegemonic theory: conflict is most likely when the gap between the leading state and a challenger is narrow (pp. 23–24). Monteiro faulted the argument for looking only at relations among great powers (Monteiro 2011, p. 37). In his account, wars recur between the United States and minor powers that refuse to accommodate it and seek to secure their survival with nuclear weapons (pp. 26, 37).

Passage 2. Geography sustains unipolarity

"Efforts to produce a counterbalance globally will generate powerful countervailing action locally." (Wohlforth 1999, p. 28)

The premise is geographic: the United States is an offshore power in North America, while every potential challenger lies in or around Eurasia (p. 28). States attend to the balance with their neighbors before the balance with a distant United States, so when a Eurasian state tries to grow strong enough to take on the United States, its neighbors move to check it first. The concentration of power needed to sustain unipolarity is therefore "lower than most scholars assume" (p. 28). A year later, Waltz countered that unipolarity is "the least durable of international configurations": a dominant power takes on too many tasks beyond its own borders, and weaker states, worried about its future behavior, seek to balance against it (Waltz 2000, pp. 27–28).

Problem 2. The distribution of power and alliance choices. States A, B, and C have capabilities of 50, 30, and 20. Joining the weaker side is balancing; joining the stronger side is bandwagoning. (a) Find the capabilities of the two camps if C balances and if C bandwagons. (b) Ten years later, the figures are A 60, B 25, and C 15. B and C, already allied, try to match A through internal balancing alone, each building up its own military, for example. By what percentage must their combined capabilities grow from that point? (c) How might C's choice change if A lies far away across the ocean and B is C's neighbor?

Problem 3. The polarity of power and the polarity of alliances. Frank Whelon Wayman measured the distribution of capabilities (power polarity) separately from the number of alliance blocs (alliance polarity). When power was multipolar, three-quarters of the wars that broke out were large; when it was bipolar, wars were small. In the twentieth century, war became more likely when alliances split into two camps, and peace held when they were divided among many groupings; in the nineteenth century, the alliance findings ran the other way. Place each case below in a two-by-two table (power bipolar or multipolar × alliances bipolar or multipolar), and predict the likelihood and scale of war. (a) Europe, 1907–1914: several great powers divided between the Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente. (b) 1955–1965: NATO and the Warsaw Pact facing off amid the U.S.-Soviet rivalry. (c) Armed multipolarity, from section 5: several great powers, with middle powers forming minilateral pacts issue by issue.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. System X: Only A has a share of 0.30 or more, so the system is unipolar. Σsᵢ² = 0.36 + 4 × 0.01 = 0.40, and CON = √(0.20 / 0.80) = 0.50.
  2. System Y: A and B both have shares of 0.225 or more, so the system is bipolar. Σsᵢ² = 0.405 + 0.0016 + 0.0018 = 0.4084, and CON = √(0.2084 / 0.80) ≈ 0.51.
  3. System Z: All five states are equal, so the system is multipolar. Σsᵢ² = 0.20, so CON = 0.
  4. X and Y differ in polarity, yet their CON scores are nearly identical. CON shows only how unevenly capabilities are distributed; it cannot tell whether they are concentrated in one state or split between two. That is probably why Wayman separately measured the share held by the top two powers (TWOCON) (this lecture's interpretation). The top two shares are 0.70 in X and 0.90 in Y.

Problem 2.

  1. (a) Balancing: B + C at 50 against A at 50, an even match. Bandwagoning: A + C at 70 against B at 30.
  2. (b) B + C total 40, so they need another 20, an increase of 50 percent. In practice A keeps growing as well, so they will also look to external balancing, bringing in states from outside the alliance.
  3. (c) The more immediate threat to C may be its neighbor B, in which case C could align with distant A to check B. Measured by capabilities alone, that is bandwagoning; measured by the nearer threat, it is balancing. This is the local countervailing action of passage 2 (this lecture's interpretation).

Problem 3. The placement of the cases is this lecture's interpretation.

  1. (a) Power multipolar, alliances bipolar. This is the cell in which wars tend to be frequent and large, which fits World War I.
  2. (b) Power bipolar, alliances bipolar. The likelihood of war is high, but wars should be small.
  3. (c) Power multipolar, alliances multipolar. By the twentieth-century findings, peace should hold, but a war that did break out could be large. The combination Wayman considered stable if the twentieth-century findings are correct, power bipolar with alliances multipolar, matches none of the cases (p. 76).

Evidence and counterarguments

Singer, Bremer, and Stuckey (1972): the answer depends on the century. Singer, Stuart Bremer, and John Stuckey asked whether the concentration of capabilities among the great powers shapes the amount of great-power war. Comparing data from 1820 to 1965, they found that in the nineteenth century a more even distribution of capabilities went with greater stability, while in the twentieth century the reverse held [secondary source]. The study could not explain why the results reversed from one period to the next (this lecture's interpretation).

Wayman (1984): power and alliances work differently. Wayman divided the years 1816–1965 into five-year periods and compared capability concentration and an index of alliance blocs with the magnitude of war in the following five years, measured as the sum of nation-months of war across the participants (pp. 68–69); his results are summarized in problem 3 (pp. 71–72). The two indexes were barely correlated with each other (p. 70), which supports the premise that they need to be measured separately. Yet none of the correlations explained even half of the variation in the magnitude of war, and Wayman regarded his results as no more than suggestive of a causal relationship (pp. 75–76).

Mansfield (1992) and Ray and Bentley (2010): the relationship does not run in one direction. Edward D. Mansfield tested concentration by breaking it into the number of great powers and the inequality among them. Concentration was related to the frequency of war, but the relationship took the shape of an inverted U, with war most frequent at intermediate levels of concentration—a pattern that neither balance-of-power theory nor power preponderance theory predicted. James Lee Ray and Patrick Bentley criticized nearly 40 years of research for inconsistent findings and a weak theoretical rationale, but concluded that the problem did not lie in the CON measure itself.
