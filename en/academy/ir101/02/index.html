The question for this session

Arms races sometimes break out even when neither side wants war. One state adds weapons for its own defense; the other reads this as a threat and arms in response; both end up less secure than before. This session asks where that structure comes from, and under what conditions states manage to cooperate within it. It offers a framework for understanding the arms race on the Korean Peninsula and the gap in deterrence across the Taiwan Strait in 2026.

1. Key concept: anarchy and the security dilemma

As session 1 showed, anarchy does not mean chaos. It means that no higher authority stands above states to enforce their promises. Each state must therefore look after its own security.

The problem this condition creates is known as the security dilemma. The term was coined by John H. Herz, a German-born American scholar, in a 1950 article. Herz argued that groups living side by side without a higher authority seek more power in order to escape the power of others; this makes the others more insecure and drives them to prepare for the worst. The pursuit of security and the accumulation of power then lock into a vicious circle.

In his 1978 article "Cooperation Under the Security Dilemma," the American political scientist Robert Jervis put the point this way: many of the means a state uses to increase its own security decrease the security of others. The spiral requires no malice. As Jervis observed, even states satisfied with the status quo can find it hard to reach goals they recognize as being in their common interest, because cooperating when the other side does not can be disastrous.

2. When cooperation is hard and when it is possible

Game theory helps clarify the problem. Suppose two states each choose between cooperating (restraining their arms) and defecting (building them up), and rank the possible outcomes by preference, with 4 the best.

In the prisoner's dilemma, defecting pays no matter what the other side does. If the other side cooperates, defection earns a 4; if it defects, defection is the only way to avoid the worst outcome, a 1. The result is mutual defection (2, 2), which leaves both worse off than mutual cooperation (3, 3).

The stag hunt takes its name from an analogy by Jean-Jacques Rousseau. If the hunters work together, they bring down a stag and everyone eats well; if one of them abandons his post to chase a hare, the rest go home empty-handed. Jervis opened his article with this analogy, likening the outcome in which everyone chases hares to an arms race and a high risk of war. Here mutual cooperation is the best outcome, so cooperating is the best choice as long as each side can trust the other to cooperate. The problem is not greed but trust.

Jervis framed the conditions that make cooperation easier in terms of the payoff cells: raise the gains from mutual cooperation and the costs of mutual defection, reduce the losses from cooperating alone and being exploited and the gains from exploiting the other side, and strengthen each side's expectation that the other will cooperate.

Repeated games and the shadow of the future. In The Evolution of Cooperation (1984), the American political scientist Robert Axelrod showed that the prisoner's dilemma changes when the same players meet again and again, in what is called a repeated game. Both of the computer tournaments he organized were won by the simplest strategy entered, tit for tat, submitted by Anatol Rapoport: cooperate on the first move, then copy whatever the other player did on the previous move. The condition is that the two states must be likely enough to meet again—that is, the future must cast a long enough shadow over the present (the shadow of the future). In a 1985 article written with Robert O. Keohane, however, Axelrod noted that this shadow is weaker in security affairs than in economic ones, because in security a successful first strike can eliminate the other side's very capacity to retaliate.

3. Jervis: the offense-defense balance and distinguishability

Jervis identified two variables that determine how severe the security dilemma is. The first is whether defensive weapons and policies can be told apart from offensive ones (distinguishability). The second is whether offense or defense has the advantage (the offense-defense balance). He named technology and geography—obstacles such as seas, large rivers, and mountain ranges—as the main factors shaping that balance. Crossing the two variables yields four worlds.

Nuclear weapons twist this framework. Jervis saw the impossibility of defense in the nuclear age as a victory not for offense but for deterrence. If both sides possess a retaliatory force that can survive a surprise attack (second-strike capability), both can be secure at once. That is why, he argued, in the context of deterrence, weapons that provide defense—missile defenses protecting cities, for example—become offensive weapons, because they threaten to neutralize the other side's capacity to retaliate.

4. A classic: Thucydides' Melian Dialogue

In the summer of 416 BC, Athens sent forces against Melos, an island colony of Sparta. Melos had at first stayed neutral, but after Athens plundered its territory it took an openly hostile stance toward Athens (5.84). In Book 5, sections 84–116, of the History of the Peloponnesian War, Thucydides narrates the expedition and records at length the exchange between the Athenian envoys, who demanded surrender, and the Melian representatives. The envoys held that justice is at issue only between equals in power, and that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" (5.89, trans. Richard Crawley). The reason they gave was security: if an island that kept its independence were left alone, Athens' subjects would take it as a sign of Athenian weakness, and only by subduing it could the empire be safe. The Melians appealed to the principle of fairness and to the hope of Spartan help; the Athenians replied that Sparta would not come. Melos surrendered that winter, and Athens executed the adult men and sold the women and children into slavery (5.116).

The line about the strong and the weak is often quoted as Thucydides' own conclusion, but the words belong to the Athenian envoys. Immediately after the dialogue, Thucydides narrates the Sicilian expedition (415–413 BC), in which the Athenian force was annihilated. In The Tragic Vision of Politics (2003), Richard Ned Lebow read beneath this sequence a lesson: the language of power ultimately brings down the strong themselves.

In the dialogue, no higher authority existed to hear the weaker side's appeal to justice, and even powerful Athens acted out of fear that appearing weak would be dangerous. The security dilemma is not a problem for the weak alone (this lecture's interpretation).

5. Historical cases

The Anglo-German naval race before World War I. Under the leadership of Alfred von Tirpitz, state secretary of the Imperial Naval Office, Germany passed Navy Laws in 1898 and 1900 that sharply expanded its battleship fleet. The calculation was that a fleet large enough to leave Britain weakened against the other great powers even if Britain won a battle would make London reluctant to confront Germany. In February 1912, negotiations in Berlin led by Richard Haldane, the British secretary of state for war, broke down when Germany demanded a British pledge of neutrality in return for acknowledging British naval superiority. At the end of July 1914, Britain had 29 capital ships to Germany's 18 [secondary source]. Jervis also discusses this case. Britain objected that the German fleet could only be an offensive weapon aimed at itself. Jervis judged the British position half correct, since Germany's navy was indeed an anti-British instrument, but noted that the British often overlooked that, whatever their intentions, the Royal Navy was likewise an important instrument of coercion against Germany (p. 170). He judged that pre-1914 Europe bore no small resemblance to the doubly dangerous world, cell ① of his four worlds (p. 211).

The Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty in the Cold War. Signed in Moscow on May 26, 1972, by U.S. President Richard Nixon and Leonid Brezhnev, general secretary of the Soviet Communist Party, the treaty limited each side to two missile-defense sites (one at its capital and one at an intercontinental ballistic missile base); a 1974 protocol reduced the number to one. The paradox that a treaty restricting defense could contribute to stability is resolved by the deterrence logic in section 3: a defensive shield able to stop retaliatory missiles looks to the other side like preparation to neutralize its capacity to retaliate. Jervis, too, cited this concern as one of the rationales for the ABM Treaty. The United States withdrew from the treaty in June 2002 [secondary source].

6. Applying it to 2026: the Korean Peninsula and the Taiwan Strait

The facts. In November 2025, the United States formally endorsed South Korea's pursuit of uranium enrichment, reprocessing, and nuclear-powered submarines; North Korea denounced the move as granting South Korea recognition as a quasi-nuclear state. The South Korean government drew up a basic plan for nuclear-powered submarines in May 2026, and on September 29 the Cabinet approved a special bill on nuclear-powered submarines, which the government aims to enact by the end of the year. North Korea unveiled a nuclear-powered submarine in December 2025 (amid suspicions of Russian technology transfer); in June 2026, Kim Jong Un ordered an exponential expansion of nuclear weapons production; and on October 3, North Korea fired a ballistic missile that it claimed was a hypersonic intermediate-range missile. In August, Japan's prime minister said Japan maintains its three non-nuclear principles but left out the phrase pledging to uphold them; in September, Japan's defense minister said acquiring nuclear-powered submarines had not been ruled out. On May 8, 2026, Taiwan's Legislative Yuan cut the government's request for a special defense budget from NT$1.25 trillion to NT$780 billion, stripping out funding for about 200,000 domestically built drones, air defenses, and the mass production of interceptor missiles.

Through the lens of the security dilemma. Clisa Geopolitics sees the nuclear domino—North Korea's nuclear buildup, South Korea's nuclear latency, and Japan's preparations, each justifying the others—as a security dilemma that no one wants. Applied to this case, Jervis's framework makes enrichment and reprocessing facilities a textbook example of low distinguishability (this lecture's interpretation). Because the same facility can produce both reactor fuel and weapons material, other states prepare against capability rather than intent. Clisa Geopolitics estimates that over the five to 10 years it takes for such facilities to come online, the time between a decision to build nuclear weapons and their completion would shrink from years to months. It therefore holds that South Korea should not race to acquire nuclear latency on its own but should use it as leverage and insurance in negotiating a phased freeze: linked step by step to whether North Korea freezes (the way NATO's 1979 Double-Track Decision led to the 1987 Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty), pursued within a joint South Korea–U.S. framework, coordinated in advance with Japan, and accompanied by expanded inspections. Nuclear-powered submarines cut both ways, depending on their mission. Jervis regarded submarine-launched ballistic missiles kept for retaliation as defensive weapons, and the ability to track an adversary's submarines as a threat to that adversary's retaliatory capacity (pp. 207–208). In Jervis's and Axelrod's terms, expanded inspections are a device for raising distinguishability, and step-by-step linkage, like tit for tat, casts the shadow of the future over the other side (this lecture's interpretation).

The Taiwan Strait presents a different picture. The drones, air defenses, and interceptor missiles that Taiwan sought to expand are highly useful for repelling an amphibious landing from across the sea and of little use for attacking the mainland. On Jervis's two variables, they are distinguishable and favor defense—an investment in the direction of cell ④, the doubly safe world (this lecture's interpretation). Yet these were precisely the items the Legislative Yuan cut, and Clisa Geopolitics warns against a structure in which the gap in deterrence rewards a China inclined to wait for the 2028 elections.

Assessment. On the Korean Peninsula, capabilities whose offensive and defensive uses are hard to tell apart are growing on one side (North Korea's hypersonic missiles) and being pursued on the other (South Korea's enrichment and reprocessing facilities), while channels for repeated contact, such as military communication lines, remain cut. This marks a drift toward the top row of Jervis's table, where offense and defense cannot be distinguished (this lecture's interpretation). The conditions for cooperation have to be built through transparency that raises distinguishability and through arrangements that link each step to the other side's behavior. This assessment should be revisited if inter-Korean military communication lines are restored this year (that is, if the third row of the forecast table resolves no), if North Korea accepts a verified freeze on nuclear testing and fissile material production, or if South Korea formally adopts a phased plan that ties its pursuit of enrichment and reprocessing to expanded inspections and to whether North Korea freezes.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It traces Jervis's argument in the original, works through the games with numbers, and looks at research that has put the theory to the test.

Reading the original

Passage 1. Offensive advantage and the risk of war

"When the offense has the advantage, a state's reaction to international tension will increase the chances of war." (Jervis 1978, p. 188)

The argument rests on two premises. The defense has the advantage when it is easier to protect and hold territory than to advance and seize it (p. 187); and because states cannot know one another's intentions, they prepare against capabilities. When the defense has the advantage, a state can offset an adversary's buildup with a smaller buildup of its own, so arms races level off at rough parity (p. 188), and even in a crisis there is little reason to strike first (p. 190). When the offense has the advantage, the steps a state takes to make itself more secure become threats to others, and the incentives for preemption and expansion grow (pp. 188–189). The sentence quoted above is the conclusion.

The argument depends on the assumption that states act on the balance they believe in (this lecture's interpretation). Jervis himself wrote that before World War I the powers believed the offense had the advantage when in fact the defense did (pp. 190–191). Criticism has focused on the charge that the balance cannot be defined or measured. In 1998, Charles L. Glaser and Chaim Kaufmann defined it as the ratio of the cost of the offensive forces needed to take territory to the cost of the defender's forces, and argued that the criticism was overstated.

Passage 2. The power of distinguishability

"A differentiation between offensive and defensive stances comes close to abolishing it." (Jervis 1978, p. 199)

The "it" is the security dilemma. The premise is that the dilemma is rooted in the fact that the weapons and policies a state uses to protect itself also give it the capability to attack (p. 199). If the two can be told apart, status quo states can show their intentions by relying on defensive means alone, while a state that piles up offensive means reveals its own. Hence the conclusion: a defensive advantage can only ease the dilemma, but distinguishability nearly eliminates it. That conclusion assumes the character of a weapon can be read from the outside. The Spanish statesman Salvador de Madariaga objected that whether a weapon is offensive or defensive depends on "which end of it you are looking at." Jervis replied that even without a sharp dividing line, one can still compare how much a weapon lends itself to one use rather than the other: weapons that cannot move at all, and forces that fight well only on their own soil, are defensive, while weapons of limited mobility are ambiguous (pp. 201, 203–204).

Working it out

Problem 1. Finding the equilibria. A Nash equilibrium is a cell in which neither state can do better by changing its choice on its own. The payoffs for cooperating (restraining arms) and defecting (building them up), listed as (A, B), are cooperate–cooperate (4, 4), cooperate–defect (0, x), defect–cooperate (x, 0), and defect–defect (1, 1). (a) Find every pure-strategy Nash equilibrium when x = 3 and when x = 5. (b) Above what value of x does the character of the game change?

Problem 2. When grim trigger sustains cooperation. The x = 5 game from problem 1 is repeated indefinitely. Both states play a grim trigger strategy: cooperate at first, but defect forever once the other side has defected even once. Payoffs in the next round are multiplied by a discount factor δ (between 0 and 1), those in the round after that by δ², and so on; the larger δ is, the more weight a state places on the future. The payoffs are T (the temptation to defect alone, 5), R (the reward for mutual cooperation, 4), P (the punishment for mutual defection, 1), and S (the sucker's payoff for cooperating alone, 0). (a) Find the condition on δ under which cooperation holds. (b) What changes if the gains from a first strike grow, so that T rises to 7? (c) What changes if mutual defection becomes more costly, so that P falls to 0.5? For the game to remain a prisoner's dilemma, P must be greater than S.

Problem 3. Placing cases in Jervis's table. Place each of the following cases in the 2×2 table from section 3 and justify your answer. (a) The Washington Naval Treaty of 1922: Japan accepted a capital-ship fleet three-fifths the size of America's, and the United States agreed not to fortify islands in the Pacific, which made it harder for the United States to fight in waters near Japan. (b) The London Naval Conference of 1930: Japan sought heavy-cruiser strength equal to 70 percent of America's, but the United States insisted on 60 percent; Japan's spokesman at the conference said that as long as the United States held that 10 percent advantage, it would be able to attack Japan.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. x = 3: If the other side cooperates, cooperating is better (4 > 3); if it defects, defecting is better (1 > 0). There are two equilibria, (cooperate, cooperate) and (defect, defect), so the game is a stag hunt.
  2. x = 5: Defecting is better whatever the other side does (5 > 4, 1 > 0). Defection is a dominant strategy, and the only equilibrium is (defect, defect), so the game is a prisoner's dilemma.
  3. (b) Compare 4 with x for the case in which the other side cooperates. If x is greater than 4, the game is a prisoner's dilemma; if x lies between 1 and 4, its rankings match those of the stag hunt in the main text.

Problem 2.

  1. Cooperating in every round yields R + δR + δ²R + … = R/(1−δ).
  2. Defecting yields T in the current round and then, as punishment, P in every round after that: T + δP/(1−δ). Because P > S, defecting is also the best response for the side being punished.
  3. Multiplying both sides of R/(1−δ) ≥ T + δP/(1−δ) by (1−δ) gives R ≥ T(1−δ) + δP, which rearranges to δ ≥ (T−R)/(T−P).
  4. (a) δ ≥ (5−4)/(5−1) = 0.25.
  5. (b) δ ≥ (7−4)/(7−1) = 0.5. In security affairs the temptation to strike first is large, and, as Axelrod and Keohane pointed out, the shadow of the future (δ) is also short (this lecture's interpretation).
  6. (c) δ ≥ (5−4)/(5−0.5) ≈ 0.22. The costlier mutual defection is, the easier cooperation becomes. If P falls below S, the game turns into chicken, and the formula no longer applies.

Problem 3.

  1. (a) Attacking across the sea became harder, so the defense had the advantage (p. 196). Judged by type alone, battleships serve both offense and defense, which puts the case in cell ② (defense advantage, not distinguishable); judged by use, under Jervis's criterion that forces that fight well only on their own soil are defensive (p. 204), it belongs in cell ④ (defense advantage, distinguishable) (this lecture's interpretation).
  2. (b) Cruisers cannot be told apart by type, which puts the case in the top row (not distinguishable). Jervis noted, however, that numbers beyond what defense requires are read as a sign of offensive intent (p. 200). Under the conditions described in (a), the defense had the advantage, so the case comes closest to cell ② (this lecture's interpretation).

Evidence and counterarguments

Wallace and Diehl test whether arms races raise the risk of war; Lieber tests the offense-defense balance.

Wallace (1979): arms races signal escalation. Michael D. Wallace asked whether serious disputes between great powers that followed an arms race were more likely to escalate to war. He identified arms races with an index formed by multiplying the two states' growth rates in military spending before a dispute. Of 28 disputes preceded by an arms race, 23 escalated to war; of 71 disputes without one, three did. Critics have questioned his classification and the construction of the index (Diehl 1983) and his handling of pairs of states drawn into the world wars (Sample 1997).

Diehl (1983): no clear relationship. Paul F. Diehl revised Wallace's classification and index and retested the relationship on serious great-power disputes from 1816 to 1970, drawn from the Correlates of War project. Of the disputes that followed mutual military buildups, 25 percent escalated to war, and nearly 77 percent of wars followed periods without an arms race. In 1997, Susan G. Sample reported that the positive relationship remained significant even after she dropped the contested pairs of states from the world wars and changed the measures and sample, and that many arms races classified as non-escalating led to war within five years. Her findings exposed the limits of looking only at escalation immediately after a dispute.

Lieber (2000): perceptions of the balance did not drive war. Keir A. Lieber tested the technological claims of offense-defense theory against four watershed technologies since the mid-19th century: railroads, the revolution in artillery and small arms, tanks, and nuclear weapons. He concluded that although technology sometimes favored the offense or the defense, perceptions of the balance had little effect on the likelihood of war. His challenge targets the perceptual link in Jervis's argument; its limitation is that it rests on a single factor, technology, and on four cases (this lecture's interpretation). By contrast, in a 2003/04 article Karen Ruth Adams reported that, among great powers and nuclear states from 1800 to 1997, the technology-based offense-defense-deterrence balance significantly predicted conquest and attack.
