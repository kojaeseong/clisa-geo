The question for this session

Arms races sometimes break out even when neither side wants war. One state adds weapons for its own defense; the other reads this as a threat and arms in response; both end up less secure than before. This session asks where that structure comes from, and under what conditions states manage to cooperate within it. It offers a framework for understanding the arms race on the Korean Peninsula and the gap in deterrence across the Taiwan Strait in 2026.

1. Key concept: anarchy and the security dilemma

As session 1 showed, anarchy does not mean chaos. It means that no higher authority stands above states to enforce their promises. Each state must therefore look after its own security.

The problem this condition creates is known as the security dilemma. The term was coined by John H. Herz, a German-born American scholar, in a 1950 article. Herz argued that groups living side by side without a higher authority seek more power in order to escape the power of others; this makes the others more insecure and drives them to prepare for the worst. The pursuit of security and the accumulation of power then lock into a vicious circle.

In his 1978 article "Cooperation Under the Security Dilemma," the American political scientist Robert Jervis put the point this way: many of the means a state uses to increase its own security decrease the security of others. The spiral requires no malice. As Jervis observed, even states satisfied with the status quo can find it hard to reach goals they recognize as being in their common interest, because cooperating when the other side does not can be disastrous.

2. When cooperation is hard and when it is possible

Game theory helps clarify the problem. Suppose two states each choose between cooperating (restraining their arms) and defecting (building them up), and rank the possible outcomes by preference, with 4 the best.

In the prisoner's dilemma, defecting pays no matter what the other side does. If the other side cooperates, defection earns a 4; if it defects, defection is the only way to avoid the worst outcome, a 1. The result is mutual defection (2, 2), which leaves both worse off than mutual cooperation (3, 3).

The stag hunt takes its name from an analogy by Jean-Jacques Rousseau. If the hunters work together, they bring down a stag and everyone eats well; if one of them abandons his post to chase a hare, the rest go home empty-handed. Jervis opened his article with this analogy, likening the outcome in which everyone chases hares to an arms race and a high risk of war. Here mutual cooperation is the best outcome, so cooperating is the best choice as long as each side can trust the other to cooperate. The problem is not greed but trust.

Jervis framed the conditions that make cooperation easier in terms of the payoff cells: raise the gains from mutual cooperation and the costs of mutual defection, reduce the losses from cooperating alone and being exploited and the gains from exploiting the other side, and strengthen each side's expectation that the other will cooperate.

Repeated games and the shadow of the future. In The Evolution of Cooperation (1984), the American political scientist Robert Axelrod showed that the prisoner's dilemma changes when the same players meet again and again, in what is called a repeated game. Both of the computer tournaments he organized were won by the simplest strategy entered, tit for tat, submitted by Anatol Rapoport: cooperate on the first move, then copy whatever the other player did on the previous move. The condition is that the two states must be likely enough to meet again—that is, the future must cast a long enough shadow over the present (the shadow of the future). In a 1985 article written with Robert O. Keohane, however, Axelrod noted that this shadow is weaker in security affairs than in economic ones, because in security a successful first strike can eliminate the other side's very capacity to retaliate.

3. Jervis: the offense-defense balance and distinguishability

Jervis identified two variables that determine how severe the security dilemma is. The first is whether defensive weapons and policies can be told apart from offensive ones (distinguishability). The second is whether offense or defense has the advantage (the offense-defense balance). He named technology and geography—obstacles such as seas, large rivers, and mountain ranges—as the main factors shaping that balance. Crossing the two variables yields four worlds.

Nuclear weapons twist this framework. Jervis saw the impossibility of defense in the nuclear age as a victory not for offense but for deterrence. If both sides possess a retaliatory force that can survive a surprise attack (second-strike capability), both can be secure at once. That is why, he argued, in the context of deterrence, weapons that provide defense—missile defenses protecting cities, for example—become offensive weapons, because they threaten to neutralize the other side's capacity to retaliate.

4. A classic: Thucydides' Melian Dialogue

In the summer of 416 BC, Athens sent forces against Melos, an island colony of Sparta. Melos had at first stayed neutral, but after Athens plundered its territory it took an openly hostile stance toward Athens (5.84). In Book 5, sections 84–116, of the History of the Peloponnesian War, Thucydides narrates the expedition and records at length the exchange between the Athenian envoys, who demanded surrender, and the Melian representatives. The envoys held that justice is at issue only between equals in power, and that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" (5.89, trans. Richard Crawley). The reason they gave was security: if an island that kept its independence were left alone, Athens' subjects would take it as a sign of Athenian weakness, and only by subduing it could the empire be safe. The Melians appealed to the principle of fairness and to the hope of Spartan help; the Athenians replied that Sparta would not come. Melos surrendered that winter, and Athens executed the adult men and sold the women and children into slavery (5.116).

The line about the strong and the weak is often quoted as Thucydides' own conclusion, but the words belong to the Athenian envoys. Immediately after the dialogue, Thucydides narrates the Sicilian expedition (415–413 BC), in which the Athenian force was annihilated. In The Tragic Vision of Politics (2003), Richard Ned Lebow read beneath this sequence a lesson: the language of power ultimately brings down the strong themselves.

In the dialogue, no higher authority existed to hear the weaker side's appeal to justice, and even powerful Athens acted out of fear that appearing weak would be dangerous. The security dilemma is not a problem for the weak alone (this lecture's interpretation).

5. Historical cases

The Anglo-German naval race before World War I. Under the leadership of Alfred von Tirpitz, state secretary of the Imperial Naval Office, Germany passed Navy Laws in 1898 and 1900 that sharply expanded its battleship fleet. The calculation was that a fleet large enough to leave Britain weakened against the other great powers even if Britain won a battle would make London reluctant to confront Germany. In February 1912, negotiations in Berlin led by Richard Haldane, the British secretary of state for war, broke down when Germany demanded a British pledge of neutrality in return for acknowledging British naval superiority. At the end of July 1914, Britain had 29 capital ships to Germany's 18 [secondary source]. Jervis also discusses this case. Britain saw the German fleet only as an offensive weapon aimed at itself, but to Germany the Royal Navy, whatever Britain's intentions, was likewise an important instrument of coercion (p. 170). He judged that pre-1914 Europe bore no small resemblance to the doubly dangerous world, cell ① of his four worlds (p. 211).

The Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty in the Cold War. Signed in Moscow on May 26, 1972, by U.S. President Richard Nixon and Leonid Brezhnev, general secretary of the Soviet Communist Party, the treaty limited each side to two missile-defense sites (one at its capital and one at an intercontinental ballistic missile base); a 1974 protocol reduced the number to one. The paradox that a treaty restricting defense could contribute to stability is resolved by the deterrence logic in section 3: a defensive shield able to stop retaliatory missiles looks to the other side like preparation to neutralize its capacity to retaliate. Jervis, too, cited this concern as one of the rationales for the ABM Treaty. The United States withdrew from the treaty in June 2002 [secondary source].

6. Applying it to 2026: the Korean Peninsula and the Taiwan Strait

The facts. In November 2025, the United States formally endorsed South Korea's pursuit of uranium enrichment, reprocessing, and nuclear-powered submarines; North Korea denounced the move as granting South Korea recognition as a quasi-nuclear state. The South Korean government drew up a basic plan for nuclear-powered submarines in May 2026, and on September 29 the Cabinet approved a special bill on nuclear-powered submarines, which the government aims to enact by the end of the year. North Korea unveiled a nuclear-powered submarine in December 2025 (amid suspicions of Russian technology transfer); in June 2026, Kim Jong Un ordered an exponential expansion of nuclear weapons production; and on October 3, North Korea fired a ballistic missile that it claimed was a hypersonic intermediate-range missile. In August, Japan's prime minister said Japan maintains its three non-nuclear principles but left out the phrase pledging to uphold them; in September, Japan's defense minister said acquiring nuclear-powered submarines had not been ruled out. On May 8, 2026, Taiwan's Legislative Yuan cut the government's request for a special defense budget from NT$1.25 trillion to NT$780 billion, stripping out funding for about 200,000 domestically built drones, air defenses, and the mass production of interceptor missiles.

Through the lens of the security dilemma. Clisa Geopolitics sees the nuclear domino—North Korea's nuclear buildup, South Korea's nuclear latency, and Japan's preparations, each justifying the others—as a security dilemma that no one wants. Applied to this case, Jervis's framework makes enrichment and reprocessing facilities a textbook example of low distinguishability (this lecture's interpretation). Because the same facility can produce both reactor fuel and weapons material, other states prepare against capability rather than intent. Clisa Geopolitics estimates that over the five to 10 years it takes for such facilities to come online, the time between a decision to build nuclear weapons and their completion would shrink from years to months. It therefore holds that South Korea should not race to acquire nuclear latency on its own but should use it as leverage and insurance in negotiating a phased freeze: linked step by step to whether North Korea freezes (the way NATO's 1979 Double-Track Decision led to the 1987 Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty), pursued within a joint South Korea–U.S. framework, coordinated in advance with Japan, and accompanied by expanded inspections. Nuclear-powered submarines cut both ways, depending on their mission. Jervis regarded submarine-launched ballistic missiles kept for retaliation as defensive weapons, and the ability to track an adversary's submarines as a threat to that adversary's retaliatory capacity (pp. 207–208). In Jervis's and Axelrod's terms, expanded inspections are a device for raising distinguishability, and step-by-step linkage, like tit for tat, casts the shadow of the future over the other side (this lecture's interpretation).

The Taiwan Strait presents a different picture. The drones, air defenses, and interceptor missiles that Taiwan sought to expand are highly useful for repelling an amphibious landing from across the sea and of little use for attacking the mainland. On Jervis's two variables, they are distinguishable and favor defense—an investment in the direction of cell ④, the doubly safe world (this lecture's interpretation). Yet these were precisely the items the Legislative Yuan cut, and Clisa Geopolitics warns against a structure in which the gap in deterrence rewards a China inclined to wait for the 2028 elections.

Assessment. On the Korean Peninsula, capabilities whose offensive and defensive uses are hard to tell apart (enrichment and reprocessing facilities, hypersonic missiles) are growing at the same time, while channels for repeated contact, such as military communication lines, remain cut. This marks a drift toward the top row of Jervis's table, where offense and defense cannot be distinguished (this lecture's interpretation). The conditions for cooperation have to be built through transparency that raises distinguishability and through arrangements that link each step to the other side's behavior. This assessment should be revisited if inter-Korean military communication lines are restored this year (that is, if the third row of the forecast table resolves no), if North Korea accepts a verified freeze on nuclear testing and fissile material production, or if South Korea formally adopts a phased plan that ties its pursuit of enrichment and reprocessing to expanded inspections and to whether North Korea freezes.

  1. How does anarchy differ from disorder? Explain why, under anarchy, even states satisfied with the status quo can be drawn into an arms race.
  2. What blocks cooperation in the prisoner's dilemma, and what blocks it in the stag hunt? Which cells of the payoff table account for the difference?
  3. What is needed for Axelrod's shadow of the future to lengthen? Why is that shadow weaker in security affairs?
  4. Of Jervis's four worlds, what conditions produce the doubly safe world? Explain also why the ABM Treaty sought to increase stability by limiting defense.
