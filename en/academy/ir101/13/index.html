The question for this session

On September 1, 2026, the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) told its Board of Governors that it could no longer say how much of Iran's 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60 percent—the stockpile Iran held as of June 13, 2025—still exists, or where it is. The IAEA has had no access to the enrichment facilities damaged in the June 2025 strikes, and after the United States and Israel struck Iran again on February 28, 2026, it halted verification altogether. On September 29, South Korea's cabinet approved a special bill on nuclear-powered submarines. North Korea's constitution, revised in March, now allows the President of the State Affairs—Kim Jong Un's title as head of state—to delegate the authority to use nuclear weapons. This session takes up four questions: How did nuclear weapons change war and strategy? Would the world be safer if they spread? Why do states build nuclear weapons, or give them up? And how great is the risk of accident and miscalculation?

1. Key concepts: the nuclear revolution, deterrence and compellence, and second-strike capability

The atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima on August 6, 1945, and on Nagasaki on August 9 had yields estimated by the U.S. Department of Energy at 15 and 21 kilotons of TNT, respectively. The Federation of American Scientists (FAS) and the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) estimated that the world held 12,187 nuclear warheads at the start of 2026: 5,420 in Russia, 5,042 in the United States, 620 in China, and about 60 in North Korea. New START, the treaty that capped U.S. and Russian strategic nuclear forces, expired in February 2026. Weapons this destructive changed the logic of war and strategy—a shift known as the nuclear revolution.

Deterrence is a threat meant to keep an adversary from starting something; compellence is a threat meant to make an adversary stop or undo something it is already doing. Thomas C. Schelling drew the distinction in Arms and Influence (1966) [secondary source]. As session 2 explained, the ability to retaliate after absorbing a surprise attack is called second-strike capability, and when both sides have it, the result is mutual assured destruction (MAD). In The Meaning of the Nuclear Revolution (1989), Robert Jervis, whose work on the security dilemma was discussed in session 2, argued that this mutual vulnerability is a fact, not a policy choice: it makes military victory between nuclear powers impossible, stabilizes the status quo, and turns crises into contests of resolve and risk-taking [secondary source]. In 1965, Glenn H. Snyder described the stability-instability paradox: the more stable the strategic "balance of terror," the easier it becomes to use force at lower levels of violence [secondary source].

2. A classic: Brodie and Schelling, from winning wars to averting them

In The Absolute Weapon, published in 1946, the year after Hiroshima, Bernard Brodie wrote: "Thus far the chief purpose of our military establishment has been to win wars. From now on its chief purpose must be to avert them." The passage is credited as the first cogent statement of the paradox that nuclear weapons are most useful when they are not used [secondary source].

In "The Threat That Leaves Something to Chance," chapter 8 of The Strategy of Conflict (1960), Schelling recast that paradox in the language of bargaining. A threat to start a nuclear war deliberately is hard to believe, because carrying it out would be catastrophic for the one making it. But a threat to keep raising the risk of events no one can control until the other side backs down can be credible [secondary source]. The idea echoes another Schelling insight, discussed in session 6—that the side whose hands are tied can get more—since both rest on gaining bargaining power through commitment. By this logic, a crisis becomes a test of who can stomach more risk, and the possibility of accident itself becomes an instrument of deterrence (this lecture's interpretation).

3. Would more nuclear weapons make the world safer? Waltz and Sagan

Kenneth N. Waltz made the case in The Spread of Nuclear Weapons: More May Be Better, an Adelphi Paper published by the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS) in 1981. The likelihood of war, he argued, falls as deterrent and defensive capabilities rise; because nuclear weapons, used responsibly, make wars hard to start, the gradual spread of nuclear weapons is better than no spread and better than rapid spread. In The Spread of Nuclear Weapons: A Debate (1995), written with Waltz, Scott D. Sagan countered that "more will be worse": new nuclear states may lack the organizational structures needed to keep their weapons under safe and rational control, raising the risk of nuclear war, whether deliberate or accidental [secondary source]. MIT 17.41 assigns the first two chapters of the book for its session 21.

In terms of the levels of analysis introduced in session 1, Waltz treats the state as a unitary actor that weighs costs and benefits, while Sagan draws on organization theory and starts from the premise that military organizations follow routines and parochial interests and make mistakes (this lecture's interpretation).

4. Why do states build nuclear weapons? Sagan's three models and the NPT

In a 1996 article, Sagan argued that the conventional view, which explains nuclear programs by national security alone, is "dangerously inadequate," and proposed three models. In the security model, states build nuclear weapons to increase their security against foreign threats, especially nuclear ones; the model also covers the choice to ally with a nuclear power and rely on its extended deterrence—its pledge to deter attacks on its allies with its own nuclear weapons (p. 57). In the domestic politics model, the weapons are political tools that advance parochial domestic and bureaucratic interests; and in the norms model, acquiring the weapons—or refraining from doing so—provides an important normative symbol of a state's modernity and identity (p. 55). While granting that the security model best explains the majority of cases, he concluded that proliferation and restraint have happened for more than one reason, and that different cases are best explained by different models (p. 85).

The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT) opened for signature on July 1, 1968, entered into force on March 5, 1970, and was extended indefinitely on May 11, 1995; 191 states have joined it, a count that includes North Korea, whose 2003 withdrawal remains contested. At its core is a bargain. The nuclear-weapon states—those that had manufactured and exploded a nuclear device before January 1, 1967 (Article IX(3))—undertake not to transfer nuclear weapons (Article I); non-nuclear-weapon states undertake not to manufacture or acquire them (Article II); all parties keep the "inalienable right" to use nuclear energy for peaceful purposes (Article IV); and all undertake to pursue negotiations in good faith on ending the nuclear arms race and on nuclear disarmament (Article VI). Article X allows a party to withdraw if it decides that "extraordinary events, related to the subject matter of this Treaty, have jeopardized the supreme interests of its country," on three months' notice to all other parties and the UN Security Council. On January 10, 2003, North Korea declared "an automatic and immediate effectuation of its withdrawal," and in Resolution 1718 (2006) the Security Council demanded that it retract the announcement. The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW), which bans the development and possession of nuclear weapons outright, was adopted on July 7, 2017, and entered into force on January 22, 2021; as of October 15, 2026, it has 75 parties. The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT), adopted by the UN General Assembly in September 1996, has yet to enter into force.

5. Accidents and miscalculation: Goldsboro and command and control

On the night of January 23–24, 1961, a B-52 bomber broke apart in the air over Goldsboro, North Carolina, and two Mark 39 hydrogen bombs fell to the ground. Parker F. Jones, who headed the nuclear weapons safety department at Sandia National Laboratories, wrote in an internal memo dated October 22, 1969, that "one simple, dynamo-technology, low voltage switch stood between the United States and a major catastrophe." Had a short circuit reached an "arm" line during the breakup, the bomb could have produced a nuclear explosion. Jones corrected the physicist Ralph Lapp's claim that five of the bomb's six safety devices had been tripped, but agreed with Lapp's central point. The memo, released in 2013 in response to a Freedom of Information Act request by the journalist Eric Schlosser, is assigned reading for session 21 of MIT 17.41. That even the organizations of the country that built the first nuclear weapons lacked adequate safeguards against accidents lends weight to Sagan's warning (this lecture's interpretation).

The design of command and control poses the same dilemma. If only the leader can authorize nuclear use, a state that loses its leader may be unable to retaliate; if authority is delegated down the chain in advance, the risk of miscalculation or unauthorized use grows. Peter D. Feaver called this the "always/never dilemma": nuclear weapons must always work when authorized and never be used without authorization [secondary source]. In Schelling's terms, predelegation makes deterrence credible by leaving something to chance—at the price of making chance itself larger (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Historical cases

The Cuban missile crisis (1962). On October 27, 1962, U.S. forces forced the Soviet submarine B-59 to the surface in the approaches to Cuba. The National Security Archive at George Washington University describes that day as one of the most dangerous of the crisis and notes that B-59 and other Soviet submarines in the area carried nuclear torpedoes of about 15 kilotons. The same day, Robert Kennedy, the attorney general and the president's brother, told the Soviet ambassador, Anatoly Dobrynin, that the president was ready to withdraw the U.S. Jupiter missiles from Turkey but would need four to five months to do so—a pledge kept as a secret understanding rather than made part of the public settlement. The outcome could have turned on chance at sea as well as on leaders' calculations, which is why both Schelling's and Sagan's arguments apply (this lecture's interpretation).

The Budapest Memorandum (1994). On December 5, 1994, the leaders of Ukraine, Russia, the United States, and the United Kingdom signed a memorandum that welcomed Ukraine's accession to the NPT as a non-nuclear-weapon state and took into account its commitment to eliminate all nuclear weapons from its territory within a specified period. Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States "reaffirm[ed]" their commitments to respect Ukraine's independence, sovereignty, and existing borders and to refrain from the threat or use of force against it. But the memorandum contains no mutual-defense clause. Beyond a pledge to seek Security Council action if Ukraine became the victim of aggression involving nuclear weapons (paragraph 4) and a promise to consult (paragraph 6), it promised nothing. Clisa Geopolitics judges that it failed because it had no means of enforcement.

South Korea's nuclear weapons effort in the 1970s. In a telegram to the U.S. embassy in Seoul dated March 4, 1975, the State Department said its basic objective was "to discourage ROK effort in this area and to inhibit to the fullest possible extent any ROK development of a nuclear explosive capability or a delivery system." South Korea's National Assembly approved ratification of the NPT on March 19, 1975, and the treaty entered into force for South Korea on April 23. In a 2017 paper, Se Young Jang argued that Canada's decision to make reactor sales conditional on ratification, more than U.S. pressure, was decisive in bringing South Korea into the NPT. The weapons program itself, Jang found, was formally suspended only in December 1976, under U.S. pressure, after a planned French reprocessing plant fell through early that year. Pressure from an ally fits Sagan's security model, but all three of his models concern the motives of states that want the bomb; the conditions that technology suppliers attach need to be considered separately (this lecture's interpretation).

7. Applying it to 2026: Iran's unaccounted-for uranium and nuclear weapons on the Korean Peninsula

The facts. According to the IAEA's September report, Iran held 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60 percent as of June 13, 2025, of which the agency had verified 432.9 kilograms. It has been more than a year since the IAEA lost "continuity of knowledge" over the stocks at facilities damaged in the June 2025 attacks. Immediately after the further U.S. and Israeli strikes of February 28, 2026, the agency halted verification; since then it has carried out only one routine inspection, at the Bushehr nuclear power plant in June. Clisa Geopolitics judges that North Korea drew a second lesson, after Libya, from the killing of Iran's supreme leader in those strikes—a lesson in the same vein as the fates of Saddam Hussein and Muammar Qaddafi discussed in session 7—and that denuclearization has effectively dropped off the negotiating agenda through 2030.

North Korea conducted six nuclear tests between October 9, 2006, and September 3, 2017, and at its Ninth Party Congress in February 2026 the ruling party formally enshrined the country's status as a nuclear weapons state. Article 3 of North Korea's 2022 law on its nuclear forces policy (the Law on the DPRK's Policy on Nuclear Forces) provides that if the command and control system for its nuclear forces is endangered by an enemy attack, a nuclear strike shall be launched automatically and immediately according to a predetermined plan; Clisa Geopolitics reads "automatically" as predelegation rather than technical automation. The delegation clause in Article 89 of the revised constitution, adopted in March 2026, came shortly after the death of Iran's supreme leader, though a causal link between the two has not been established. For South Korea, Clisa Geopolitics ranks conditional nuclear latency as one element of its top-ranked combination of options: building enrichment and reprocessing capabilities and nuclear-powered submarines within the NPT, while announcing in advance that Seoul will take the next step if North Korea refuses a freeze. It ranks an independent nuclear arsenal, which would require withdrawing from the NPT, at the very bottom, because it would inflict irreversible damage on the alliance with the United States and on the economy and could set off a proliferation cascade that reaches Japan. Public opinion runs the other way: in a Gallup Korea poll conducted August 25–27, 2026, 65 percent of South Koreans supported building the country's own nuclear weapons and 30 percent opposed.

Through the lens of theory. Sagan's security model explains why North Korea, having watched a non-nuclear Iran come under attack and lose its leader, is all the more determined to keep its weapons; by writing its nuclear status into a party congress decision and the constitution, it has also made the weapons part of the regime's identity, which overlaps with the norms model. In the security model, South Korea's restraint rests on the credibility of U.S. extended deterrence. Through the lens of the domestic politics model, public support for an independent arsenal can strengthen the hand of political forces that want one. Yet at least one survey has found that support drops sharply when respondents are reminded of costs such as sanctions or a weakened alliance (Asan Institute for Policy Studies), so it is hard to say that opinion has already settled in favor of the bomb. Restraint rests on NPT norms, in the terms of the norms model; on the alliance, in the terms of the security model; and on the cost of sanctions. Highly enriched uranium whose whereabouts are unknown could be diverted or leak regardless of any state's intentions, which is the kind of gap in control Sagan warns about; Waltz would answer that deterrence would hold even if Iran got the bomb. North Korea's delegation of launch authority makes deterrence more credible in Schelling's sense but raises the risk of accidental use. (This entire paragraph is this lecture's interpretation.)

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows Sagan and the Goldsboro memo in the original, works through the conditions for deterrence and the accumulation of accident risk with numbers, and looks at the debates that have put the theories to the test.

Reading the original

Passage 1. More than a tool of security

"Nuclear weapons, like other weapons, are more than tools of national security; they are political objects of considerable importance in domestic debates and internal bureaucratic struggles and can also serve as international normative symbols of modernity and identity." (Sagan 1996/97, p. 55)

The premise is that decisions to acquire weapons are shaped not by a single national calculation but by domestic actors and international norms. The conclusion is that states facing the same threat can make different choices, so that policies to stop proliferation cannot rely on reducing threats alone (this lecture's interpretation). Sagan borrowed Secretary of State George Shultz's line "proliferation begets proliferation" to sum up the security model, under which the history of proliferation looks like a strategic chain reaction (pp. 57–58).

Passage 2. A single switch

"One simple, dynamo-technology, low voltage switch stood between the United States and a major catastrophe!" (Jones 1969, memo, p. 2)

On the same page, Jones wrote that the Mark 39 Mod 2 bomb "did not possess adequate safety for the airborne alert role in the B-52." The premise is that a real path to accidental detonation existed; the conclusion is that the design did not adequately block it. The sentence supports Sagan's argument that nuclear safety depends not only on the rationality of leaders but also on the technical details of systems that organizations design and maintain (this lecture's interpretation).

Working it out

Problem 1. The conditions for deterrence. Country A would gain 10 by seizing part of Country B's territory. If B retaliates, A suffers a loss of 100. (a) If B is certain to retaliate, does A attack? (b) If A puts the probability of retaliation at p, for what values of p does deterrence hold? (c) Suppose retaliation would also cost B 50, so that B calculates it would rather not retaliate. How does A reason? How does Schelling's "threat that leaves something to chance" try to solve this problem?

Problem 2. How accident risk accumulates. Suppose a nuclear-armed state has a 0.2 percent chance each year of a serious nuclear weapons accident. (a) What is the probability that it has no accident in 50 years? (b) If there are ten such states and accidents occur independently, what is the probability that none of them has an accident in 50 years? (c) Suppose five of the ten are new nuclear states with an annual accident probability of 0.5 percent, and the other five remain at 0.2 percent. What is the probability of no accident anywhere in 50 years? (d) Which side of the Waltz-Sagan debate do these results support?

Problem 3. Placing cases in theoretical categories. Assign each of the following to one of these categories and justify your answer: (i) the security model; (ii) the domestic politics model; (iii) the norms model; (iv) the stability-instability paradox; (v) an organizational accident. (a) A state that starts a nuclear program in response to threats from a nuclear-armed neighbor; (b) a state whose atomic energy agency and military push a nuclear program to win budgets and prestige; (c) a state that has the technology but does not build weapons because of its identity as a model citizen of the international community; (d) two nuclear-armed states that avoid all-out war but repeatedly clash on a small scale along their border; (e) the 1961 breakup of a bomber over Goldsboro and the fall of its hydrogen bombs.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. (a) Attacking yields 10 − 100 = −90, so A does not attack.
  2. (b) The expected gain from attacking is 10 − 100p. For deterrence to hold, this must be negative, so p must exceed 0.1.
  3. (c) If A believes B would rather not retaliate, p approaches zero and deterrence collapses. Instead of committing B to retaliate in advance, Schelling's threat creates a risk of uncontrollable escalation that keeps p above zero. In this problem, a risk greater than 10 percent is enough.

Problem 2.

  1. (a) 0.99850 ≈ 0.905, or about 90.5 percent. The probability of at least one accident is about 9.5 percent.
  2. (b) 0.998500 ≈ 0.368, or about 36.8 percent. The probability of an accident somewhere rises to about 63.2 percent.
  3. (c) 0.998250 × 0.995250 ≈ 0.606 × 0.286 ≈ 0.173, or about 17 percent. The probability of an accident rises to about 83 percent.
  4. (d) A small risk in any one state accumulates as the number of nuclear states grows and time passes, and if new nuclear states are riskier, as Sagan argues, it accumulates much faster. Waltz would reply that new nuclear states also behave cautiously, so the real question is whether the 0.5 percent in (c) is realistic. (All the numbers are hypothetical.)

Problem 3.

  1. (a) (i). An external nuclear threat drives the program.
  2. (b) (ii). The interests of domestic organizations drive the decision.
  3. (c) (iii). Restraint becomes a symbol of national identity.
  4. (d) (iv). Stability at the strategic level makes lower-level clashes easier.
  5. (e) (v). The danger arose from organizational and technical flaws, independent of any leader's decision.

Evidence and counterarguments

Sagan (1996/97): is the security model enough? Sagan observed that in the security model, proliferation looks like a chain reaction in which "proliferation begets proliferation" (pp. 57–58). He conceded that the security model accounts for most cases but concluded that proliferation and restraint have multiple causes and that different cases fit different models (p. 85). The limitation is that with several models available, any case can be explained by one of them, which makes it hard to say in advance which model will apply (this lecture's interpretation).

Waltz (1981) and Sagan (1995): does proliferation bring stability? Waltz held that nuclear weapons raise the cost of war and so lower its likelihood, making gradual proliferation stabilizing; Sagan held that new nuclear states' organizational controls are imperfect, raising the risk of deliberate or accidental nuclear war [secondary source]. The absence of all-out war between nuclear powers since 1945 is cited in Waltz's favor; near misses driven by accident or chance that could have ended in catastrophe—Goldsboro, or B-59 in 1962—are cited in Sagan's. The limitation is that because nuclear war has never happened, outcomes cannot settle the dispute (this lecture's interpretation).

Lapp's account and Jones's memo (1969): can the record of accidents be trusted? In Kill and Overkill, Lapp wrote that the Goldsboro bomb had six safety devices, five of which had been tripped. Jones corrected this: the bomb had four, not six; one did not work in the air, one was "set off" by the fall, and two were rendered ineffective by the breakup. Yet he agreed with Lapp's central point—only a single switch had held. Popular accounts of accidents may exaggerate, but going back to the original record does not always shrink the danger (this lecture's interpretation).
