The question for this session

On September 17, 2026, South Korea's minister of unification, who oversees Seoul's policy toward North Korea, told the National Assembly that humanitarianism takes precedence over ideology and that the government was preparing to send medical equipment to the North. The 166 types of equipment involved had received exemptions from UN sanctions. On September 29, however, the assembly's National Defense Committee failed to adopt a resolution condemning North Korea over a land mine incident, because the governing and opposition parties could not agree on language calling for a review of the aid to North Korea then under way. South Korea, a party to this dispute, was itself an aid recipient until the late 1990s. This session takes up four questions: Why are some countries rich and others poor? Does aid promote growth? Why do donors give aid? And by what should development be measured?

1. Key concepts: development, aid, and fungibility

Development is commonly measured as growth in income per person. (The Korean edition of these notes uses two words: gaebal for development as a matter of policy and aid, and baljeon for the transformation of a society as a whole.) Official development assistance (ODA) consists of the grants and concessional, low-interest loans that donor governments provide to developing countries; the Development Assistance Committee (DAC) of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) first defined it in 1969. Humanitarian aid, given to save lives during wars, disasters, and famines, serves a different purpose from development aid.

A concept that is easy to overlook in assessing aid is fungibility. Suppose an aid-funded hospital treats patients, but the recipient government shifts to its military the money it had planned to spend on hospitals. Then what the aid actually increased was military spending. The effect of aid should be judged not by where the aid went but by what changed compared with what would have happened without it (this lecture's interpretation).

2. Rich countries and poor countries: modernization theory, dependency theory, and the institutional approach

Modernization theory holds that all societies develop through similar stages. In The Stages of Economic Growth (1960), W. W. Rostow set out five: the traditional society, the preconditions for take-off, the take-off, the drive to maturity, and the age of high mass consumption. In political science, Seymour Martin Lipset hypothesized in a 1959 article that the wealthier a country is, the more likely it is to sustain democracy [secondary source]. Modernization theory assumes that poverty is a starting point—the condition of a society that has not yet developed.

Dependency theory turns that assumption on its head. In "The Development of Underdevelopment" (1966), Andre Gunder Frank criticized the conventional view that economic development proceeds through a succession of capitalist stages, arguing instead that the expansion of capitalism had penetrated even the most isolated sectors of the underdeveloped world [secondary source]. Underdevelopment, in this view, is not a stage that precedes development but a product of a society's relationship with world capitalism. The rapid growth of South Korea and Taiwan, both deeply integrated into world markets, posed a serious challenge to the theory (this lecture's interpretation).

The institutional approach traces the difference to institutions. In "The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development" (2001), Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James A. Robinson used the mortality rates of European settlers to estimate the effect of institutions. Where endemic disease made mortality high, Europeans set up extractive states designed to drain resources, as in Belgian rule over the Congo; where large numbers of Europeans migrated and settled, they built "Neo-Europes"; and the institutions established then have persisted to the present (pp. 1369–1370). Measuring institutions by the degree of protection against the risk of expropriation in 64 former colonies (p. 1377), the authors estimated that raising Nigeria's institutions to the level of Chile's could, in the long run, increase Nigeria's income as much as sevenfold (p. 1371). Once institutions were controlled for, distance from the equator and location in Africa were not significant (p. 1372).

In a 2001 article, Jeffrey D. Sachs and Andrew M. Warner surveyed and extended research on the resource curse: the finding that countries rich in natural resources tend, paradoxically, to grow more slowly. The political version of the curse, the claim that oil impedes democracy, is taken up in the Going deeper section.

3. The East Asian developmental state

In MITI and the Japanese Miracle (1982), Chalmers Johnson argued that Japan's growth came from an industrial policy that differed both from socialist central planning and from the Anglo-American regulatory state; the book established the concept of the developmental state [secondary source]. In Asia's Next Giant (1989), Alice H. Amsden treated South Korea as a case of late industrialization and argued that rapid growth becomes possible when the state can control and discipline rent seeking by private firms [secondary source]. In Governing the Market (1990), Robert Wade read the success of Taiwan and its neighbors as neither a triumph of the free market nor the result of government intervention alone, and focused instead on how markets and state administration worked together.

The developmental-state approach looks less at the stages of the market or at the world system than at the capacity of the state and its relationship with business. That the state in question was, in South Korea, an authoritarian government puts the approach at odds with the perspective of Amartya Sen, discussed in section 5 (this lecture's interpretation).

4. Does aid work, and why do donors give it?

In a 2000 article, Craig Burnside and David Dollar built a policy index from the budget balance, the inflation rate, and trade openness, and analyzed data for 1970–1993 divided into six four-year periods. Aid raised growth in developing countries with good policies but had little effect in countries with poor policies, and the allocation of aid was not much influenced by the quality of policy.

In a 2004 comment, William Easterly, Ross Levine, and David Roodman applied the same method but extended the data through 1997 and filled in missing observations. The interaction term of aid and policy then ceased to be significant. Burnside and Dollar defended their conclusion in a reply in the same issue. MIT 17.41 pairs a 2005 article by Sachs, a longtime advocate of more aid, with a 2009 newspaper op-ed by Dambisa Moyo, who called for ending it [secondary source].

The small effect of aid may stem from the fact that it was never given for growth alone (this lecture's interpretation). In a 2000 article analyzing how a range of donors allocate aid, Alberto Alesina and Dollar reported that where aid goes is shaped as much by political and strategic considerations as by recipients' economic needs and policy performance, and that a colonial past and political alliances are major determinants. Other things being equal, however, countries that democratized received more aid.

5. A classic: Sen's Development as Freedom

In Development as Freedom (1999), Amartya Sen defined development as the expansion of the real freedoms that people enjoy, setting himself against narrower views that equate development with growth in gross national product or personal incomes, with industrialization, or with technological progress. Development, in his account, means removing the major sources of unfreedom, such as poverty and tyranny, poor economic opportunities, and systematic social deprivation (p. 3). He identified five instrumental freedoms—political freedoms, economic facilities, social opportunities, transparency guarantees, and protective security—each of which, he argued, helps to advance a person's overall capability (p. 10). A capability is a real opportunity to achieve functionings, such as being well nourished or being educated [secondary source].

Sen viewed famine through the same lens. Because democratic governments must win elections and face public criticism, they have strong incentives to prevent famines; no famine, he wrote, has ever occurred in a functioning democracy anywhere in world history, and famines have instead struck colonial territories, one-party states, and military dictatorships (p. 16). The Human Development Index (HDI), first published by the UN Development Programme (UNDP) in 1990, is a measure combining longevity, education, and income that Sen designed with Mahbub ul Haq.

In a 1997 lecture, Sen rebutted the "Lee thesis"—named for Lee Kuan Yew, the former prime minister of Singapore—which holds that authoritarianism is better for growth, arguing that it rests on selectively chosen cases. By this standard, even if authoritarian governments drove South Korea's rapid growth, the development of that period cannot be judged by growth rates alone (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Historical cases

South Korea: from aid recipient to donor. From 1945 until the late 1990s, South Korea received about $12.7 billion in aid. It began giving aid of its own by establishing the Economic Development Cooperation Fund (EDCF) in 1987 and the Korea International Cooperation Agency (KOICA) in 1991. It graduated from World Bank development lending in 1995, and in 2000 it was moved on the DAC List of Aid Recipients from Part I, which covered ODA recipients, to Part II, which covered countries in transition. Its accession to the DAC was approved in November 2009, and in January 2010 it became the committee's 24th member. South Korea kept troops in Vietnam from 1965 to 1973, at one point about 50,000 of them. In return it obtained about $516 million in economic benefits in 1965–1969 and the continuation of aid, but it could not prevent the withdrawal of the U.S. Army's 7th Infantry Division from South Korea in 1971. The case is consistent with Alesina and Dollar's finding that alliances shape aid (this lecture's interpretation).

North Korea's famine of the 1990s and international food aid. Cut off from Soviet support when the Soviet Union collapsed, and governed by a regime that failed to respond in time, North Korea suffered a severe famine in the 1990s. In a 2007 study with a foreword by Sen, Stephan Haggard and Marcus Noland estimated that 600,000 to one million people died. Although food shortages had been evident since 1991, the North Korean government did not appeal for international food aid until 1995, and two-thirds of the roughly $2.3 billion in aid it received from 1995 to 2005 was food. Haggard and Noland concluded that about 30 percent of food aid was diverted, mainly to elites including the military, and that the government cut food imports as aid deliveries rose [secondary source]. The case illustrates fungibility: what aid increased may have been not so much the total supply of food as the foreign exchange the government saved (this lecture's interpretation).

7. Applying it to 2026: the People's Lives tab and humanitarian aid to North Korea

The facts. The Defense Committee's resolution failed because the governing Democratic Party of Korea rejected the opposition People Power Party's demand for a review of aid to North Korea. According to Bank of Korea estimates, North Korea's economy grew 3.5 percent in 2025, its third straight year of growth in the 3 percent range, driven by cooperation with Russia and trade with China. Clisa Geopolitics' People's Lives tab measures people's lives using the seven categories of human security defined by the United Nations. Personal safety, community, political, and economic security are scored from 0 to 100, while food, health, and environment appear as alerts, and the categories are not added together. The economic score follows the method of the Human Development Index, setting per capita gross national income of $100 at 0 and $75,000 at 100; South Koreans score 95.7 ($56,000 at purchasing power parity). For North Koreans, there are no income figures at purchasing power parity and no homicide statistics, so the economic and personal safety categories are marked "not measurable," and because North Korea was dropped from the UN Food and Agriculture Organization's (FAO) list of countries requiring external assistance for food in March 2025, the food category also reads "data gap." North Koreans' political score, which measures freedom from state violence, is 5.3, and among the futures projected through 2030 it improves substantially only in one: regime change in North Korea, assigned a probability of 1 percent.

Through the lens of theory. That growth in the 3 percent range coexists with a political score of just over 5 supports Sen's argument that development should not be measured by income alone. That North Korea's growth is driven by its relations with patrons echoes its experience in the 1960s, when it extracted as much aid as it could from both sides of the Sino-Soviet split. Medical equipment for North Korea, too, can hardly escape the problem of fungibility: while the equipment is used for health care, the North Korean government can divert the money it saves to other purposes. Assessment. The dispute in the National Assembly in September 2026 turned less on how much the medical equipment would improve the lives of North Koreans than on whether to link aid to the North with its land mine provocation. By Sen's standard, the value of humanitarian aid should be measured by the capabilities it restores to people. The same approach underlies Clisa Geopolitics' estimate that if its recommendation—to pursue a phased freeze together with coexistence as a special relationship, the status the two Koreas' 1991 Basic Agreement gives their ties—were carried out, separated families would be able to confirm whether relatives are alive and exchange letters by 2030 in 28 of 100 futures rather than 13. That figure reflects the effect of the recommendation as a whole, not of the medical equipment. The judgment that the dispute turned on the link to provocation rather than on the effectiveness of aid should be revisited if, when the National Assembly reviews the Inter-Korean Cooperation Fund in the 2027 budget in December 2026, monitoring of distribution and the effect on ordinary North Koreans become the main points of contention.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows the arguments of the institutional approach and of Sen in the original, works through differences in growth rates and the fungibility of aid with numbers, and looks at research that has put the theories to the test.

Reading the original

Passage 1. From mortality to institutions

"We hypothesize that settler mortality affected settlements; settlements affected early institutions; and early institutions persisted and formed the basis of current institutions." (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001, p. 1373)

The hypothesis rests on three premises: there were different types of colonization policy, which created different sets of institutions; the colonization strategy was influenced by whether settlement was feasible; and the colonial state and its institutions persisted after independence (p. 1370). The conclusion is that using mortality as an instrumental variable for institutions makes it possible to filter out reverse causation, in which countries get better institutions because they are rich. The inference depends on the assumption that mortality affects income only through institutions, and the critics take aim at that assumption. If settlers brought with them not only institutions but also knowledge and skills, mortality may be capturing differences in human capital rather than in institutions (this lecture's interpretation). Edward L. Glaeser and his coauthors (2004) argued that human capital is a more fundamental source of growth than institutions.

Passage 2. From income to freedom

"Development can be seen, it is argued here, as a process of expanding the real freedoms that people enjoy." (Sen 1999, p. 3)

The premise is that income and goods are merely means of expanding what people can do and be. Because different people convert the same income into different capabilities, income is an imperfect yardstick, and the conclusion follows that development should be measured by freedom itself (this lecture's interpretation). Sen held that freedom is both a constituent part of development (p. 4) and an instrument for expanding other freedoms (p. 10). Criticisms and refinements center on which capabilities should count. Sen declined to endorse a predetermined list of capabilities, whereas Martha C. Nussbaum has defended a list of ten "central human capabilities" [secondary source].

Working it out

Problem 1. Differences in growth rates and the rule of 70. If income grows at g percent a year, it takes about 70 ÷ g years to double (the "rule of 70"). Country A has a per capita income of $1,000 and grows 7 percent a year. Country B has a per capita income of $2,000 and grows 3.5 percent a year. (a) How many years does each country take to double its income? (b) What will each country's per capita income be after 40 years? (c) After how many years will A catch up with B?

Problem 2. The fungibility of aid. A recipient government has a budget of 100. Without aid, it spends 20 on health, 30 on the military, and 50 on everything else. A donor provides 10 in aid, earmarked for health. (a) If the government keeps total health spending at 20 and shifts all the domestic funds it saves to the military, how much does it spend on health and on the military, and what did the aid actually increase? (b) If it spends 3 of the 10 it saves on additional health care and shifts the rest to the military, what percentage of the aid went to increasing health spending? (c) To find out whether the aid really increased health spending, what should the donor use as the point of comparison?

Problem 3. Placing cases in theoretical categories. Assign each of the following to one of these categories and justify your answer: (i) the extractive state of the institutional approach; (ii) the developmental state; (iii) the political motives of aid; (iv) Sen's account of famine; (v) the resource curse. (a) North Korea's extraction of the maximum aid from both sides of the Sino-Soviet split in the 1960s; (b) Belgian rule over the Congo; (c) South Korea's industrialization in the 1960s and 1970s, in which the state disciplined private firms while building up export industries; (d) North Korea's famine of the 1990s, in which the government did not request international food aid until 1995; (e) an authoritarian state that uses oil export revenue to keep taxes low and maintain its security forces.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. (a) A takes 70 ÷ 7 = 10 years, and B takes 70 ÷ 3.5 = 20 years.
  2. (b) Over 40 years, A doubles four times, to about $16,000, and B doubles twice, to about $8,000 (about $14,970 and $7,920 with exact compounding).
  3. (c) After t years, A's income is 1,000 × 2^(t/10) and B's is 2,000 × 2^(t/20). Setting the two equal gives 2^(t/10 − t/20) = 2, so t/20 = 1, and A catches up after about 20 years (about 21 years, more precisely).

Problem 2.

  1. (a) The government spends 10 of its own funds and the 10 in aid on health, keeping total health spending at 20, and shifts the 10 it saves to the military, which raises military spending to 40. What the aid actually increased was military spending, by 10.
  2. (b) Health spending is 20 + 3 = 23, and military spending is 30 + 7 = 37. Of the 10 in aid, the share that increased health spending is 3, or 30 percent.
  3. (c) Not the project the aid paid for, but health spending in the absence of aid (20).

Problem 3.

  1. (a) (iii). The patrons used aid as an instrument of strategic competition.
  2. (b) (i). Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson cite it as a textbook example of an extractive state.
  3. (c) (ii). Amsden saw the state's discipline of rent seeking by firms as a condition for South Korea's rapid growth.
  4. (d) (iv). That a one-party state waited until 1995 to request international aid despite food shortages is consistent with Sen's argument that without elections and public criticism, the incentive to prevent famine is weak (this lecture's interpretation).
  5. (e) (v). This is the path by which oil revenue keeps taxes low and builds up the security apparatus, which Michael L. Ross tested, as discussed below.

Evidence and counterarguments

Albouy (2012) and Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2012): are the mortality data reliable? David Y. Albouy reported that 36 of the 64 countries in the sample had been assigned mortality rates from other countries, often on mistaken or conflicting grounds. He also pointed out that the data mixed mortality rates for groups that are hard to compare, such as laborers, bishops, and soldiers on campaign, and found that once these problems are corrected, the confidence intervals for the instrumental-variable estimates are often infinite. Acemoglu and his coauthors countered that Albouy had discarded about 60 percent of the data, including all of Latin America and much of Africa, and that reducing the influence of outliers actually strengthens their results. The limitation is that no data exist to replace the old mortality records (this lecture's interpretation).

Nunn and Qian (2014) and Christian and Barrett (2024): does food aid fuel civil war? Exploiting the fact that the volume of U.S. food aid varies with U.S. wheat production, Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian reported that increases in aid raise both the incidence and the duration of civil conflict in recipient countries. Paul Christian and Christopher B. Barrett pointed out that civil conflict, U.S. wheat production, and interest rates all followed the same inverted-U trend, and that when trends coincide, even an unrelated instrument can appear strong. In the working-paper version of their study, correcting for nonstationarity in the time series turned the estimated effect of aid negative and statistically insignificant. The limitation is that both studies deal with food aid, so their findings cannot be carried over directly to medical aid (this lecture's interpretation).

Ross (2001) and Haber and Menaldo (2011): does oil hinder democracy? Using data on 113 countries for 1971–1997, Ross reported that oil and mineral exports are strongly associated with authoritarianism even outside the Middle East, whereas other commodity exports are not. He also found some support for each of three mechanisms: a rentier effect, in which low taxes and patronage dampen pressure for democratization; a repression effect, in which the security apparatus grows; and a modernization effect, in which social change is slowed. Stephen Haber and Victor Menaldo argued that earlier regressions without country fixed effects miss characteristics that differ from country to country, and used long historical data series to examine change within countries. Rising resource dependence did not strengthen authoritarianism, and many of their models even showed a "resource blessing." The difference comes down to whether one compares countries with one another or follows change within a single country (this lecture's interpretation).
