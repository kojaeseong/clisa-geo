The question for this session

The bargaining model in session 5 treated the state as a single rational actor. Yet states facing the same structure make different choices depending on who is making the decision. This session asks three questions. Do leaders misread their adversaries in predictable ways? Do people take bigger gambles when they stand to lose? And what kinds of leaders, judged by their backgrounds, start conflicts most often? Finally, it applies these theories to four decision-makers in 2026. It does not diagnose anyone's psychology; instead, it states in advance what each theory predicts and which signals would refute it.

1. Key concept: the return of the individual

Kenneth Waltz's first image, introduced in session 1, looks for the causes of war in human nature or in individual leaders. In their 2001 article "Let Us Now Praise Great Men," Daniel Byman and Kenneth M. Pollack observed that political scientists had largely written individuals out of their explanations; they tested the usual arguments for why individuals matter little against five cases and rejected them. The individual level has come back by three routes: the way leaders take in information (perception), regularities in how people make risky choices, and the experiences and beliefs leaders accumulate before they come to power.

2. Jervis: misperception follows patterns

In Perception and Misperception in International Politics (1976), Robert Jervis, whom session 2 introduced, argued that the misperceptions of policymakers are not random errors but follow recurring patterns.

Cognitive consistency. People fit new information to the images they already hold and do not readily abandon those images even when the contrary evidence is clear. They also tend to believe that the policy they favor is superior on many unrelated criteria at once, which keeps them from weighing what they stand to gain against what they stand to lose (chapter 4) [secondary source].

Misusing the lessons of history. Analogies are shortcuts that let decision-makers borrow insights from earlier events, but they obscure how the present case differs. Jervis identified several variables that determine which events become lessons: whether the decision-maker experienced the event firsthand, whether it came early in his or her career, and whether it had major consequences for the person or the country. Because such lessons are drawn from few cases and are rarely tested, they are overgeneralized, and people remember the outcome while paying little attention to its causes (chapter 6) [secondary source].

Seeing the adversary as more unified and calculating than it is. Decision-makers tend to see an adversary's behavior as more centrally planned and coordinated than it really is. They read accidents, unintended consequences, and chance as design, and they overestimate how firmly the adversary's leaders control their own government (chapter 8). A related tendency is to see the adversary's actions as aimed at oneself while discounting the possibility that one's own actions provoked them (chapter 9) [secondary source].

Groups can amplify misperception. In Victims of Groupthink (1972), Irving L. Janis used the term groupthink for the process by which a highly cohesive group reaches consensus prematurely and then closes its eyes to alternatives. He cited Pearl Harbor (1941), the Korean War (1950), the Bay of Pigs invasion (1961), and the Johnson administration's escalation of the Vietnam War as failures, and the Marshall Plan and the Cuban missile crisis (1962) as counterexamples [secondary source].

3. Prospect theory: bigger gambles in the face of loss

Expected utility theory in economics holds that people choose on the basis of final outcomes and their probabilities. In "Prospect Theory," published in Econometrica in 1979, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky demonstrated a different regularity experimentally. Offered a choice between an 80 percent chance of receiving 4,000 and a sure 3,000, 80 percent of 95 respondents chose the sure thing. When the same numbers were turned into losses, 92 percent chose the gamble: an 80 percent chance of losing 4,000. This pattern of avoiding risk when facing gains and accepting it when facing losses is called the reflection effect (p. 268).

People value outcomes not as final states but as gains and losses measured from a reference point, and they feel a loss more keenly than a gain of the same size (loss aversion). The value function captures this. A position better than the reference point lies in the domain of gains; a position worse than it lies in the domain of losses.

In two articles published in Political Psychology in 1992, Jack S. Levy introduced the theory and laid out the issues that arise in applying it to international politics. The core hypothesis of this line of work is that states take greater risks to avoid losses than to make gains. In "Why Hawks Win," a 2007 article assigned in both model courses, Kahneman and Jonathan Renshon argued that every bias on the list they had drawn from 40 years of psychological research favors hawks. One example: loss aversion makes it hard to end a war even after the outcome is all but decided [secondary source].

4. Who are the leaders? Research on leader attributes

In a 2014 article, Michael C. Horowitz and Allan C. Stam, drawing on a dataset they built of more than 2,500 national leaders from 1875 to 2004, reported that leaders who had served in the military without seeing combat, and leaders who had been rebels, were the most likely to initiate militarized disputes and wars. In Why Leaders Fight (2015), written with Cali M. Ellis, they reported that leaders with combat experience tended, if anything, to be more cautious, and that older leaders, especially those 70 or older, were more inclined toward military action [secondary source].

In Leaders at War (2011), Elizabeth N. Saunders argued that "internally focused" presidents, who see threats as arising from other states' domestic regimes, choose interventions that change the target state's institutions, whereas "externally focused" presidents, who see threats as arising from other states' foreign policies, choose interventions that leave those institutions alone. Kennedy exemplifies the first type; Eisenhower and Johnson, the second [secondary source].

5. A classic: Thucydides and the Sicilian expedition

In 415 BC, the Athenian assembly voted to launch an expedition to Sicily. Nicias, who opposed it, calculated that if he greatly inflated the size of the force required, the assembly would balk at the scale and change its mind. Thucydides wrote that the effect was the opposite: everyone alike fell in love with the enterprise. The older men hoped for conquest, those in their prime for the sight of distant lands, and the mass of the people and the soldiers for immediate pay (6.20–26). The 60 warships first approved grew to 134, along with 5,100 hoplites (6.43), and the expedition ended with the destruction of the Athenian force in 413 BC. Thucydides' account, in which what each group wanted shaped what it expected, fits the theme of Jervis's chapter 10: desires and fears alter perception (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Historical cases

The Iran hostage rescue mission, 1980. Sixty-six Americans were taken hostage at the U.S. Embassy in Tehran on November 4, 1979; 53 were still being held in April 1980. The rescue mission that President Jimmy Carter approved for April 24–25 (Operation Eagle Claw) was aborted when only five helicopters were usable at the staging site, one short of the six the mission required. During the withdrawal, a collision killed eight U.S. servicemen [secondary source]. In 1998, Rose McDermott analyzed this as a decision made in the domain of losses: a gamble with a high probability of failure, taken in the hope of recouping larger losses (p. 11). She also argued that the same Carter, while in the domain of gains from January to October 1979, kept the exiled shah of Iran from entering the United States, but after shifting into the domain of losses chose the riskier course of letting him in.

7. Applying it to 2026: four decision-makers

The facts. For each decision-maker, the Power Structures tab of Clisa Geopolitics presents hypotheses drawn from that leader's decision record, along with forecasts that test them (as of October 2, 2026).

Putin: where is the reference point? As early as a private meeting with President George W. Bush in 2001, President Vladimir Putin described the breakup of the Soviet Union as a loss in which Russia had given Ukraine away, and in 2022, when Russia annexed four Ukrainian regions, he declared that their residents were Russian citizens forever. Clisa Geopolitics judges that the ban on ceding territory in Russia's 2020 constitutional amendments extends to the annexed territories. In a Levada Center poll in September 2025, 80 percent of respondents supported ending the war immediately, but support fell to 33 percent if the return of the annexed territories were a condition. If the annexed land has become the reference point, any concession becomes a loss, and prospect theory predicts that Russia will keep fighting to avoid it (this lecture's interpretation). Yet despite his warning in September 2022 that Russia would use all available means, Putin responded to the Ukrainian incursion into Kursk Oblast in August 2024 with a conventional counteroffensive and the deployment of North Korean troops, and he has avoided direct combat with NATO. Prospect theory does not say which risks a leader will choose to run.

Kim Jong Un: the lessons of history. Two days after North Korea's fourth nuclear test (January 6, 2016), a commentary from the Korean Central News Agency (KCNA) argued that the regimes of Saddam Hussein and Muammar Qaddafi, having voluntarily abandoned the foundations of their nuclear programs, could not escape ruin. In March 2026, after the U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran, Kim Jong Un vowed to make North Korea's status as a nuclear weapons state irreversible. Jervis held that lessons drawn from events with major consequences are overgeneralized (chapter 6) and that evidence inconsistent with existing images is accepted only slowly (chapter 4). But Jervis held that events experienced firsthand leave stronger lessons, so this framework alone may not fully explain why lessons drawn from what happened to other countries have persisted for so long (this lecture's interpretation). Meanwhile, Kim has prepared, but held back from, steps that would be costly to his relations with China and the United States, such as a seventh nuclear test. That restraint can be read as the caution of a leader protecting a status he has already secured (this lecture's interpretation).

Trump: seeing the adversary as a single calculation. On September 10, President Donald Trump said that Iran was holding out in order to influence the U.S. midterm elections, and in late September he rejected Iran's proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days. Iran lost its supreme leader, Ali Khamenei, to air strikes in February 2026. Read against Jervis's chapter 8, an explanation that treats the holdout of such a government as a single calculated plan is the first that should be questioned, whether or not it proves correct (this lecture's interpretation). If Iran's stance remains unchanged after the November 3 midterms, that explanation will be weakened. On the question of risk-taking, Trump has chosen standoff means such as air strikes and targeted killings when using force, and has avoided seizing territory with ground troops.

Xi Jinping: predictions that depend on the reference point. In 1990, as a local official, Xi Jinping wrote about regional development in gradualist terms, invoking the image of dripping water that eventually wears through stone. In January 2021, he judged that time and momentum were on China's side. Abroad, too, he has built up pressure incrementally, and he has withdrawn economic instruments such as rare-earth export controls only through time-limited suspensions that leave their legal basis in place. If the status quo is the reference point, China is in the domain of gains amid a favorable trend, and this behavior fits the prediction that it will avoid risk. If unification is the reference point, however, the status quo itself is a loss, and the prediction changes (this lecture's interpretation).

Assessment. The behavior of all four leaders broadly fits the theories in this session, but it can also be explained by rational calculation. The independent explanatory power of psychological theories has to be tested where their predictions and those of rational choice diverge: in behavior after a reference point or a trend has shifted. This assessment should be revisited if Russia signs a ceasefire along the entire current line of contact while Ukrainian forces hold part of Donetsk Oblast, or if China takes a large gamble, such as a blockade of Taiwan, even though the situation around Taiwan is moving in its favor.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows prospect theory in the original, uses the value function to work out choices by hand, and looks at research that has tested the theory and research on leaders' backgrounds.

Reading the original

Passage 1. Losses loom larger than gains

"A salient characteristic of attitudes to changes in welfare is that losses loom larger than gains." (Kahneman and Tversky 1979, p. 279)

The argument begins from the premise that people value changes, not final states: what people attach value to, the authors wrote, is not the final state of wealth or welfare but changes in it (p. 277). To this they added two observations: the pain of losing a sum of money appears greater than the pleasure of gaining the same amount, and most people find a bet offering an even chance of winning or losing the same amount distinctly unattractive, the more so the larger the stakes (p. 279). The sentence above is the conclusion, and it is why the losses side of the value function is steeper. The conclusion assumes that the reference point is clear. As Levy pointed out in 1997, experiments supply a baseline in the form of a certain status quo, but in foreign policy riskless options are rare, and what counts as the reference point is itself open to dispute [secondary source].

Passage 2. Identical in terms of final states

"Note, however, that when viewed in terms of final states, the two choice problems are identical." (Kahneman and Tversky 1979, p. 273)

In problem 11, respondents were first given 1,000 and then asked to choose between a 50 percent chance of gaining another 1,000 and a sure gain of 500; 84 percent of the 70 respondents chose the sure gain. In problem 12, respondents were first given 2,000 and then asked to choose between a 50 percent chance of losing 1,000 and a sure loss of 500; 69 percent of the 68 respondents chose the gamble (p. 273). In both problems, the final states are an even chance of 2,000 or 1,000 versus a sure 1,500. Expected utility theory implies the same choice in both, yet the choices were reversed, which leads to the conclusion that people treat the money they were first given as the reference point and attend only to changes from it. The argument assumes that choices made in an experiment can stand in for real decisions. Because foreign policy decisions involve high stakes, are made by groups, and are made against an adversary, Levy (1997) and Boettcher (1995) pointed out the limits of carrying such results over to foreign policy.

Working it out

The problems below use the value function from the figure in the main text: v(x) = x^0.88 for gains and v(x) = −2.25 × (−x)^0.88 for losses. To keep the arithmetic simple, the probabilities themselves serve as decision weights. Reference values: v(10) = 7.6, v(20) = 13.9, v(50) = 31.3, v(100) = 57.5. The cost of war is set at 0.

Problem 1. Gain frame and loss frame. A state is choosing what to do about disputed territory worth 100. (a) In a frame in which it sees itself as currently holding nothing, it can gain 50 for certain through negotiation, or use force and have a 50 percent chance of gaining 100 and a 50 percent chance of gaining nothing. (b) In a frame in which it sees itself as already holding all 100, it can lose 50 for certain through negotiation, or use force and have a 50 percent chance of losing 100 and a 50 percent chance of losing nothing. For each frame, find the expected value and the prospect value of both options, and say which option the state chooses.

Problem 2. When the reference point moves. Last year, state A seized 20 units of state B's territory. Under a proposed ceasefire, A would keep 10 and return 10. If the war continues, A has a 50 percent chance of keeping all 20 and a 50 percent chance of losing all of it. (a) If A's reference point remains where it was before the seizure (0), what does A choose? (b) What if A has grown used to the seized land and its reference point has shifted to 20? (c) B's reference point is where it stood before the seizure (holding 20). What does B choose? Taking (b) and (c) together, what can be said about the prospects for a ceasefire?

Problem 3. Placing statements among the patterns of misperception. Match each of the following statements, (a) and (b), to Jervis's patterns and give your reasons. (a) Immediately after the Beslan hostage crisis in 2004, President Putin said that Russia had shown weakness and that the weak get beaten, and that outside forces wanted to tear off a juicier piece of Russia. (b) In a speech in March 2023, President Xi explicitly pointed to Western countries led by the United States as the source of all-around containment of China. (c) What would have to be established before these statements could be called misperceptions?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. The expected value of both options is 50 in (a) and −50 in (b), and the final states are also the same: a sure 50 versus an even chance of 100 or 0.
  2. (a) The sure option is worth v(50) = 31.3; the gamble, 0.5 × 57.5 = 28.8. The state chooses the sure negotiated outcome.
  3. (b) The sure option is worth −2.25 × 31.3 = −70.4; the gamble, 0.5 × (−2.25 × 57.5) = −64.7. The state chooses the gamble of using force. Only the frame changed, yet the choice reversed, so whether a state already regards the territory as its own determines whether negotiation succeeds (this lecture's interpretation).

Problem 2.

  1. (a) The ceasefire is a gain of 10, worth 7.6; war is worth 0.5 × 13.9 ≈ 7.0. A chooses the ceasefire.
  2. (b) The ceasefire is a loss of 10, worth −2.25 × 7.6 = −17.1; war is worth 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × (−2.25 × 13.9) ≈ −15.6. A keeps fighting.
  3. (c) For B, the ceasefire is a loss of 10 (−17.1), while war offers a 50 percent chance of losing nothing and a 50 percent chance of losing 20, for a value of −15.6. B keeps fighting, too.
  4. Although the ceasefire and the war have the same expected value, when both sides are in the domain of losses, both choose the gamble. If, as Levy (1997) summarized, the reference point shifts quickly after gains but only slowly after losses [secondary source], the period just after territory has changed hands is an especially difficult time to reach a ceasefire (this lecture's interpretation).

Problem 3.

  1. (a) Seeing many separate outside actions as a single plan aimed at Russia comes close to the patterns of chapters 8 and 9; drawing from one event the lesson that weakness invites attack comes close to the pattern of chapter 6 (this lecture's interpretation).
  2. (b) Bundling the separate measures of many countries into a single containment effort under one command comes close to the pattern of chapter 8 (this lecture's interpretation).
  3. (c) If the other side really was acting in concert, the perception was accurate, so one would have to show a gap between perception and reality. Jervis's patterns only indicate where suspicion is warranted.

Evidence and counterarguments

McDermott (1998): the same president behaved differently in different positions. McDermott compared Carter's two decisions (section 6 of the main text) with two of Eisenhower's: the U-2 incident and the 1956 Suez crisis. She determined each president's domain from memoirs, interviews, opinion polls, the number of times Congress overrode a presidential veto, and other international events (p. 10). She found that after the U-2 was shot down, Eisenhower, in the domain of losses, took the risk of issuing a false public explanation, whereas at Suez, in the domain of gains, he chose the sure gain of winning over opinion in the Third World (pp. 11–12). McDermott treated risk propensity as a product of a leader's situation rather than of personality (pp. 2, 4). The limitation, as she acknowledged, is that the domain is hard to determine, and there is a risk of classifying cases with the outcome already known (this lecture's interpretation).

Boettcher (1995) and Levy (1997): hard to take outside the laboratory. After reviewing studies that applied prospect theory to international politics, William A. Boettcher III ran an experiment that presented probabilities in words rather than numbers. The results supported prospect theory only partially and varied with the set of verbal probability expressions used. He called for more attention to how probability information reaches decision-makers. Because the findings come from a single experiment, however, they are of limited generality (this lecture's interpretation). In 1997, Levy argued that the theory's problems of external validity are greater than its problems of internal validity, and he proposed two questions for research: how decisions come to be framed, and how individual loss aversion plays out in group decisions [secondary source].

Horowitz and Stam (2014): background rather than situation. Horowitz and Stam extended Archigos, a dataset of national leaders, coded leaders' military backgrounds, and tested the hypothesis that military experience changes leaders' judgments about the utility of force. The results are those described in section 4 of the main text. Unlike McDermott, they locate attitudes toward force not in a leader's situation but in the experience the leader has accumulated (this lecture's interpretation). Reviewers criticized the approach for failing to measure decisive variables such as ideology and noted cases, such as that of British Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher, in which the indicators and actual behavior diverge [secondary source]. Rather than competing, the two approaches can be seen as complementary: a leader's situation operates on top of a baseline disposition shaped by experience (this lecture's interpretation).
