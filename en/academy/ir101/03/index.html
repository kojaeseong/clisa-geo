The question for this session

In the fall of 2026, both the United States and Iran want the Strait of Hormuz reopened. Yet it remains closed, and the phased agreement the two sides signed in June collapsed within three weeks. If both want the same outcome, why does the strait stay shut? This session introduces three analytical concepts for answering that question: what actors want (interests), how their choices combine (interactions), and the rules that structure how they combine (institutions).

1. Key concept: actors and interests

In their textbook World Politics: Interests, Interactions, Institutions, the American political scientists Jeffry A. Frieden, David A. Lake, and Kenneth A. Schultz explain outcomes in international politics as the product of these three elements. The starting point is the actor. States are actors, but so are domestic groups such as political parties, firms, bureaucracies, and militaries; individuals such as presidents and supreme leaders; and multinational corporations and transnational advocacy groups. When domestic groups want different things, the state can no longer be treated as a single actor and has to be disaggregated.

An interest is what an actor seeks to gain through political action—its preferences over the possible outcomes. The interests most commonly attributed to states are security, economic prosperity, power, status, and the promotion of an ideology or values. In his 1999 essay "Actors and Preferences in International Relations," Frieden distinguished between a preference, which is a ranking of outcomes, and a strategy, which is the means an actor chooses to get as close as it can to those outcomes given its environment and the preferences of others. Because observed behavior reflects both preferences and the environment, inferring preferences from behavior alone can easily mislead [secondary source].

2. Interactions: strategic choice, cooperation, and bargaining

An interaction is the way the choices of two or more actors combine to produce a political outcome. Most interactions in international politics are strategic. Because the result of one actor's choice depends on what others choose, each anticipates what the others will do and picks its best response. In Strategic Choice and International Relations (1999), a volume they edited, Lake and Robert Powell proposed taking the strategic interaction of actors, rather than particular states or policies, as the unit of analysis. Since no single state's choice determines the outcome, what needs explaining is how choices fit together. The actors themselves—states, parties, firms, or individuals—are specified as the problem requires, and the analyst then makes what Lake and Powell call methodological "bets" about the most productive ways to analyze the interaction.

Interactions come in two kinds. Cooperation means moving together toward an outcome that leaves at least one party better off without leaving anyone worse off. Bargaining is a matter of distribution, in which one side's gain is the other's loss. Most real problems mix the two: a trade agreement benefits both countries (cooperation), but which tariffs to cut and by how much is a question of dividing the gains (bargaining). Bargaining power comes from coercion, which imposes costs on the other side; from an outside option, which an actor can fall back on if talks fail; and from agenda setting, which determines what is discussed first. Why bargaining sometimes ends in war even when there is a bargaining range—a set of deals both sides prefer to war—is the subject of session 5, which covers information problems, commitment problems, and indivisible issues.

3. What makes cooperation hard

Cooperation that would benefit everyone does not happen automatically. The first obstacle is the problem of collective action, examined in detail in section 5. The second is the difficulty of monitoring. If states cannot verify whether others are keeping their promises, they may decline to cooperate at all, fearing that they alone will pay for keeping their word. The third is the incentive to defect. Where an actor gains more by breaking the rules while others keep them, the temptation persists even when compliance can be verified. The prisoner's dilemma and the "shadow of the future," both discussed in session 2, concern this problem.

4. What institutions do

An institution is a set of rules, known and shared by the actors concerned, that structures how they interact [secondary source]. Institutions include not only organizations but also treaties and established practices, and they reduce the obstacles to cooperation in four ways.

  • Setting standards. Defining in advance what counts as keeping or breaking a commitment makes violations visible and leaves less room for excuses.
  • Monitoring and verification. A third party checks compliance, easing the difficulty of monitoring.
  • Dispute resolution. Procedures for settling disagreements over interpretation keep small disputes from bringing down the whole agreement.
  • Decision-making rules. Rules such as voting procedures and vetoes lower the cost of reaching joint decisions.

Institutions are not neutral. Because they embody the outcome of the bargaining that created them, they tend to favor those who wrote the original rules, as the veto held by the five permanent members of the UN Security Council illustrates. The debate between Robert O. Keohane, who held that institutions provide information and lower transaction costs (session 1), and John J. Mearsheimer, who countered that institutions merely reflect the distribution of power, is taken up in detail in session 9.

5. A classic: Olson's The Logic of Collective Action

In The Logic of Collective Action (1965), the American economist Mancur Olson argued that a shared interest does not by itself lead people to act together. When a good is a public good—one from which no one can easily be excluded—each individual has an incentive to free ride, relying on others' contributions without paying the cost. The larger the group, the less any one member's contribution affects the outcome, the smaller the share of the benefit that member receives, and the less anyone notices a failure to contribute. Collective action therefore requires a small group, compulsory contributions, or selective incentives—rewards that go only to those who contribute [secondary source].

In 1966, Olson and Richard Zeckhauser applied this logic to alliances. If an alliance's security is a public good, the larger members will bear a disproportionate share of the burden of common defense (p. 268). The argument rests on the assumption that no member can be excluded from the benefit. If particular members can be excluded, free riding becomes harder, and whoever holds the power to exclude gains bargaining leverage (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Historical cases

The Algiers Accords of 1981. The accords, concluded through Algerian mediation on January 19, 1981, ended the hostage crisis that began with the seizure of the U.S. embassy in Tehran in November 1979. The two governments moved Iranian assets into escrow accounts in a third country and had the Central Bank of Algeria confirm simultaneously that the hostages had been released and the assets deposited. This solved the problem of monitoring and verification, and 52 Americans were freed. Remaining disputes over claims were referred to a newly created Iran–United States Claims Tribunal. The tribunal, based in The Hague, was composed of three judges appointed by each government and three from third countries, and it received about 3,800 claims by the filing deadline of January 19, 1982. Assigning dispute resolution to a separate body kept the remaining disputes from unsettling the agreement on the hostages' release (this lecture's interpretation).

The 2015 Iran nuclear deal (JCPOA). Concluded on July 14, 2015, by Iran, the P5+1 (the five permanent members of the UN Security Council plus Germany), and the European Union, the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action performed all four institutional functions. On standards, it capped uranium enrichment at 3.67 percent and stocks of low-enriched uranium at under 300 kilograms for 15 years. Monitoring and verification fell to the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), and sanctions were lifted only after the IAEA confirmed, on January 16, 2016, that Iran had taken its key steps. Disputes were to pass through a Joint Commission under a procedure lasting roughly 35 days. The voting rule was unusual. If a participant notified the Security Council of significant non-performance, the council would vote on a resolution to continue the termination of sanctions; if that resolution was not adopted, the earlier sanctions would return after 30 days (a provision limited to 10 years). Under this "snapback" mechanism, a single permanent member's veto would bring sanctions back rather than block them (treating this as a decision-making rule is this lecture's classification). The United States nevertheless withdrew from the deal on May 8, 2018. Because the State Department had characterized it as a set of political commitments—neither a treaty nor an executive agreement—it did not bind the next administration.

7. Applying it to 2026: the Iran war and the Strait of Hormuz

The facts. The war that began with U.S. and Israeli air strikes on Iran on February 28, 2026, has lasted more than seven months, and Mojtaba Khamenei has succeeded his father, Ali Khamenei, who was killed. After the April ceasefire and the memorandum of understanding of June 17 effectively collapsed on July 8 (the memorandum formally expired on August 17), the United States resumed its naval blockade, and Iran has restricted shipping through the strait by attacking merchant vessels and laying mines. Iran lets through ships from countries it considers friendly, such as China and India, after coordination or payment of a transit fee ($2 million per ship in one reported case), while blocking ships from the United States, Israel, and their Western allies. In late September, President Donald Trump rejected a roadmap Iran had put forward on September 25 for a seven-day ceasefire and the reopening of the strait. Iran insists that the war end on all fronts, including Lebanon, that its frozen assets be returned, and that the blockade be lifted first; the United States insists that Iran first renounce nuclear weapons and that freedom of navigation through the strait be restored. Seventy percent of South Korea's crude oil imports pass through the strait. On September 18, South Korean President Lee Jae-myung said that South Korea would not send troops to take part or intervene in the war, and the presidential office said this was not a rejection of the U.S. request concerning Hormuz. Expanding the mission of the Cheonghae Unit, a South Korean naval unit operating off the Arabian Peninsula, is being discussed as a leading option.

Through the lens of interests. Control of the strait is Iran's strategy, not its preference (this lecture's interpretation). The Strategic Analysis tab of Clisa Geopolitics identifies the survival of the Islamic Republic as Iran's first-ranked goal and holds that the strait gives Iran more leverage when it is opened selectively than when it is closed. The two countries' interests overlap on reopening the strait, but on the nuclear issue the U.S. goal of eliminating Iran's nuclear capability collides with Iran's goal of keeping the right to enrich. Domestic variables—Israel's general election on October 27, the U.S. midterm elections in November, and the legitimacy of Mojtaba Khamenei—also shape the choices of each country's leadership.

Through the lens of interactions. Where interests overlap, there is room for cooperation; what each side concedes first is a matter of bargaining. The U.S. blockade and air strikes are coercion, the transit fees paid by ships of friendly countries are Iran's outside option, and whether the nuclear issue goes first on the agenda is a contest over agenda setting (this lecture's interpretation). Restored freedom of navigation would come close to being a public good from which no one can easily be excluded. For now, however, by permitting or blocking passage country by country, Iran has turned passage into an excludable good and uses it as leverage (this lecture's interpretation). Drawing on Olson and Zeckhauser, Clisa Geopolitics holds that South Korea's military contribution would affect the politics of burden sharing more than the outcome of the war, though it treats this application as conditional. The drop in the Middle East's share of South Korea's crude oil imports over one year, from 65.2 percent to 53.1 percent (as of April 2026), has in effect widened South Korea's outside options in dealing with Iran over passage through the strait (this lecture's interpretation).

Through the lens of institutions. The June memorandum of understanding collapsed within three weeks because it did not make clear whether the Israel-Lebanon front fell within the scope of the ceasefire. This is a failure of standard setting (this lecture's interpretation). Clisa Geopolitics holds that for the next agreement to last, asset transfers routed through a third country must be carried out simultaneously with each side's steps, adjacent fronts such as Lebanon must be explicitly included, and implementation must proceed in phases tied to IAEA verification. The three conditions correspond, respectively, to Algiers-style simultaneous confirmation, standard setting, and monitoring and verification (this lecture's interpretation). At the same time, Clisa Geopolitics points out that these conditions mainly secure Iran's compliance and do nothing to prevent a U.S. reversal. Because they constrain only one side, the structure resembles the institutional bias discussed in section 4 (this lecture's interpretation).

Assessment. The deadlock over Hormuz did not arise because the two sides' interests are wholly incompatible. Their interests overlap on reopening the strait but collide on the nuclear issue, and the two countries have found neither a sequence for exchanging concessions on the overlapping part first nor a mechanism to sustain that sequence. Two causes can be identified (this lecture's interpretation). One is a divergence in how the two sides assess who is winning, evident in Washington's rejection of the roadmap in the belief that it was winning; this is the information problem examined in session 5. The other is the absence of institutions to sustain a sequenced exchange. Without standards, verification, and simultaneous implementation, even a newly signed agreement is likely to collapse as the June agreement did. South Korea, which is not a party to the conflict, can do less to change the outcome than to reduce its own costs by widening its outside options. This assessment should be revisited if a comprehensive agreement emerges that is not divided into phases, if Iran makes the first concession on the nuclear issue, or if a phased agreement concluded without simultaneous third-country implementation and IAEA verification holds for at least 90 days.

Discussion questions

  1. By permitting passage country by country, Iran turns freedom of navigation from a public good into an excludable good. Does this work to South Korea's advantage or disadvantage?
  2. Could the June 2026 memorandum of understanding have held if it had explicitly covered the Lebanon front? What other institutional elements were missing?
  3. The United States is asking for contributions to securing the strait, and Iran can single out contributing countries for pressure. What kind of contribution can South Korea choose?

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows Olson and Zeckhauser's theory of alliances in the original, works through free riding, bargaining, and monitoring with numbers, and looks at research that has put the theory to the test.

Reading the original

Passage 1. Small allies rely on the spending of large ones

"There will also be a tendency for the 'larger' members ... to bear a disproportionate share of the burden" (Olson and Zeckhauser 1966, p. 268)

In the words left out, the authors define the "larger" members as "those that place a higher absolute value on the public good." The premise is that an alliance's deterrent is a public good from which no member can be excluded. Each nation takes its allies' spending as given and spends on defense only up to the point at which the value it gains from additional defense equals the cost. If a large nation already spends enough, the authors' hypothetical small nation, Little Atlantis, is better off spending nothing on defense at all (pp. 269–270). The authors measured size by gross national product (GNP) and tested the theory on 1964 data for 14 NATO members (Iceland excluded). The Spearman rank correlation coefficient between the members' rankings by GNP and their rankings by defense spending as a share of GNP was 0.490, significant at the 5 percent level on a one-tailed test (p. 275).

Later researchers attacked the assumption of a pure public good. The authors themselves acknowledged that when members have interests unrelated to the alliance, such as the forces Portugal was using to suppress the independence movement in Angola, both the degree of underprovision and the disproportion in burdens decline (p. 272). Todd Sandler (1977) and others built on this caveat to develop a joint-product model, in which defense produces both a public good, deterrence, and private benefits enjoyed only by the nation that provides it. The larger the private benefits, the more small nations spend on their own account, which weakens the exploitation hypothesis: the claim that small allies ride on the spending of large ones.

Passage 2. Alliances always underprovide defense

"The alliance output will always be suboptimal so long as the members of the alliance place a positive value on additional units of defense." (Olson and Zeckhauser 1966, p. 271)

Each nation weighs only its own gain from additional defense against the cost and leaves its allies' gains out of the calculation. For the alliance as a whole to reach the optimum, members would have to keep spending until the combined value to all of them equaled the cost, so the level at which each nation stops on its own falls short of that point. In periods of all-out war or exceptional insecurity, when nations devote all of their additional income to defense, the disproportion in burdens disappears (p. 270), but the authors wrote that underprovision has no such exception (p. 271). They argued that the optimum could be reached if members shared the cost of additional spending in proportion to the additional benefits each receives (pp. 271, 278), and they recommended marginal cost-sharing arrangements like the one used to finance NATO infrastructure (p. 279). Underprovision is the result of each nation deciding on its own, and a cost-sharing rule changes the terms of that decision.

Working it out

Problem 1. Public goods and free riding. Five countries, A through E, each decide on their own whether to send one ship to patrol the strait. Sending a ship costs the sending country 10, and each additional ship gives every one of the five countries a benefit of 4. (a) However many ships the other countries send, by how much does a country's net payoff change if it sends a ship rather than staying out? What is the equilibrium? (b) Compare each country's payoff when all five send a ship with its payoff when none do. (c) What is the equilibrium if country A alone gains 12 from each ship, while the others still gain 4?

Problem 2. Outside options. If two countries reach an agreement, they divide 100 between them. If talks break down, country A gets 20 and country B gets 10 (their outside options). Assume that they split equally the gains that agreement creates beyond their outside options. (a) What is the range of possible shares for country A, and what is the outcome of the agreement? (b) What happens if country B secures another supplier and its outside option rises to 40? (c) What happens if the outside options are 60 for country A and 50 for country B?

Problem 3. Monitoring and the expected value of cheating. A country that secretly violates an agreement gains an extra 30; if the violation is detected, sanctions return and it loses 100. The probability of detection is p. Assume that the country keeps the extra 30 even if it is caught, that it cares only about expected value, and that it complies when the expected values are equal. (a) Does violating pay when p = 0.1? When inspections raise p to 0.5? What is the minimum p that deters a violation? (b) Suppose that even after a violation is detected, sanctions actually return with a probability of only 0.4 (because, for example, a single country can block them with a veto). What is the minimum p now?

Solutions

Problem 1.

  1. (a) It changes by 4 − 10 = −6. Whatever the other countries do, a country is better off not sending a ship (a dominant strategy), so the equilibrium is that no one sends one.
  2. (b) If all five send a ship, each country gets 5 × 4 − 10 = 10; if none do, each gets 0. Because a ship is worth 20 to the five countries together, more than its cost of 10, the optimum is for all five ships to go, yet the equilibrium is zero ships. This is the underprovision described in passage 2.
  3. (c) For country A, 12 − 10 = 2 > 0, so it sends a ship even if no one else does, and the others stay out. Country A gets 2, and each of the others gets 4 at no cost. This is what Olson called a privileged group, one in which a single member has an incentive to bear the cost alone, and it has the structure of passage 1, in which the large nation bears a disproportionate share of the burden.

Problem 2.

  1. (a) Country A must receive at least 20 and country B at least 10, so A's share can range from 20 to 90. The gains from agreement, 70, are split equally, giving A 55 and B 45.
  2. (b) The gains from agreement shrink to 40, so A gets 40 and B gets 60. A's own outside option is unchanged, yet its share falls by 15. This calculation is the same as the symmetric Nash bargaining solution, with the payoffs from breakdown as the disagreement point.
  3. (c) Because 60 + 50 = 110 exceeds 100, there is no bargaining range, and no agreement is reached.

Problem 3.

  1. (a) Violating yields an expected value of 30 − 100p; complying yields 0. When p = 0.1, the expected value of violating is 20 > 0, so the country violates; when p = 0.5, it is −20 < 0, so the country complies. The country complies whenever 30 − 100p ≤ 0, that is, when p ≥ 0.3; at exactly p = 0.3, it complies by assumption.
  2. (b) The expected value of violating becomes 30 − 40p, so p must be at least 0.75. The effects of monitoring and enforcement multiply, so if one is weak, the other must be much stronger. A design like that of the JCPOA, discussed in section 6, in which a veto brings sanctions back rather than blocking them, can be seen as a way of raising the 0.4 close to 1 (this lecture's interpretation).

Evidence and counterarguments

Two studies test Olson and Zeckhauser's burden-sharing hypothesis over different periods and with different methods, and they reach different conclusions.

Oneal (1990): the link between size and burden had weakened. John R. Oneal asked whether Olson's theory, which had explained differences in the defense burdens of NATO members well in the early Cold War, continued to hold. He found that the relationship between economic size and defense burden (military spending as a share of GDP) had weakened to the point of statistical insignificance, and he traced the change to the pursuit of private interests by Greece, Turkey, and Portugal and to growing cooperation among the other European members. In the regressions that tested this explanation, he converted each member's burden into an index relative to the NATO average for that year and dropped those three countries. He nevertheless concluded that NATO was a privileged group producing a relatively pure public good. A limitation is that the study looks only at defense spending and does not measure directly the benefits each member receives (this lecture's interpretation).

Sandler and Murdoch (2000): after the Cold War, members paid in line with their benefits. Sandler and James C. Murdoch assessed NATO burden sharing in the 1990s by two criteria: ability to pay and benefits received. Using data from 1988 to 1999 for the 15 members other than Iceland, they computed rank correlations between defense burden and GDP and compared each member's share of the alliance's defense burden with its shares of NATO's population, GDP, and exposed borders. For 1990–1999, they found no evidence that large members had carried the share of small ones; burdens broadly matched the benefits each member received. They judged that the joint-product model still fit after the Cold War and predicted that enlargement could widen the disparities again. Their conclusion differs from Oneal's, but because the two studies cover different periods and use different methods, it is hard to say outright which is right (this lecture's interpretation).
