The question for this session

On October 8, 2026, President Donald Trump said that he would not attack Iran before the midterm elections on November 3. When air strikes on Iran resume, in other words, may depend not only on what Iran does but also on the U.S. electoral calendar. Through session 5, this course treated the state as a single actor and looked at bargaining between states. This session goes inside the state. It examines how interest groups, political regimes, and ratification procedures shape foreign policy, and how well the hypothesis that leaders try to cover up crises at home with conflicts abroad actually holds up.

1. Key concept: looking inside the state

To use Waltz's classification from session 1, this session looks for causes in the second image: politics within the state. The starting point is that the leaders who set foreign policy are also politicians who must hold on to power at home. Even as they bargain with their counterparts abroad, they must win the support of legislators, voters, and interest groups. This session divides the pathways of domestic influence into three: interest groups with a large stake in policy; the political regime, which determines how leaders are chosen and replaced; and ratification procedures, through which agreements between states are accepted at home.

2. Interest groups: trade policy and security policy

As Olson's logic from session 3 suggests, when a policy's benefits are concentrated on a few and its costs are spread across many, the few find it easier to organize. Tariffs are the classic example. A tariff brings large gains to industries that compete with imports, while its costs are shared in small amounts by all consumers. Export industries, fearing retaliation by other countries, favor openness. In a 1988 article, Jeffry A. Frieden examined U.S. foreign economic policy from 1914 to 1940—a period in which the United States moved from high tariffs and an aversion to international cooperation after World War I to leadership of the international economic order from the late 1930s on—from the perspective of sectoral conflict among industries.

Security policy is no different. In Myths of Empire (1991), Jack Snyder tested three theories—realism, misperception, and domestic coalition politics—to explain why the great powers of the industrial age embarked on expansion they could not sustain. He argued that when narrow interest groups form a coalition through logrolling, each agreeing to support the others' demands, expansion that was no one's first priority becomes a policy that everyone supports, and the myths of expansion invented to hold the coalition together take on a life of their own [secondary source].

3. Political regimes and audience costs

The type of political regime changes the price that leaders pay at home. In his 1994 article "Domestic Political Audiences and the Escalation of International Disputes," James D. Fearon modeled a crisis as a public contest watched by domestic audiences. A leader who issues a public threat and then backs down pays a political price at home; this is known as an audience cost. Because the cost grows the longer a standoff lasts, holding firm is itself a signal of resolve. In his model, once a crisis is under way, differences in audience costs matter more than differences in military capabilities, and the side with the stronger domestic audience—a democracy, for example—is always less likely to back down. Democracies, he concluded, can therefore signal their intentions more credibly than authoritarian states can. Audience costs can be seen as a device through which leaders manufacture for themselves the costly signals discussed in session 5 (this lecture's interpretation).

Bruce Bueno de Mesquita and his colleagues proposed another link through selectorate theory. The selectorate is the group that has a say in choosing the leader; within it, the winning coalition is the group whose support the leader needs to stay in office. According to their 1999 article, leaders of democracies, whose winning coalitions are large, must keep their jobs through policy success, so they devote more resources to the wars they fight and choose only wars they expect to win. The authors used this logic to explain the democratic peace discussed in session 1.

4. Two-level games and the win-set

In a 1988 article, Robert D. Putnam proposed viewing the politics of many international negotiations as a two-level game. At Level I, each country's negotiators work out a tentative agreement; at Level II, groups within each country decide whether to ratify it. Putnam called the set of all Level I agreements that could win an up-or-down vote among domestic constituents the win-set. Because agreement is possible only where the two countries' win-sets overlap, the larger the win-sets, the easier agreement becomes.

A small win-set, however, can be an advantage at the bargaining table. Putnam gave as an example the line "I'd like to accept your proposal, but I could never get it accepted at home," and he quoted Thomas C. Schelling's observation that "the power of a negotiator often rests on a manifest inability to make concessions and meet demands." The side whose hands are tied gets more. This advantage arises when those who hold the power of ratification at home take a harder line toward the other side than the negotiator does. Narrow the win-set too far, though, and the agreement itself may collapse. Putnam identified three determinants of win-set size—domestic preferences and coalitions, ratification institutions, and negotiators' strategies—and he distinguished voluntary defection, in which a party deliberately reneges on an agreement, from involuntary defection, in which a party cannot keep its promise because ratification has failed.

5. The diversionary war hypothesis

The diversionary war hypothesis holds that leaders cornered by economic hardship or sagging support start conflicts abroad to divert public attention and reap a rally-round-the-flag effect. Since Jack S. Levy's 1989 review, researchers have often tested the hypothesis in the form that leaders at greater risk of losing office are more likely to initiate conflicts [secondary source]. Plausible as it sounds, war is a costly gamble for the leader who starts it. If selectorate theory is right, leaders of systems with large winning coalitions are more likely to lose office over a failed war.

6. A classic: Kant's fourth preliminary article and wars fought on credit

Session 1 looked at the definitive articles of Immanuel Kant's Perpetual Peace (1795) and at his prediction that citizens who would themselves bear the hardships of war would hesitate to start one. The fourth preliminary article of the same work takes up how a state's fiscal institutions can weaken that brake. It reads: "No public debts shall be incurred in connection with the external disputes of the state." Kant had no objection to borrowing for the domestic economy, such as building roads and new settlements or laying in stores against bad harvests. His target was a system of credit used in disputes between states. He called this system, which can grow without limit while avoiding any immediate demand for repayment, "a dangerous money power"—in effect, a war chest—and he held that war made easy in this way, combined with the inclination of those in power to wage it, posed a great obstacle to perpetual peace (Academy edition, vol. 8, pp. 345–346).

Citizens' consent restrains war only when citizens feel its costs right away. When a war is fought on borrowed money, its costs pass to future taxpayers, and the brake at home weakens. Fiscal institutions, in other words, change the price that domestic audiences put on war (this lecture's interpretation).

7. Historical cases

The Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930 and the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934. Signed by President Herbert Hoover on June 17, 1930, the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act began as a pledge to protect agriculture, but during Senate deliberations, senators traded votes on behalf of industries in their own states and ended up raising tariffs on more than 20,000 imported goods. Canada and a number of other countries responded with retaliatory tariffs [secondary source]. The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934 delegated authority to the president in advance, for a period fixed by Congress, to conclude tariff agreements with other countries and to proclaim the tariff changes needed to carry them out without further approval from Congress. Individual agreements required neither the consent of two-thirds of the Senate nor a separate vote in Congress. The act can be read as a case in which removing the domestic ratification stage reduced the room for interest groups to trade votes on each agreement (this lecture's interpretation).

The failed ratification of the Treaty of Versailles, 1919–1920. President Woodrow Wilson failed to win the consent of two-thirds of the Senate for the treaty, which contained the Covenant of the League of Nations. The Republican majority, led by Henry Cabot Lodge, demanded reservations that would prevent the League from drawing the United States into war without the consent of Congress, and Wilson refused them. On November 19, 1919, the Senate rejected the treaty, with reservations attached, 39–55; when it voted again on March 19, 1920, the tally was 49 in favor and 35 against, seven votes short of two-thirds [secondary source]. Putnam pointed out that some Americans opposed the treaty because it was too harsh and others because it was too lenient (p. 443). When opposition comes from both directions, a negotiator will have trouble winning ratification whichever way the agreement is revised. Because Wilson could not honor an agreement he had signed once ratification failed at home, the case can be read as an example of involuntary defection (this lecture's interpretation).

The Falklands War of 1982. Facing economic stagnation and mass protests against military rule, Argentina's military junta invaded the Falkland Islands on April 2, 1982. The prevailing assessment is that the government of Leopoldo Galtieri sought to stir up patriotism, divert attention from economic hardship and human rights abuses, and shore up its faltering legitimacy. Argentina surrendered on June 14, and the defeat hastened the junta's collapse and the return to civilian rule in 1983 [secondary source].

8. Applying it to 2026: the U.S. election clock, consent in South Korea's National Assembly, and the budget in Taiwan's Legislative Yuan

The facts. In 2026, the U.S. House of Representatives passed resolutions three times limiting the president's military action against Iran (the third time on September 15, by 220–204), but the Senate rejected such a resolution on September 24 by 49–50. To stop the president's military action in a legally binding form, Congress would have to override a presidential veto, which requires a two-thirds vote in both chambers. The National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) for fiscal year 2026 bars the use of funds to reduce U.S. Forces Korea (USFK) below 28,500 troops or to complete the transfer of wartime operational control (OPCON) in a way that departs from the plan agreed between Seoul and Washington (the restriction lifts 60 days after the secretary of defense issues a certification and submits an assessment). In South Korea, dispatching the armed forces abroad requires the consent of the National Assembly, the country's legislature (Constitution, Article 60(2)), and on September 10 the government told the National Assembly that no decision had been made on the contribution in the Strait of Hormuz, such as sending troops, that President Trump had requested. On September 18, President Lee Jae-myung said that South Korea would not send troops to take part or intervene in the war. Ruling-party lawmakers on the relevant committee said that consent from the National Assembly would not come easily unless the safety of South Korean citizens could be guaranteed. The ruling Democratic Party of Korea holds 161 of the 300 seats, a majority on its own. On May 8, Taiwan's Legislative Yuan cut the ceiling on the special defense budget requested by the government from NT$1.25 trillion to NT$780 billion. The provision specifying the items to be purchased passed 59–51 in the version proposed by the Kuomintang (KMT) and the Taiwan People's Party (TPP); the Legislative Yuan can cut the budget but cannot increase it (Constitution of the Republic of China, Article 70). On August 27, the Legislative Yuan passed a supplementary budget of NT$240 billion for drones and unmanned systems.

Through the lens of theory. The NDAA's USFK provision is a device by which Congress has removed in advance the option of a U.S. president using troop reductions as leverage. This reverses the advantage described in section 4: here Congress is friendlier to the other country than the president is, so outcomes unfavorable to South Korea drop out of the U.S. win-set, and the provision works in South Korea's favor in negotiations between Seoul and Washington (this lecture's interpretation). The South Korean government, for its part, can present the requirement of the National Assembly's consent as a small win-set in negotiations over a contribution in the Strait of Hormuz. Because the ruling party holds a majority on its own and the president has already ruled out sending combat troops, however, Washington will believe that the constraint really comes from the legislature only if opposition within the ruling party becomes visible (this lecture's interpretation). Clisa Geopolitics' Strategic Analysis tab holds that a noncombat expansion of the Cheonghae Unit, a South Korean naval unit on an antipiracy mission, is possible within the framework of the National Assembly's existing consent, but that sending combat troops would require new consent. In Taiwan, a Legislative Yuan controlled by the opposition set a special defense budget different from the government's proposal, and Clisa Geopolitics warns against a structure in which the resulting gap in deterrence rewards a China inclined to wait for Taiwan's 2028 elections. President Trump's statement that he would not attack before the election is a signal that runs opposite to one strand of the diversionary war hypothesis, which holds that leaders facing elections resort to force. With the U.S. naval blockade still in place, however, a single statement cannot test the hypothesis (this lecture's interpretation).

Assessment. In the second half of 2026, foreign policy on three issues—the Iran war, a South Korean contribution in the Strait of Hormuz, and Taiwan's defense budget—is being shaped as much by domestic veto players—the U.S. electoral calendar and Congress, the South Korean National Assembly's power of consent, and the Legislative Yuan's power over the budget—as by the balance of power between states. This assessment should be revisited if the United States launches large-scale air strikes on Iran before the midterm elections; if the National Assembly promptly approves the government's request for consent to a deployment without changing its terms, showing that the requirement of consent was no constraint at all in negotiations; or if, in reviewing the 2027 budget, the Legislative Yuan passes without cuts the defense budget proposed by the Executive Yuan, Taiwan's cabinet, which calls for an 18 percent increase over the previous year.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows Putnam's argument in the original, works through the win-set and audience costs with numbers, and looks at research that has put audience costs and the diversionary war hypothesis to the test.

Reading the original

Passage 1. Agreement requires overlapping win-sets

"Thus, agreement is possible only if those win-sets overlap, and the larger each win-set, the more likely they are to overlap." (Putnam 1988, p. 438)

The argument rests on two premises: that a tentative Level I agreement is ratified at each country's Level II only by an up-or-down vote, without amendment, and that the win-set is defined as the set of all agreements that would pass that vote (p. 437). Because any successful agreement must, by definition, fall within the win-set of every party (pp. 437–438), overlap is required. Within this framework, negotiations can fail not only because the other side cheats but also because ratification fails—that is, through involuntary defection (p. 438). The argument depends on the simplification of an up-or-down vote and on the assumption that negotiators faithfully represent the wishes of their domestic constituents. Yet the 1978 Bonn summit, Putnam's own example, was a case in which powerful minorities within each government used pressure from abroad to achieve policies they had wanted all along (pp. 428–429). Negotiators, in other words, can have preferences that differ from those of their constituents.

Passage 2. The weakness of a large win-set

"The larger the perceived win-set of a negotiator, the more he can be 'pushed around' by the other Level I negotiators." (Putnam 1988, p. 440)

If agreement is reached within the range where the two win-sets overlap, then the smaller one side's win-set, the further the overlap narrows toward the end that favors that side, so a small win-set becomes a bargaining advantage (p. 440). The key word is "perceived." If bargaining power comes from the size of the win-set the other side believes in, negotiators have an incentive to exaggerate their domestic constraints, and the other side will not readily take their word for it. The information problem of session 5 thus reappears at the domestic level (this lecture's interpretation). Schelling, whom Putnam quotes, also warned that such strategies risk staking out an immovable position that goes beyond what the other side is able to concede, inviting stalemate or breakdown (p. 440).

Working it out

Problem 1. Calculating the win-set. In a trade agreement, A and B are setting x, the extent to which A opens its market (from 0 to 100). A does better the smaller x is, and B does better the larger x is. A's legislature will ratify any x of 50 or less, and B's legislature any x of 30 or more. Assume that the negotiators settle at the midpoint of the range where the two overlap. (a) What are the range of agreement and the point of agreement? (b) What if A's legislature takes a harder line and will ratify only an x of 35 or less? (c) What if it will ratify only an x of 25 or less?

Problem 2. Audience costs and threats. A's leader threatens B and demands a concession. If B concedes, A gains the value of the issue, 4, without war. If B stands firm, A's leader either fights or backs down. Fighting gains A the 4 but costs 10 in war; backing down costs an audience cost a (a = 0 if the threat was made in private). Assume that A's leader fights when fighting and backing down are worth the same. B receives −1 if it concedes, −3 if there is a war, and +2 if A backs down. (a) What happens when a = 0, and what happens when a = 8? (b) What condition on a makes the threat credible? (c) What happens if a = 8 but B believes that a = 0?

Problem 3. Sorting cases by theory. Assign each of the following to two-level games, audience costs, or diversionary war, and justify your answer. (a) The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, which delegated authority to the president in advance to put tariff agreements into effect, so that individual agreements did not require ratification by Congress; (b) the invasion of the Falklands by Argentina's military junta in 1982; (c) President Trump's statement on October 8, 2026, that he would not attack Iran before the midterm elections.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. (a) The range that satisfies both conditions is 30–50, and the point of agreement is 40.
  2. (b) The range is 30–35, and the point of agreement is 32.5. The range has narrowed, but the outcome has moved 7.5 toward A. The side whose hands are tied gets more.
  3. (c) No value is both 25 or less and 30 or more, so agreement is impossible. If the negotiators sign anyway, ratification will fail on one side or the other, which is involuntary defection.

Problem 2.

  1. (a) If a = 0, A's leader gets 4 − 10 = −6 from fighting and 0 from backing down, so the leader backs down. Knowing this, B stands firm and gets +2. If a = 8, fighting (−6) is better than backing down (−8), so the leader fights. Knowing this, B prefers conceding (−1) to war (−3), and A gets 4 without war.
  2. (b) −6 ≥ −a, that is, a ≥ 6. The audience cost must be at least as large as the net cost of war, 10 − 4 = 6.
  3. (c) B expects A to back down and stands firm, and A fights. Audience costs serve deterrence only if the other side knows about them; if it does not, they can instead lead to war. This connects with the fourth of Snyder and Borghard's reasons below (this lecture's interpretation).

Problem 3.

  1. (a) Two-level games. By removing the domestic ratification stage for individual agreements, the act reduced the room for interest groups to trade votes on each agreement and shrink the win-set. Of the determinants Putnam identified, it falls under ratification institutions (this lecture's interpretation).
  2. (b) Diversionary war. The basis is the assessment that the junta sought to divert attention and shore up its legitimacy amid economic hardship and protests. Selectorate theory's baseline expectation, however, is that a military government with a small winning coalition can more easily survive policy failure, so the junta's fall after defeat can be explained only if the defeat also destroyed its support within the military, its winning coalition (this lecture's interpretation).
  3. (c) The statement is a signal that runs opposite to one strand of the diversionary war hypothesis, which holds that leaders facing elections resort to force. Insofar as it avoids a period when the risk of failure is high, it can also be read through selectorate theory. With the U.S. naval blockade still in place, however, a single statement cannot confirm either reading (this lecture's interpretation).

Evidence and counterarguments

The first two studies below (Tomz; Snyder and Borghard) test whether audience costs actually exist; the third (Chiozza and Goemans) tests the diversionary war hypothesis.

Tomz (2007): voters punish leaders who back down. Michael Tomz set out to measure audience costs directly, using experiments embedded in public opinion surveys. Audience costs appeared under a wide range of conditions, grew with the severity of the threat, and were especially large among politically active citizens, who are in a position to influence policy. He also offered preliminary evidence that citizens impose these costs because they worry about the international reputation of their country and its leader. The limitation is that respondents were reacting to hypothetical scenarios rather than real crises.

Snyder and Borghard (2011): audience costs are a penny, not a pound. Jack Snyder and Erica D. Borghard reported finding little evidence of audience costs in actual crises since 1945. They gave four reasons: leaders consider unambiguous threats reckless; the public cares more about the substance of policy than about whether words match deeds; the public values the nation's reputation and honor even when no threat has been made; and authoritarian states threatened by democracies do not perceive audience costs the way the theory expects. The limitation is that the study is qualitative and depends on the interpretation of cases. When Marc Trachtenberg reached a similar conclusion in 2012, Branislav L. Slantchev countered that the case evidence and its interpretation were unpersuasive.

Chiozza and Goemans (2004): leaders at risk are, if anything, less likely to be targeted. Arguing that country-year data cannot capture leaders' motives, Giacomo Chiozza and Henk E. Goemans used leader-year data and a two-stage probit model to estimate jointly the probability that a leader loses office and the probability that the leader's country becomes the target of a crisis. Leaders at greater risk of losing office were, if anything, less likely to be targeted, and being targeted did not change their probability of losing office, so there was no evidence of a rally effect. By contrast, Patrick James and John R. Oneal reported in 1991, using U.S. data, that domestic factors mattered most in uses of force short of full-scale war. The limitation is that the study looks mainly at the targets of crises, so whether leaders initiate disputes must be tested separately.
