The question for this session

On July 24, 2026, the United States began imposing tariffs on 60 countries under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, citing forced labor, and South Korean goods were hit with a rate of 12.5 percent. In late September, Washington and Beijing extended their tariff truce to January 10, 2027, but left semiconductor export controls off the table. If trade benefits both sides, why do states impose tariffs even on their allies and block exports of particular goods? This session takes up three questions: Within a country, who gains from trade and who loses? Who builds and maintains an open trading order? And when do trade ties become weapons of national security?

1. Key concepts: comparative advantage and the gains from trade

A country has a comparative advantage in a good it can produce at a lower opportunity cost than other countries can. Even a country with an absolute advantage in both goods can, by specializing in the one in which it has a comparative advantage and importing the other, increase the total quantity of goods available to the two countries. That increase is the gains from trade.

Those gains, however, are a sum for the country as a whole. Industries that compete with imports may lose, and nothing guarantees that the winners will compensate the losers. Section 2 asks along what lines the interest groups discussed in session 6 divide. Export controls are measures by which a government subjects exports of particular goods and technologies to licensing or bans them outright.

2. Winners and losers from trade: factor and industry theories

In "Protection and Real Wages" (1941), Wolfgang F. Stolper and Paul A. Samuelson formalized the distributional effects of trade. The Stolper–Samuelson theorem is usually summarized this way: when the relative price of a good rises, the real income of the factor of production used intensively in making it rises, and the real income of the other factor falls [secondary source]. Add the Heckscher–Ohlin theorem, which holds that countries export goods that use intensively the factors with which they are abundantly endowed, and the conclusion follows: when trade opens and the prices of exports rise, owners of abundant factors gain and owners of scarce factors lose.

In Commerce and Coalitions (1989), Ronald Rogowski turned this theorem into a theory of political cleavages. As the costs of trade fall and trade expands, owners of abundant factors gain political strength as supporters of free trade, while owners of scarce factors demand protection. In the examples he gave in a 1987 article, the late nineteenth-century United States, where land was abundant and capital scarce, saw conflict between town and country (the Populist era), with farmers favoring free trade and capitalists and workers together demanding protection; in nineteenth-century Britain, where land was scarce, the result was also conflict between town and country, but with landowners demanding protection.

The factor theory assumes that factors of production move freely between industries. In the Ricardo–Viner model, or specific-factors model, which treats equipment and skills as tied to particular industries, capitalists and workers in the same industry share the same interests. Firms and workers in export industries together favor openness, and firms and workers in import-competing industries together seek protection, so political conflict divides along industry lines rather than class lines. Which of the two theories is right is taken up in the Going deeper section.

Both theories predict that South Korean farmers will oppose opening the country's agricultural market and that semiconductor firms and their workers alike will favor openness. Farmers, after all, own a scarce factor, land, and also work in an import-competing industry. The two theories part ways over workers in import-competing industries: Do they join other workers in supporting openness, or do they join their own employers in demanding protection? (This paragraph is this lecture's interpretation.)

3. Who builds an open trading order? Hegemonic stability theory

In The World in Depression, 1929–1939 (1973), Charles P. Kindleberger traced the breadth, depth, and length of the Great Depression to the absence of any country to stabilize the world economy. A stabilizer must keep its market open to goods that cannot find buyers in a slump, provide long-term lending countercyclically, and supply liquidity in a crisis. Britain had played that role in the nineteenth century, but after 1929, in his diagnosis, "the British couldn't and the United States wouldn't" [secondary source].

In "State Power and the Structure of International Trade" (1976), Stephen D. Krasner recast the problem in terms of state power and state interests rather than public goods (this lecture's interpretation). States, he argued, pursue four goals: aggregate national income, social stability, political power, and economic growth. His conclusion was that openness is most likely under a hegemonic distribution of potential economic power, in which that power is concentrated in a single country, and especially when the hegemon is on the rise. The prediction fit the openness of 1820–1879, the gradual closure of 1880–1900, and the openness of 1945–1960, but not the periods 1900–1913 and 1919–1939 or the years after 1960. Krasner explained these anomalies by the inertia of domestic politics: once adopted, a policy persists until a new crisis exposes its limits (p. 341) [secondary source]. Arguments of this kind are known as hegemonic stability theory, and Robert O. Keohane's After Hegemony, discussed in session 9, is a rebuttal to them.

4. Trade and security: security externalities and weaponized interdependence

In a 1993 article, Joanne Gowa and Edward D. Mansfield argued that trade produces security externalities. Because trade allows domestic resources to be used more efficiently, it frees more resources that can be diverted to military purposes, and so it increases the potential military power of the countries that engage in it (p. 408). Since trade with an adversary harms a state's security and trade with an ally strengthens it, they predicted that free trade would be more likely within alliances, and that this tendency would be stronger in bipolar systems, in which states find it harder to switch alliances. In trade data for the great powers from 1905 to 1985, alliances substantially increased trade between partners, and the effect was larger in bipolar systems (pp. 408, 414). In effect, their argument applies to trade the relative-gains logic of John J. Mearsheimer, discussed in session 9 (this lecture's interpretation).

In "Weaponized Interdependence" (2019), Henry Farrell and Abraham L. Newman argue that global economic networks, such as financial payments and the internet, harden into asymmetric structures in which exchange is concentrated in a few central nodes, or hubs (pp. 48–49). A state with legal or physical jurisdiction over a hub gains two kinds of power: the panopticon effect, the ability to see the information that passes through the hub, and the chokepoint effect, the ability to block or penalize third parties' use of it (pp. 55–56). To use these powers, the state also needs the necessary domestic laws and institutions (pp. 56–57). In March 2012, after the European Union barred the provision of financial messaging services to Iranian financial institutions under sanctions, the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (SWIFT) disconnected Iranian banks (pp. 67–68).

Farrell and Newman dealt with hubs in financial and communications networks; extending the idea of a chokepoint to goods whose supply a few countries control, such as semiconductor equipment and materials or rare earths, is this lecture's interpretation. Export controls are a way of using such chokepoints, and by the distinction Robert A. Pape drew, discussed in session 9, they are often closer to economic warfare, which aims to weaken an opponent's capabilities, than to sanctions.

5. A classic: Ricardo's wine and cloth

In "On Foreign Trade," chapter 6 of the first edition of On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817; chapter 7 in the third edition of 1821), David Ricardo demonstrated comparative advantage with numbers. In England, making cloth takes the labor of 100 men for a year and making wine takes 120; in Portugal, wine takes 80 and cloth 90. Portugal buys its cloth from England, where it takes 100 men, even though Portugal could make it with 90, because it gains more by devoting its capital to wine and buying cloth with that wine. Ricardo held that the rule that sets the relative value of goods within a country does not govern exchange between countries, and he attributed this to the difficulty with which capital moves from one country to another.

Ricardo's arithmetic treats each country as a single unit. It does not ask what becomes of Portugal's clothmakers and their workers, or whether the added income becomes military power for the other country. Stolper and Samuelson, in section 2, filled the first of these gaps, and Gowa and Mansfield, in section 4, the second (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Historical cases

Britain's repeal of the Corn Laws, 1846. The Corn Law of 1815 favored landowners by barring imports of foreign grain until domestic grain prices reached a set level. Amid a campaign for repeal by manufacturers, the failure of Ireland's potato crop brought famine, and the Conservative prime minister, Robert Peel, came around to repeal. On May 15, 1846, the House of Commons passed the repeal bill 327–229 [secondary source]. The case fits Rogowski's conflict between town and country (this lecture's interpretation).

Japan's export restrictions on South Korea, 2019. Beginning on July 4, 2019, Japan required case-by-case licenses for exports to South Korea of three products: fluorinated polyimide, photoresist, and hydrogen fluoride. On August 28, it removed South Korea from its white list of countries given preferential export treatment. At the time, South Korea depended on Japan for about 94, 92, and 44 percent, respectively, of its imports of the three products. South Korea filed a complaint with the World Trade Organization (WTO) and set out to produce more of its own materials, parts, and equipment. Japan announced in March 2023 that it would lift the restrictions [secondary source]. Here one U.S. ally used a chokepoint against another, which security externalities have difficulty explaining, and the targeted country tried to get around the chokepoint through domestic production and alternative sources of supply (both observations are this lecture's interpretation).

7. Applying it to 2026: Section 301 tariffs, the tariff truce, and semiconductors and rare earths

The facts. The Section 301 tariffs that took effect on July 24 set rates of 12.5 percent for South Korea and Japan and 10 percent for Taiwan and the European Union, and a rate of 12.5 percent was also applied to China. For semiconductors, a Section 232 proclamation issued on January 14 imposed a 25 percent tariff on certain high-performance chips. In late 2025, the United States revoked the validated end-user (VEU) status of the China plants of Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix and issued licenses, valid only for 2026, to bring equipment into the plants, while prohibiting expansion or upgrades. In April, China's State Council Decree No. 835 gave Beijing a legal basis for sanctioning companies that comply with U.S. controls. Exports of Nvidia's H200 chips to China were permitted, subject to a volume cap. China has suspended until November 10, 2026, the expanded rare earth export controls it announced on October 9, 2025, and as of October 12 no notice of an extension had been confirmed. South Korea relies on China for about half of its rare earths, and in the second quarter of 2026 it accounted for about 83 percent of global supply of high-bandwidth memory (HBM).

Through the lens of theory. Judged by its rates alone, this round of Section 301 tariffs makes no distinction between South Korea and Japan, which are U.S. allies, and China, a rival. China also faces separate tariffs that are suspended under the truce, so overall tariff levels cannot be compared, but taken on its own this measure does not fit Gowa and Mansfield's prediction that trade within alliances would be kept more open. Whether the reason is domestic distribution or the pursuit of tariff revenue or bargaining leverage cannot be determined from this case alone. The United States holds chokepoints in semiconductor equipment and design software; China holds one in rare earths. Opening H200 exports and suspending the rare earth controls can be read as a calculation that it is better to hold a chokepoint than to use it. South Korean companies are caught between Washington's equipment licenses and Beijing's Decree No. 835. (Everything above in this paragraph is this lecture's interpretation.) Clisa Geopolitics likewise judges that China's rare earth controls deter most when they are held in reserve rather than used. South Korea's 83 percent share of HBM looks like a hub in Seoul's hands, but the Strategic Analysis tab, pointing to precedents in which using a chokepoint for coercion between allies backfired, treats HBM as a passive defense that raises the cost to the United States of coercing South Korea.

Assessment. In the second half of 2026, the United States and China are bargaining with tariffs and export controls while each holds the other's chokepoints. In this round of Section 301 tariffs, Washington imposed on South Korea and Japan the same 12.5 percent rate as on China but did not put its framework of semiconductor controls up for trade, and Beijing is holding its rare earth controls under a suspension that expires on November 10. This assessment should be revisited if the United States and China reach a deal that trades the framework of semiconductor export controls for tariff concessions, or if China lets the suspension lapse and reimposes the expanded rare earth controls.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows the arguments of Gowa and Mansfield and of Farrell and Newman in the original, works through the gains from trade and the distribution of a tariff's costs with numbers, and looks at research that has tested the links between alliances and trade, between trade and conflict, and between trade and political coalitions.

Reading the original

Passage 1. Trade becomes military power

"As a consequence, trade enhances the potential military power of any country that engages in it." (Gowa and Mansfield 1993, p. 408)

The argument rests on two premises: that trade increases the resources available for military use, and that because there is no authority above states, they must watch the potential military power of allies and adversaries alike (p. 408). It follows that trade with an adversary carries social costs greater than its private costs and trade with an ally yields social benefits greater than its private benefits, so in neither case is free trade optimal (pp. 408–409). A country with enough market power to shift its terms of trade can narrow that gap by imposing tariffs on adversaries (p. 409), and the conclusion is discrimination: openness within alliances and restriction outside them. The argument depends on the assumption that added income turns into military power and on the assumption of market power. The authors acknowledged that because their model leaves out how alliances are formed, they could not establish the direction of causation (p. 413, n. 28), and critics attacked their empirical results (see Morrow et al. 1998, below).

Passage 2. Networks harden into asymmetry

"Key global economic networks … tend to generate ever more asymmetric topologies in which exchange becomes centralized, flowing through a few specific intermediaries." (Farrell and Newman 2019, p. 49)

The premise concerns how networks grow. New participants attach themselves to nodes that already have many connections, users gravitate toward larger networks, and centralized communication is more efficient (pp. 51–52). Once a hub has formed, it is hard to dislodge, because replacing it would require many participants to move at once (p. 53). The authors set their argument against Anne-Marie Slaughter's claim that globalization disperses power into decentralized networks and against the prediction by Joseph S. Nye and Keohane that asymmetries would diminish over time (pp. 48–49). The weak link is the assumption that hubs are hard to replace. The more often networks are weaponized, the stronger the incentive for states that fear becoming targets to build alternative hubs (this lecture's interpretation). The authors themselves discussed European proposals in 2018 for an alternative payment channel but judged it unclear whether such a channel could work (pp. 69–70).

Working it out

Problem 1. Comparative advantage and the gains from trade. Countries A and B each have 120 hours of labor. Producing one unit of semiconductors takes 1 hour in A and 6 hours in B; producing one unit of rice takes 2 hours in A and 3 hours in B. (a) What is the opportunity cost of one unit of semiconductors in each country, measured in rice, and where does comparative advantage lie? (b) Without trade, if each country divides its labor equally between the two goods, what is world output? What is world output if B specializes entirely in rice and A produces 10 units of rice and puts the rest of its labor into semiconductors? (c) If A gives B 20 units of semiconductors in exchange for 20 units of rice, is each country's consumption better than it was without trade?

Problem 2. The distributional effects of a tariff. Country C imports rice at a world price of 10. Domestic demand is Qd = 100 − 4P and domestic supply is Qs = 2P, and amounts calculated as price × quantity are in units of 100 million South Korean won. If the government imposes a tariff of 2 per unit, the domestic price becomes 12. (a) What are imports before and after the tariff? (b) What are the consumers' loss, the producers' gain, the tariff revenue, and the loss that goes to no one? (c) If there are 10 million consumers and 100,000 rice producers, what is the loss or gain per person? (Hint: the consumers' loss is the price increase × the average of quantity demanded before and after the tariff, and the producers' gain is the price increase × the average of quantity supplied before and after the tariff. The loss left over is the deadweight loss.)

Problem 3. What the factor and industry theories predict. Country D is abundant in capital and labor and scarce in land. Semiconductors are an export industry, and rice competes with imports. Falling transport costs cause trade to expand. Predict whether each of the following will side with free trade or protection under (a) the factor theory and (b) the industry theory: shareholders in semiconductor firms, workers in semiconductor plants, farmworkers employed on rice farms, and owners of farmland. (c) Which group would you need to observe to tell the two theories apart?

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. (a) The opportunity cost of one unit of semiconductors is 0.5 units of rice in A (1 ÷ 2) and 2 units of rice in B (6 ÷ 3). A has an absolute advantage in both goods, but its comparative advantage lies in semiconductors; B's lies in rice.
  2. (b) Without trade, A produces 60 units of semiconductors and 30 of rice and B produces 10 and 20, so world output is 70 units of semiconductors and 50 of rice. With specialization, B produces 40 units of rice, and A produces 10 units of rice (20 hours) and 100 units of semiconductors (100 hours), so world output is 100 units of semiconductors and 50 of rice. Semiconductor output rises by 30 with no reduction in rice.
  3. (c) A consumes 80 units of semiconductors and 30 of rice, and B consumes 20 and 20. Both countries have the same amount of rice as before and more semiconductors. As long as the rate of exchange falls between the two countries' opportunity costs (0.5 to 2 units of rice per unit of semiconductors), both countries gain.

Problem 2.

  1. (a) Before the tariff, demand is 60 and supply 20, so imports are 40; after the tariff, demand is 52 and supply 24, so imports are 28.
  2. (b) The consumers' loss is 2 × (60 + 52) ÷ 2 = 112, the producers' gain is 2 × (20 + 24) ÷ 2 = 44, and the tariff revenue is 2 × 28 = 56. The remaining 112 − 44 − 56 = 12 is the deadweight loss, which comes from inefficient production (4) and reduced consumption (8).
  3. (c) The loss per consumer is 11.2 billion won ÷ 10 million people = 1,120 won, and the gain per producer is 4.4 billion won ÷ 100,000 people = 44,000 won. Because the losses are small and widely dispersed while the gains are large and concentrated, producers find it easier to organize, as the logic of Mancur Olson, discussed in sessions 3 and 6, predicts.

Problem 3.

  1. (a) Factor theory: the shareholders and both groups of workers, as owners of abundant capital and labor, side with free trade; the owners of scarce land side with protection.
  2. (b) Industry theory: shareholders and workers in semiconductors side with free trade; farmworkers and farmland owners in the rice industry side with protection.
  3. (c) The farmworkers. If they join semiconductor workers in supporting free trade, the factor theory fits; if they join farmland owners in demanding protection, the industry theory fits.

Evidence and counterarguments

Gowa and Mansfield (1993) and Morrow et al. (1998): do alliances increase trade? In contrast to the Gowa and Mansfield results discussed in the main text, James D. Morrow, Randolph M. Siverson, and Tressa E. Tabares analyzed trade among the great powers from 1907 to 1990 with a gravity model in 1998 and reported that joint democracy and common interests increased trade but that alliances, even with polarity controlled for, generally did not. In 1999 the authors published a correction of computational errors; whether the results on alliances changed could not be confirmed. Both studies are limited to samples of great powers.

Oneal and Russett (1997) and Barbieri (1996): does trade reduce conflict? This question runs in the opposite direction. Using data from 1950 to 1985, John R. Oneal and Bruce Russett reported that the pacifying effects of trade had not been sufficiently appreciated. Using data from 1870 to 1938, Katherine Barbieri reported that when interdependence is very high, militarized disputes actually increase. The conclusions differ depending on the period, the sample, and the measures used (this lecture's interpretation).

Hiscox (2001, 2002): class or industry? Michael J. Hiscox traced the fact that trade conflicts have sometimes divided along class lines and sometimes along industry lines to the degree to which factors of production move between industries. When mobility is high, class coalitions tend to form; when it is low, industry coalitions do. In data on six Western economies over two centuries, mobility varied with industrialization and regulation, and the changes broadly matched the shape of the coalitions that formed over trade policy. The limitation is that mobility is hard to measure directly (this lecture's interpretation).
