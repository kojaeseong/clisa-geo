The question for this session

In late September 2026, the United States and China extended their tariff truce to January 10, 2027. Is this a sign that the two great powers have turned toward cooperation, or a breather before the next confrontation? The same facts yield different answers depending on what one chooses to look at. This session introduces the three leading theories of international politics—realism, liberalism, and constructivism—along with levels of analysis, which determine where to look for causes. It also takes up falsifiability, a standard for judging theories.

1. Key concept: theories as lenses, and anarchy

Writing in Foreign Policy in 1998, the American political scientist Stephen M. Walt argued that theory is what allows anyone to make sense of the blizzard of information that arrives every day. A theory is a lens: it selects which of countless facts matter and explains how they connect. Because no single approach can capture the full complexity of contemporary world politics, Walt held, it is better to keep several competing ideas in view at once.

All three theories begin from anarchy. In international politics, anarchy does not mean chaos; it means that there is no central authority above states to enforce rules on them. The three theories disagree about what this condition produces.

2. Levels of analysis: Waltz's three images

In Man, the State, and War (1959), the American political scientist Kenneth N. Waltz sorted existing explanations of the causes of war into three images. The first image locates the cause in human nature or in individual leaders. The second looks to the political and economic systems inside states; the third, to the anarchy of the international system that states together make up. The first and second images, Waltz held, explain the causes of particular wars, whereas the third explains the permissive cause that makes war possible at any time: "wars occur because there is nothing to prevent them." Reviewing the book in 1960, the American political scientist J. David Singer called the three images levels of analysis, and in a 1961 article he compared what follows from taking the international system or the state as the unit of analysis.

3. Realism: power and survival

Realism treats states as the principal actors and sees them as competing for power. Hans J. Morgenthau, the leading figure of classical realism, added a statement of six principles of political realism to the 1954 second edition of Politics Among Nations (first published in 1948). There he held that politics is governed by objective laws rooted in human nature and that states pursue "interest defined in terms of power." In Waltz's scheme, Morgenthau located the cause in the first image (this lecture's interpretation).

In Theory of International Politics (1979), Waltz moved the cause to the third image. This is structural realism. Whatever their dispositions, states in a system with no higher authority must look after their own security (self-help); weaker states therefore pool their strength against stronger ones, and balances of power form again and again. John J. Mearsheimer's offensive realism goes further, holding that great powers seek to maximize their power. For realists, cooperation is possible but fragile, because states are sensitive less to what they gain together than to who gains more—what are known as relative gains. Walt identified realism's weakness as its inability to explain change in international politics.

4. Liberalism: interdependence, international institutions, and democracy

Liberalism accepts anarchy but sees more room for cooperation, on three grounds. The first is economic interdependence. In Power and Interdependence (1977), Robert O. Keohane and Joseph S. Nye gave the name complex interdependence to a condition in which societies are linked through multiple channels, issues are not ranked in a fixed hierarchy, and military force plays only a minor role. The second is international institutions. In After Hegemony (1984), Keohane argued that institutions provide information and lower transaction costs, so cooperation can persist even after a hegemon declines. The third is domestic political systems; the best-known version is the democratic peace, the proposition that democracies almost never go to war with one another. Walt identified liberalism's weakness as a tendency to underrate the role of power.

5. Constructivism: anarchy is what states make of it

Constructivism holds that shared ideas, above all identities and norms, shape states' interests more than material power does. In a 1992 article, Alexander Wendt faulted neorealism for treating anarchy as necessarily a self-help system and neoliberalism for taking self-interested states as its starting point. "Self-help and power politics are institutions, not essential features of anarchy," he wrote. "Anarchy is what states make of it." His example—that British missiles and Soviet missiles mean different things to the United States—illustrates the point. In Social Theory of International Politics (1999), Wendt distinguished a Hobbesian culture, in which states see one another as enemies; a Lockean culture, in which they see one another as rivals; and a Kantian culture, in which they see one another as friends. Walt judged that constructivism cannot predict the content of ideas in advance and is therefore better at explaining the past than at anticipating the future.

6. A standard for judging theories: falsifiability

In Logik der Forschung (1934), the philosopher of science Karl Popper proposed falsifiability as the criterion that separates science from non-science: a scientific claim must make predictions that could turn out to be wrong. A theory that can explain whatever happens looks strong but in fact predicts nothing. Experiments are hard to run in international politics, but predictions can still be checked later if their deadlines and conditions are stated in advance. On the Forecasts and Track Record tab of Clisa Geopolitics, each forecast states its deadline and resolution conditions beforehand; once the deadline passes, the outcome is published and the Brier score is recalculated.

7. Classics and thinkers: Kant's prediction and its test

Liberalism has its roots in Immanuel Kant. In Perpetual Peace (1795), he set out three definitive articles for perpetual peace: a republican constitution, a federation of free states, and a cosmopolitan right limited to universal hospitality. By a republic he meant not a democracy but a system that separates legislative from executive power, and the federation he envisioned was a league of peace without coercive power. If the consent of citizens were required to decide whether to go to war, he reasoned, citizens who would themselves bear the hardships of war "would think very carefully before beginning so bad a game." He also argued that the spirit of commerce leads states to promote peace out of self-interest rather than morality.

This prediction was later tested against evidence. In 1983, Michael W. Doyle reported that constitutionally secure liberal states had not gone to war with one another for almost 200 years and attributed this to Kant's definitive articles. Sebastian Rosato countered in 2003 that it is not clear whether peace among democracies is due to democracy, and Doyle himself pointed out that liberal states are not peaceful toward nonliberal ones.

8. Historical cases: the end of the Cold War and the three theories

The Cold War ended as the communist regimes of Eastern Europe fell in 1989 and the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991. In a 1992 article, the historian John Lewis Gaddis observed that none of the approaches to international relations that claimed predictive power had in fact foreseen it.

Once the Cold War was over, each of the three theories offered its own explanation. On the realist side, William C. Wohlforth held that the Soviet Union's relative decline vis-à-vis the United States and the West produced its retreat; in a 2000 article written with Stephen G. Brooks, he argued that material pressure arising from changes in the structure of global production drove Soviet foreign policy and that new ideas themselves grew out of that pressure. Closer to liberalism (this lecture's classification), Daniel Deudney and G. John Ikenberry held that Gorbachev was responding to the Western state system and to the common vulnerability created by nuclear weapons. On the constructivist side, Rey Koslowski and Friedrich V. Kratochwil argued in 1994 that the revolutions in Eastern Europe and Soviet nonintervention did not stem from shifts in relative capabilities and so did not fit neorealist assumptions, and that the revolutions of 1989 changed the rules of superpower rivalry and the norms of the international system. Walt summed up the record this way: realism and liberalism failed to foresee the end of the Cold War, whereas constructivism explained it through new ideas, such as common security, that Gorbachev embraced. A 2011 special issue of a scholarly journal still left the debate unresolved. After the fact, any theory can supply an explanation, which is why prediction made in advance tests a theory more strictly than explanation after the event (this lecture's interpretation).

9. Applying it to 2026: the U.S.-China tariff truce

The facts. In April 2025, the United States and China imposed tariffs of more than 100 percent on each other. After several rounds of negotiation, they agreed on a one-year truce at a summit in Busan, South Korea, on October 30. Following reciprocal state visits by the two leaders in May and September 2026, they extended the truce by two months, to January 10, 2027. In May, the two sides described their relationship as one of constructive strategic stability and agreed to allow exports of Nvidia's H200 chips; by September, semiconductor export controls had dropped off the agenda. On Taiwan, President Trump remarked in May that he did not want someone seeking independence. At the September summit, Xi Jinping pressed the United States to oppose Taiwanese independence, but Washington reaffirmed that its Taiwan policy had not changed. Session 5 returns to this case through the bargaining model of war. The three paragraphs below and the last column of the table apply each theory's logic to the case and are this lecture's interpretation.

Through a realist lens, the truce is a breather. Leaving semiconductor export controls off the September agenda can be read as wariness about relative gains: a refusal to bargain over the technology that will shape the future distribution of power. The May decision to allow H200 exports, however, cuts against this account. A realist could point out that the approval came with a volume cap and per-customer limits, but if such qualifications are attached every time an inconvenient fact appears, the theory becomes, as section 6 showed, one that explains everything and predicts nothing.

Through a liberal lens, the truce is a compromise forced by the costs of interdependence. Tariffs above 100 percent were costly for both sides, and deals such as chip export licenses multiply intertwined interests. The repeated summits and the AI dialogue channel created in September are institutions in embryo, however weak. Yet an extension of only two months, with no binding mechanism, means that institutionalization remains shallow even by liberal standards.

Through a constructivist lens, what matters is how the two countries define each other. Describing the relationship as one of constructive strategic stability declares an intention to treat the other side not as an enemy to be eliminated but as a rival to compete with while coexisting. If that declaration is carried into practice and repeated, the relationship could be seen as moving toward what Wendt called a Lockean culture. Constructivists look less at the label than at whether behavior consistent with it recurs, and at what comes to be accepted as legitimate conduct over Taiwan.

How to tell which lens fits better. Whether the truce is extended again will do little to separate the lenses, because realism expects the breather to be prolonged and liberalism expects the compromise to hold. An agreement of a year or longer with binding mechanisms would strengthen the liberal account; a string of extensions lasting a few months each, while technology controls harden into law, would strengthen the realist account (this lecture's interpretation). Clisa Geopolitics puts at 20 percent the probability that, by January 3, 2027 (the end of the 119th Congress), the president signs into law a bill—either stand-alone legislation or the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA)—containing at least one of two provisions: a ban on sales of equipment and services to designated companies such as CXMT and YMTC, or a requirement that U.S. controls apply extraterritorially if allies fail to align by a deadline. This figure does not assume that any one theory is correct; it is a judgment that weighs several possibilities together. No forecast yet turns a constructivist expectation into a question with a deadline. Whether the U.S. government uses the word "oppose" about Taiwanese independence in official documents, for example, could serve as an observation of what the two countries regard as legitimate conduct. Given Walt's point that the content of ideas is hard to know in advance, framing such questions is itself a task for constructivism (this lecture's interpretation).

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It traces Wendt's argument in the original, works through levels of analysis and the three lenses in a set of problems, and looks at research that has put the democratic peace to the test.

Reading the original

Passage 1. Threats are constructed

"Social threats are constructed, not natural." (Wendt 1992, p. 405)

The argument starts from a thought experiment. Two actors meet for the first time. Each wants to survive and has material capabilities, but neither has any biological or domestic drive for power, glory, or conquest, and the two have no history with each other (p. 404). The reasoning begins with a first gesture. When one side advances, retreats, or takes up arms, the other infers its intentions from the gesture and responds (pp. 404–405). As these exchanges repeat, relatively stable expectations take shape. The conclusion follows: a self-help system and a cooperative system alike are created through this process.

Realism counsels assuming the worst. Wendt answered that most decisions rest on probabilities and that interaction produces those probabilities (p. 404). Realist critics ask whether a state's present gestures alone give others grounds to trust its intentions (this lecture's interpretation). In a 2000 review essay on Wendt's 1999 book, Dale C. Copeland faulted Wendt for failing to consider how uncertainty affects the behavior of states.

Passage 2. The power of a single predator

"One predator will best a hundred pacifists because anarchy provides no guarantees." (Wendt 1992, p. 408)

Suppose there is a predator, a state that has become predisposed to aggression for biological, domestic, or systemic reasons. Other states then have no choice but to engage in competitive power politics (p. 407). When there are many states, however, the outcome depends on their collective identity. If that identity is strong, the predator is defeated and collective security takes hold; if the predator is strong enough, balance-of-power politics returns (p. 408).

In Waltz's terms, Wendt took the cause of self-help out of the structure of anarchy and relocated it in interaction at the same, systemic level, while leaving the sources of predatory dispositions to the first and second images (this lecture's interpretation). Realists would seize on this concession to argue that the result is no different from self-help. Wendt's reply is that "mature" anarchies are not easily reduced to a Hobbesian condition by predation (p. 409).

Working it out

Problem 1. Sorting by level of analysis and lens. Place each of the following statements within Waltz's images and the three lenses, and justify your answer. (a) Democratic leaders avoid war with other democracies because they fear losing elections. (b) When a rival grows quickly, any state, regardless of regime type, responds by adding allies. (c) After decades of cooperation, Germany and France stopped regarding each other as potential enemies. (d) In July 1914, the Kaiser's impetuous temperament made the crisis worse.

Problem 2. Testing a hypothesis with numbers. The data below are hypothetical, for practice. Ten thousand dyad-years (a dyad-year is one pair of states observed over one year) are sorted by whether both states are democracies and whether the two are allies, and the militarized disputes in each group are counted.

Pair Allied dyad-years Disputes Nonallied dyad-years Disputes
Democracy–democracy 1,500 3 500 2
All other pairs 1,000 2 7,000 56

(a) Setting alliances aside, compare the dispute rates (per 1,000 dyad-years) of the two groups. (b) Compare the rates again, separately for allied and nonallied pairs. (c) The democratic peace holds that regime type produces peace. The common-interests explanation holds that shared interests, such as having the same enemy, produce it, and it measures common interests by alliances. How would each explanation interpret these results?

Problem 3. Relative gains and falsifiable predictions. In 1988, Joseph M. Grieco expressed a state's utility as its own gain − k × (the other state's gain − its own gain). Here k, which is zero or greater, measures a state's sensitivity to relative gains; if there is no agreement, both states gain zero. (a) For what range of k will state A accept deal 1 (A +6, B +10)? (b) For what range will it accept deal 2 (A +5, B +7)? (c) Which deal will A choose? Name an observation that would falsify the liberal prediction, in which k = 0, and one that would falsify the realist prediction, in which k is large.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. (a) Second image; liberalism. The cause is a domestic institution: elections. Because leaders on both sides face this constraint, the effect is strongest between democracies; that is the logic of the structural model discussed below.
  2. (b) Third image; realism. "Regardless of regime type" rules out domestic factors.
  3. (c) Third image; constructivism. The cause lies in long interaction and the change in identity it produced. If the cause is taken instead to be the institutions of European integration or economic interdependence, liberalism is also a defensible answer (this lecture's interpretation).
  4. (d) First image. Temperament alone does not fit neatly under any of the lenses, which shows that level of analysis and lens are separate axes (this lecture's interpretation).

Problem 2.

  1. (a) For democratic pairs, 5 ÷ 2,000 = 2.5 per 1,000; for all other pairs, 58 ÷ 8,000 = 7.25, about 2.9 times as high.
  2. (b) Among allied pairs, the rates are 2.0 and 2.0, with no difference; among nonallied pairs, they are 2 ÷ 500 = 4.0 and 56 ÷ 7,000 = 8.0, a twofold difference.
  3. (c) The common-interests explanation would attribute much of the difference to alliances. Proponents of the democratic peace would counter that a difference remains among nonallied pairs and that, because democracies ally with one another readily, alliances may themselves be a result of democracy. This table alone cannot show whether alliance is a confounding variable or a mediating variable. Democratic nonallied pairs account for only two disputes, so a change of a single dispute would shift the rate considerably (this lecture's interpretation).

Problem 3.

  1. (a) 6 − 4k ≥ 0, so 0 ≤ k ≤ 1.5.
  2. (b) 5 − 2k ≥ 0, so 0 ≤ k ≤ 2.5.
  3. (c) 6 − 4k > 5 − 2k if and only if k < 0.5. A therefore chooses deal 1 if k < 0.5 and deal 2 if 0.5 < k ≤ 2.5, and it rejects both if k > 2.5. If A repeatedly passes over deal 1, which offers the larger gain to A itself, by choosing deal 2 or rejecting both, the liberal prediction is wrong; if A repeatedly chooses deal 1, the realist prediction is wrong. Unless k is specified in advance, any choice can be explained after the fact (this lecture's interpretation).

Evidence and counterarguments

Constructivism is hard to test with data, so this section turns to the democratic peace, which has a large body of statistical research behind it, and to the challenges it has faced.

Maoz and Russett (1993): democracy itself reduces conflict. Zeev Maoz and Bruce Russett asked why democracies, although they become involved in disputes about as often as nondemocracies, almost never fight one another. Using data on disputes between pairs of states from 1946 to 1986, they tested a normative model, in which norms of compromise keep disputes from escalating, and a structural model, in which institutional constraints on mobilization make armed conflict difficult. Even with other factors taken into account, democracy consistently lowered the likelihood of disputes and of escalation. Both models found support, but the normative model proved more robust. The period studied coincides with the Cold War.

Farber and Gowa (1995): peace may have come from common interests. When Henry S. Farber and Joanne Gowa divided the record into periods, they found no significant relationship between regime type and the probability of war before World War I; disputes short of war were in fact significantly more frequent between democratic pairs. The relationship the democratic peace predicts appeared only after World War II. The authors attributed it to common interests during the Cold War and supported that claim with an analysis of alliance formation. In Waltz's terms, they explained peace between domestic systems by interests arising in the international system; if alliances are a result of democracy, however, the explanation weakens (this lecture's interpretation).

Gartzke (2007): the capitalist peace. Erik Gartzke argued that economic development, free markets, and common interests among states predict fewer disputes and wars, and that this capitalist peace accounts for what is usually measured as the effect of regime type. In 2011, Allan Dafoe countered that these results depend on dropping some communist states and the observations before 1966 from the sample, on regional control variables, and on misspecified temporal controls. He concluded that the democratic peace remains a highly robust empirical association. Dafoe reanalyzed a single study by Gartzke.
