The question for this session

Sessions 5 through 7 dealt mainly with wars between states. Yet of the eight conflict zones to which Clisa Geopolitics assigned its highest severity level in October 2026, four—Sudan, the Sahel, Ethiopia's Tigray region, and Yemen—center on civil wars in which a government is fighting armed groups. This session asks three questions. Where do civil wars break out? Why are civil wars so hard to end through negotiation? And why do nonstate groups use violence against civilians, and does that violence achieve its aims?

1. Key concepts: civil war and terrorism

A civil war is a war fought within a single country between the government and an organized armed group over control of the state or of territory. James D. Fearon and David D. Laitin count a conflict as a civil war if it killed at least 1,000 people over its course, with a yearly average of at least 100, and at least 100 deaths on each side.

The definition of terrorism is itself contested. Andrew Kydd and Barbara F. Walter define it as violence that nonstate actors direct against civilians in pursuit of political goals (2006, p. 51). The definition leaves out violence by states and attacks on soldiers. In practice, it is governments that designate groups as terrorist organizations. In October 1997, the U.S. State Department designated 30 groups, among them Hezbollah and Hamas, as Foreign Terrorist Organizations (FTOs), and in March 2025 it designated Yemen's Houthis (under the name Ansarallah). Separately, on August 24, 2026, the United States removed Syria from its list of State Sponsors of Terrorism, a different list that applies to states. Designation is also an instrument of diplomacy (this lecture's interpretation).

2. The causes of civil war: opportunity and conditions, greed and grievance

In their 2003 article "Ethnicity, Insurgency, and Civil War," Fearon and Laitin analyzed 161 countries with populations of at least half a million from 1945 to 1999. The large number of civil wars, they found, was not the product of ethnic conflict after the end of the Cold War but the accumulation of conflicts that had begun in the 1950s and 1960s and never ended. Once per capita income was taken into account, countries that were more ethnically or religiously diverse were no more likely to experience civil war. Fearon and Laitin saw the civil wars of this period as insurgencies—guerrilla warfare based in the countryside—and argued that what separates high-risk countries from low-risk ones is the presence of conditions that favor insurgency: poverty, which marks a weak state and makes rebel recruitment easier; political instability; rough terrain; and a large population. They attributed the elevated risk in oil-exporting countries to state weakness as well. Governments that depend on oil revenues have weaker state apparatuses than their income would suggest, and oil revenues raise the payoff from seizing control of the state.

In a 2004 article, Paul Collier and Anke Hoeffler tested two accounts of rebellion, one based on greed and the other on grievance. Grievance refers to motives such as inequality or a lack of political rights; greed refers to the opportunity to build a rebel organization. In data from 1960 to 1999, measures of grievance explained little, while economic measures explained far more. Their 2002 manuscript found that the risk of a civil war beginning within five years peaked at about 22 percent when primary commodity exports made up about 32 percent of gross domestic product (GDP), compared with about 1 percent for countries with no such exports. A male secondary school enrollment rate 10 percentage points higher lowered the risk from 11.5 percent to 8.6 percent. Both studies emphasize opportunity over motive, but Fearon and Laitin locate that opportunity mainly in the weakness of the state, whereas Collier and Hoeffler locate it in rebels' ability to finance and recruit (this lecture's interpretation).

3. The commitment problem in ending civil wars

The commitment problem introduced in session 5 is especially acute when it comes to ending civil wars. In her 1997 article "The Critical Barrier to Civil War Settlement," Walter analyzed 41 civil wars from 1940 to 1990. Over the same period, 55 percent of interstate wars ended in negotiated settlements, compared with 20 percent of civil wars. Of the 17 civil wars that went to formal negotiations, eight ended in settlements that both sides had worked out together and that ended the fighting for at least five years.

The barrier is disarmament. After an interstate war, each side can keep its army and withdraw behind its own border, but ending a civil war requires the rebels to lay down their arms and come under the authority of a single state. Once disarmed, rebels have no means of resisting if the government breaks the agreement, so they find disarmament hard to accept even when the deal would benefit both sides. Walter's remedy is a third-party guarantee. The guarantor must be willing to use force if necessary and must demonstrate its resolve through costly signals, such as stationing troops on the ground. In her data, all six settlements backed by a third-party guarantee succeeded, while only two succeeded without one, in Colombia and Yemen.

In The Logic of Violence in Civil War (2006), Stathis N. Kalyvas argued that even the violence of civil war follows a calculation. Because insurgents hide among the population, armed groups depend on denunciations from civilians for the information they need to identify their enemies. Selective violence—killing chosen individuals—is more common in areas where one side holds dominant but incomplete control, while indiscriminate violence aimed at whole groups tends instead to drive people to defect [secondary source]. That is why indiscriminate violence so easily backfires in counterinsurgency operations (this lecture's interpretation).

4. The strategies of terrorism

Kydd and Walter treat terrorism as a costly signal. Because talk is cheap, weak actors use violence to demonstrate their strength and resolve, hoping to change how their adversaries and the population judge the situation (pp. 50, 58). They sort the goals of terrorist organizations into regime change, territorial change, policy change, social control, and status quo maintenance (pp. 52–53), and they identify five strategies: attrition, intimidation, provocation, spoiling, and outbidding.

Because spoiling exploits the commitment problem described in section 3, the responses Kydd and Walter propose for it are commitment devices such as third-party monitoring and power sharing (p. 74).

5. Suicide terrorism and whether terrorism works

In "The Strategic Logic of Suicide Terrorism" (2003), Robert A. Pape compiled all 188 suicide terrorist attacks carried out worldwide from 1980 to 2001. Religious fanaticism, he argued, cannot explain them, and he pointed out that the group responsible for the most suicide attacks was Sri Lanka's Tamil Tigers, a Marxist-Leninist organization. His conclusion was that suicide terrorism is a coercive strategy aimed at forcing democracies to withdraw from territory. Of the five suicide terrorist campaigns he examined (two in Lebanon, one in Gaza and the West Bank, one in Sri Lanka, and one in Turkey), the attackers' political cause made greater gains after the campaign in every case except Turkey, and suicide terrorism spread, he argued, because terrorist organizations learned that it works.

In "Why Terrorism Does Not Work" (2006), Max Abrahms reached a different conclusion. The 28 groups on the State Department's 2001 list of Foreign Terrorist Organizations had pursued 42 policy objectives, and they achieved three of them, or 7 percent. Groups that mainly attacked civilians failed regardless of the nature of their objectives; every success came from guerrilla groups that mainly attacked military targets. Abrahms explained this with correspondent inference theory from social psychology. Target countries infer intentions from consequences, so a group that kills civilians is taken to be seeking the destruction of the society itself, even when its demands are limited, and it wins no concessions. The two conclusions diverge partly because Pape looked at concessions by the target and Abrahms at whether objectives were achieved, and partly because their samples differ (this lecture's interpretation).

6. Classics and thinkers: Thucydides on Corcyra, Mao Zedong on protracted war

After describing the civil strife at Corcyra during the Peloponnesian War, Thucydides wrote that the entire Greek world was thrown into turmoil, with popular leaders everywhere trying to bring in the Athenians and oligarchs trying to bring in the Spartans. In peacetime there would have been neither pretext nor desire to issue such invitations, but in war either faction could always call on an ally (3.82.1). War, in his view, is a harsh teacher that drags people's characters down to the level of their circumstances (3.82.2), and the cause of all these evils was a hunger for power born of greed and ambition (3.82.8).

In "On Protracted War" (1938), written during China's war of resistance against Japan, Mao Zedong argued that a side that is weak but large can reverse the balance of strength by avoiding decisive battles and wearing down a stronger opponent through mobile and guerrilla warfare. The Communist Party he led carried guerrilla methods into its civil war with the Nationalists (the Kuomintang) as well and won in 1949. Fearon and Laitin's list of conditions for insurgency—rough terrain, a large population, and a weak state—shows where such a strategy is easiest to use (this lecture's interpretation).

7. Historical cases

Beirut, 1983. On October 23, 1983, bombs struck the barracks of U.S. Marines and French troops in Beirut, killing 241 American and 58 French service members. The U.S. government attributes the attacks to the group that would later become Hezbollah. The Marines left Lebanon on February 26, 1984, and the last French troops on March 31 [secondary source]. Pape cites the case as one in which coercion worked, and Abrahms, too, counted Hezbollah's expulsion of the multinational peacekeepers as one of his three successes. Because the targets were military personnel, however, the attacks do not count as terrorism under Kydd and Walter's definition (this lecture's interpretation).

Angola and Mozambique. The Bicesse Accords, signed in May 1991 by the Angolan government and the rebel National Union for the Total Independence of Angola (UNITA), called for demobilizing 152,000 combatants and forming a unified army, but implementation was overseen only by the UN Angola Verification Mission (UNAVEM II), whose mandate was limited to verification. UNITA rejected the results of the September 1992 elections and returned to war. In Mozambique, by contrast, the Rome General Peace Accords of October 1992 brought in about 7,500 UN peacekeepers, the units of the rebel Mozambican National Resistance (RENAMO) were demobilized or integrated into the government army, and the peace held until 2013 [secondary source]. Both cases postdate Walter's period of analysis, but they fit her predictions (this lecture's interpretation).

8. Applying it to 2026: the disarmament barrier and the internationalization of civil wars

The facts. In Yemen, the collapse of the 2022 truce has reignited the civil war. On the night of October 9, the Saudi-led coalition launched a large-scale operation against the Houthis and said it had destroyed 136 targets. On October 10, an attack on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh killed 12 people, including one American, and wounded 309 (according to Saudi Arabia's General Authority of Civil Aviation), and the Saudi government blamed the Houthis. The same day, President Donald Trump said he would consider having the United States join the air strikes. Earlier, on October 5, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan had invoked collective deterrence measures under the Mecca Joint Defense Agreement. In Lebanon, talks on implementing the ceasefire are deadlocked over Hezbollah's refusal to disarm. In Gaza, on July 31, the Board of Peace announced an agreement on the phased disarmament of Hamas and the phased withdrawal of Israeli forces, but Israel has not formally said that it accepts it. In Syria, on August 25, the day after the United States removed Syria from its list of State Sponsors of Terrorism, the Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF) announced that they would disband and merge into the government army. Following a September 18 terrorist attack on police headquarters in Kohat (31 killed), Pakistan struck 10 sites in Afghanistan on September 24, citing drone attacks launched from Afghanistan.

Through the lens of theory. Lebanon and Gaza are stuck at the disarmament barrier that Walter identified. Even for the Gaza agreement, which trades disarmament for withdrawal step by step, Walter's criteria make the decisive question whether forces capable of enforcing implementation are actually deployed. In Syria, the integration of rebel forces has begun, which means the country has entered the period Walter regarded as one of extreme vulnerability. From Abrahms's perspective, the attack on a civilian airport that Saudi Arabia blamed on the Houthis is likely to lead Riyadh to read Houthi aims in maximal terms and so to provoke retaliation rather than concessions. Indeed, what followed the attack was not a concession but word that the United States would consider joining the air strikes. The Mecca agreement on Saudi Arabia's side and Iran, with the Houthis as its proxy, on the other illustrate the internationalization of civil war that Thucydides observed. This entire paragraph is this lecture's interpretation.

Going deeper

This section is for readers who want to go a step beyond the main text. It follows Walter's argument in the original, works through disarmament and the signaling effect of terrorist attacks with numbers, and looks at research that has put the theories to the test.

Reading the original

Passage 1. In a civil war, the two sides cannot keep separate armies

"...adversaries in a civil war cannot retain separate, independent armed forces if they agree to settle their differences." (Walter 1997, p. 336)

When a civil war ends with the two sides joined in a single state, the armies and borders that could deter the other side from violating the agreement disappear (p. 337), and there is no legitimate government or legal system to enforce it (p. 335). The rebels therefore pass through a period of extreme vulnerability between demobilization and integration (p. 336), and because each side knows what the other could gain by exploiting the agreement, neither can trust the other's promises (p. 338). Walter's conclusion is that negotiations fail not because the issues are irreconcilable but because the parties cannot, on their own, arrange credible guarantees (p. 335). The argument rests on the assumption that ending a civil war means merging into a single army. A settlement that partitions the country or leaves rebel units in place could lower the barrier (this lecture's interpretation).

Passage 2. What makes a guarantee credible

"...the guarantor must be willing to use force if necessary..." (Walter 1997, p. 339)

Walter held that the guarantor's military capabilities must be sufficient to punish whichever side violates the agreement and that the guarantor must demonstrate its resolve through costly signals, such as stationing troops or creating a trip wire (pp. 339–340). In her data, she treated an outside power's promise to protect the parties during the implementation period as a guarantee and classified guarantees as stronger or weaker by the size of the force involved (pp. 345–346). One question remains: who guarantees the guarantor's own promise? If a guarantor can be attacked and pull out, as the multinational force in Beirut did in 1984, an attrition strategy can target the guarantor rather than the parties to the civil war (this lecture's interpretation). The possibility that guarantors chose only the cases that were easiest to settle is taken up in Fortna's study below.

Working it out

Problem 1. The government's incentives after disarmament. A government and a rebel group are fighting over a prize worth 100. If the war continues, the government wins with a probability of 70 percent, and the winner takes all 100; the war costs each side 20. A proposed peace settlement gives the government 60 and the rebels 40. The rebels either disarm and accept the settlement or keep fighting. Once the rebels have disarmed, the government can either honor the agreement (60) or break it and take all 100, but breaking it costs 10 in repression. If the two options are worth the same, the government honors the agreement. (a) Find the bargaining range, and say whether the proposed settlement falls within it. (b) What happens without a guarantee? (c) Suppose a guarantor punishes a government that breaks the agreement with probability q, inflicting a loss of 50. How large must q be for the government to honor the agreement?

Problem 2. The signaling effect of a terrorist attack. A government is deciding whether to grant an armed group's demand. Conceding costs 30. If the government holds out, it pays 50 if the group is the highly resolved (strong) type, because the attacks will continue, and 0 if the group is the weak type. The government initially puts the probability that the group is strong at 0.3. A strong group carries out a major attack with a probability of 0.9, a weak group with a probability of 0.2. If the two options cost the same, the government holds out. (a) What does the government choose before any attack? (b) After observing a major attack, what probability should the government assign to the group's being strong, and what does it choose? (c) Suppose the attack targeted civilians, so that the government puts at 0.6 the probability that, if the group is strong, a concession will not end its demands and the attacks will continue. What does the government choose then?

Problem 3. Classifying cases by strategy. Place each of the following under one of Kydd and Walter's five strategies, and justify your answer. (a) the yearlong blockade of fuel supply routes to Mali's capital, Bamako, maintained into 2026 by JNIM, a jihadist insurgent group; (b) the 1983 bombing of the multinational force's barracks in Beirut; (c) a hypothetical case: a week before the government and the main rebel group are to sign a peace agreement, a breakaway faction opposed to the deal sets off a bomb in a market in the capital; (d) a hypothetical case: two armed groups competing for the same base of support take turns carrying out ever larger attacks.

Show solutions

Problem 1.

  1. (a) The government's expected value from war is 0.7 × 100 − 20 = 50, and the rebels' is 0.3 × 100 − 20 = 10. The bargaining range consists of the divisions that give the government between 50 and 90, and 60 falls within it.
  2. (b) Once the rebels disarm, the government gets 100 − 10 = 90 by breaking the agreement, more than the 60 it gets by honoring it. Knowing this, the rebels expect 0 after disarming and choose war (10). The outcome (50, 10) is worse for both sides than the settlement (60, 40).
  3. (c) 90 − 50q ≤ 60 gives q ≥ 0.6.

Problem 2.

  1. (a) The expected cost of holding out is 0.3 × 50 = 15, less than the cost of conceding, 30, so the government holds out.
  2. (b) The probability that a strong group attacks is 0.3 × 0.9 = 0.27, and that a weak group attacks is 0.7 × 0.2 = 0.14, so the probability that the group is strong is 0.27 ÷ 0.41 ≈ 0.66. The expected cost of holding out, 0.66 × 50 ≈ 33, now exceeds 30, so the government concedes. The attack conveys information because a major attack is hard for a weak group to carry out.
  3. (c) The expected cost of conceding is 30 + 0.66 × 0.6 × 50 ≈ 50, more than the roughly 33 of holding out, so the government holds out. When the same attack is read both as a signal of resolve and as a signal of maximalist aims, it fails to extract a concession. This puts Abrahms's logic into numbers (this lecture's interpretation).

Problem 3.

  1. (a) Intimidation. The blockade shows the population that the government cannot protect even the capital's fuel supply, and it fits the condition of a weak state. Because the convoys being targeted also include military escorts, however, whether it counts as terrorism under the definition is open to a caveat, as in (b) (this lecture's interpretation).
  2. (b) Attrition. By imposing heavy costs on outside powers whose interests were peripheral, the attack brought about their withdrawal. As section 7 noted, however, whether it counts as terrorism under the definition is open to a caveat.
  3. (c) Spoiling. The attack signals to the government that the moderates on the rebel side cannot keep the agreement.
  4. (d) Outbidding. The attacks are meant to show the base of support that each group is more resolved than its rival.

Evidence and counterarguments

Cederman, Wimmer, and Min (2010): excluded ethnic groups rebel. Lars-Erik Cederman, Andreas Wimmer, and Brian Min built a new dataset, Ethnic Power Relations (EPR), covering 1946 to 2005 and analyzed it with ethnic groups rather than countries as the unit. Representatives of groups excluded from power, especially groups that had recently lost power, were more likely to start armed conflicts with the government, and the likelihood rose with a group's capacity for mobilization and its history of past conflict. Grievances that did not show up in country-level analysis thus come into view as political exclusion. The limitation is that experts decide which groups are politically relevant and how far each is excluded (this lecture's interpretation).

Ashworth and colleagues (2008) and Pape's reply: can cases of suicide terrorism alone reveal its causes? Scott Ashworth, Joshua D. Clinton, Adam Meirowitz, and Kristopher W. Ramsay criticized Pape's data for including only cases in which suicide terrorism occurred, which amounts to sampling on the dependent variable. Such data can show what share of suicide terrorism cases involved an occupation, but not the probability that an occupation produces suicide terrorism or the share of suicide terrorism that occupations cause. The attributable risk of occupation that they calculated ranged from about −0.96 to 0.94, and they concluded that the data say almost nothing. In the same issue, Pape replied that his book examined the factors that make suicide terrorism more or less likely and that concerns about sample bias diminish in a study that gathers the entire universe of cases. Settling the debate would require comparison with groups that did not use suicide terrorism.

Fortna (2004): peacekeepers make peace last. Virginia Page Fortna analyzed how long peace lasted after civil wars from 1944 to 1997 and reported that, even after accounting for how difficult the cases were, peacekeeping reduced the risk of renewed war by more than 55 percent. Because peacekeepers deployed with the parties' consent went more often to the hard cases in which government and rebel forces were deadlocked, the suspicion that guarantors chose only easy cases would, if anything, make the effect look smaller than it is. The limitation is that when missions are broken down by type, only one estimate is statistically significant [secondary source].
