Russia

President Vladimir Putin (Prime Minister Mikhail Mishustin)

Power Structures · Vladimir PutinArmed forces · Russia

In the fifth year of its war in Ukraine, Russia is pressing a war of attrition aimed at seizing what remains of Donetsk under Ukrainian control. Moscow has engaged with mediation by U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner without yielding on its core territorial demands, and in September's Duma election United Russia won its largest-ever share of seats, a display of the Kremlin's grip at home.

Grand strategy

Great-power status and regime survival. To secure both, Moscow demands a privileged sphere of influence over the former Soviet space.

Must achieve

  • Ukrainian neutrality and legal recognition of the occupied territories (including all of the Donbas)
  • A permanent halt to NATO's eastward expansion
  • Non-Western economic networks that bypass sanctions (China, India, the global South)

Can concede

  • Adjustments to parts of the Zaporizhzhia and Kherson fronts
  • Ukrainian membership in the EU, so long as it does not amount to a military alliance
  • Technical agreements such as prisoner exchanges and Black Sea navigation
  • Some of its support for North Korea and Iran

Will never concede

  • Ukrainian membership in NATO
  • Returning Crimea and the Donbas
  • Outside measures that deny the regime's legitimacy

Moscow calculates that a war of attrition favors it, and it wants not a ceasefire but a final settlement that locks in its territorial gains in law, so that no future Western government can reverse them. It is buying time while Washington's attention is drawn to the Middle East, and although its dependence on China has deepened, it has widened an alternative network of partners through its ties with North Korea and Iran.

What to watch

  • The pace of offensives toward Kramatorsk and Sloviansk after Kostiantynivka
  • Signs of escalation, such as airspace violations along NATO's eastern frontier (Poland and the Baltic states)
